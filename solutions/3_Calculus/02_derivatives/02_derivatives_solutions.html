Exercise 1. Derivative of a Polynomial and the Slope of a Tangent Line

The question

For

$$f(x)=3x^5-2x^3+7x-4$$

find $f'(x)$. Then compute $f'(0)$ and $f'(1)$, determine the signs of the obtained values, and explain what they say about the slope of the tangent line to the graph at these two points.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A polynomial is a linear combination of power functions $x^n$. To differentiate it, we combine two basic rules:

  • Linearity (sum and constant multiple rule): $(af(x)+bg(x))' = af'(x)+bg'(x)$, so we differentiate term by term.
  • Power rule: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}$ for any integer $n\ge1$, and the derivative of a constant is $0$.

Geometrically, the value $f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the graph $y=f(x)$ at $x=a$:

  • $f'(a)>0$: the tangent line slopes upward from left to right, and the function is locally increasing,
  • $f'(a)<0$: the tangent line slopes downward, and the function is locally decreasing,
  • $f'(a)=0$: the tangent line is horizontal.

1. Differentiate term by term

Apply the power rule to each term of $f(x)=3x^5-2x^3+7x-4$:

  • $\dfrac{d}{dx}(3x^5) = 3\cdot5x^{5-1} = 15x^4$,
  • $\dfrac{d}{dx}(-2x^3) = -2\cdot3x^{3-1} = -6x^2$,
  • $\dfrac{d}{dx}(7x) = 7\cdot1x^{1-1} = 7$,
  • $\dfrac{d}{dx}(-4) = 0$.

Putting the terms together gives the derivative formula:

$$f'(x)=15x^4-6x^2+7.$$

2. Compute $f'(0)$ and $f'(1)$

Substitute $x=0$ into $f'(x)$:

$$f'(0)=15(0)^4-6(0)^2+7=0-0+7=7.$$

Substitute $x=1$ into $f'(x)$:

$$f'(1)=15(1)^4-6(1)^2+7=15-6+7=16.$$

3. Signs and geometric interpretation

  • At $x=0$, we have $f'(0)=7>0$. The tangent line to the graph at $(0,-4)$ has a positive slope of $7$, meaning it rises $7$ units vertically for each $1$ unit horizontally. Near $x=0$, only the linear part $7x-4$ dominates because higher powers of $x$ vanish much faster than $x$.
  • At $x=1$, we have $f'(1)=16>0$. The tangent line at $(1,4)$ also has a positive slope, and because $16>7$, the graph is rising more than twice as steeply at $x=1$ as it is at $x=0$.

4. Global behavior notice

Notice that we can complete the square in $f'(x)$:

$$f'(x)=15\left(x^2-\frac{1}{5}\right)^2+\frac{32}{5}\ge\frac{32}{5}>0\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.$$

So in fact every tangent line to this polynomial has positive slope, and $f(x)$ is strictly increasing on the entire real line.

Quick verification

Near $x=0$, a polynomial behaves like its constant and linear terms:

$$f(x)=-4+7x-2x^3+3x^5 \approx -4+7x\quad\text{for }x\text{ close to }0.$$

The linear approximation at $x=0$ is $L_0(x)=-4+7x$, whose slope is $7=f'(0)$. ✓

Similarly, using a small step $h=0.001$ at $x=1$:

$$\frac{f(1.001)-f(1)}{0.001}=\frac{4.016024028015003-4}{0.001}\approx 16.024 \approx 16 = f'(1).\quad\checkmark$$

A concrete example

At $x=0$, the point on the curve is $(0,f(0))=(0,-4)$ and the tangent line has equation

$$y = -4 + 7x.$$

At $x=1$, the point on the curve is $(1,f(1))=(1,4)$ and the tangent line has equation

$$y = 4 + 16(x-1) = 16x-12.$$

If we move slightly from $x=1$ to $x=1.01$, the tangent line predicts $y\approx 4 + 16(0.01) = 4.16$, while the exact value is $f(1.01)\approx 4.1624$.

How I would explain this in class

To differentiate a polynomial, we apply the power rule $\frac{d}{dx}(x^n)=nx^{n-1}$ term by term and drop the constant $-4$. That gives $f'(x)=15x^4-6x^2+7$. Plugging in $x=0$ gives $7>0$ and plugging in $x=1$ gives $16>0$. Both values are positive, so the graph is rising at both points, and since $16>7$, the tangent line at $x=1$ is much steeper than at $x=0$.

Exercise 2. Powers, Roots, and the Domain

The question

For

$$f(x)=2\sqrt{x}-\frac{3}{x^2}+x^{3/2}$$
  1. rewrite the function using powers,
  2. determine the domain,
  3. compute the derivative,
  4. compare the domain of the function with the domain of its derivative.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

The general power rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^p\right)=px^{p-1}$$

works not only for positive integers $p$, but for any real exponent $p\in\mathbb{R}$ (on the domain where $x^p$ is differentiable). Radicals and fractions with $x^n$ in the denominator look like separate rules until we rewrite them using fractional and negative exponents:

$$\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2},\qquad \frac{1}{x^n}=x^{-n},\qquad x^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{x^m}.$$

Before differentiating, we must always check the domain — a derivative can only exist at interior points of the function's domain where the resulting formula is also defined.

1. Rewrite the function using powers

Convert each radical and reciprocal into power form $x^p$:

  • $2\sqrt{x} = 2x^{1/2}$,
  • $-\dfrac{3}{x^2} = -3x^{-2}$,
  • $x^{3/2}$ is already in power form.

Thus,

$$f(x)=2x^{1/2}-3x^{-2}+x^{3/2}.$$

2. Determine the domain of $f$

Look at the restrictions imposed by each term:

  • $\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}$ and $x^{3/2}=(\sqrt{x})^3$ require $x\ge0$ (for real values),
  • $\dfrac{3}{x^2}$ requires $x\neq0$ so that we do not divide by zero.

Combining $x\ge0$ and $x\neq0$ gives the domain of $f$:

$$D_f=(0,\infty).$$

3. Compute the derivative

Apply the power rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^p)=px^{p-1}$ term by term for $x>0$:

  • $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(2x^{1/2}\right) = 2\cdot\frac{1}{2}x^{1/2-1} = x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$,
  • $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(-3x^{-2}\right) = -3\cdot(-2)x^{-2-1} = 6x^{-3} = \frac{6}{x^3}$,
  • $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(x^{3/2}\right) = \frac{3}{2}x^{3/2-1} = \frac{3}{2}x^{1/2} = \frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}$.

Combining the three terms:

$$f'(x)=x^{-1/2}+6x^{-3}+\frac{3}{2}x^{1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}+\frac{6}{x^3}+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}.$$

4. Compare the domains of $f$ and $f'$

In $f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}+\dfrac{6}{x^3}+\dfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}$:

  • $\sqrt{x}$ requires $x\ge0$,
  • $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ and $\dfrac{6}{x^3}$ require $x\neq0$.

Therefore, the domain of the derivative is also

$$D_{f'}=(0,\infty)=D_f.$$

Note that if the term $-\dfrac{3}{x^2}$ had not been present, $2\sqrt{x}+x^{3/2}$ would have had domain $[0,\infty)$, while its derivative $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}+\dfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}$ would have lost the endpoint $x=0$ because $\sqrt{x}$ has a vertical tangent at $x=0^+$. Here, $x=0$ was already excluded from $D_f$ by the $-\dfrac{3}{x^2}$ term, so the two domains match: $D_{f'}=D_f=(0,\infty)$.

Quick verification

Let us evaluate both formulas at $x=1$ and check with a small increment:

  • Using our formula: $f'(1) = 1 + 6 + \dfrac{3}{2} = 8.5$.
  • At $x=1$, $f(1) = 2(1) - 3(1) + 1 = 0$.
  • At $x=1.0001$, $f(1.0001) = 2\sqrt{1.0001} - \dfrac{3}{(1.0001)^2} + (1.0001)^{3/2} \approx 0.000849911$.
  • Difference quotient: $\dfrac{f(1.0001)-f(1)}{0.0001} \approx 8.499 \approx 8.5 = f'(1)$. ✓

A concrete example

At $x=4\in(0,\infty)$:

$$f(4)=2\sqrt{4}-\frac{3}{4^2}+4^{3/2}=4-\frac{3}{16}+8=11.8125,$$

and the rate of change at $x=4$ is

$$f'(4)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4}}+\frac{6}{4^3}+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{4}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{6}{64}+3=3.59375.$$

Every term of $f'(x)$ is positive for $x>0$, so $f(x)$ is strictly increasing on all of $(0,\infty)$.

How I would explain this in class

Instead of using the quotient rule on $\frac{3}{x^2}$ or memorizing separate root rules, rewrite everything as powers: $f(x)=2x^{1/2}-3x^{-2}+x^{3/2}$. Since square roots need $x\ge0$ and dividing by $x^2$ forbids $x=0$, the domain is $(0,\infty)$. Then the power rule gives $f'(x)=x^{-1/2}+6x^{-3}+\frac{3}{2}x^{1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}+\frac{6}{x^3}+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}$, which has the exact same domain $(0,\infty)$.

Exercise 3. The Product Rule — Why Is It Needed?

The question

For

$$f(x)=(x^2+1)e^x$$

compute the derivative. Then compare the correct result with the incorrect idea

$$f'(x)\stackrel{?}{=}(2x)e^x$$

and explain which term is missing.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When a function is a product of two variable factors $u(x)$ and $v(x)$, the derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives. Instead, we must use the product rule:

$$(u(x)v(x))'=u'(x)v(x)+u(x)v'(x).$$

Why does it have two terms? Imagine a rectangle with side lengths $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ and area $A(x)=u(x)v(x)$. When $x$ changes by a tiny amount $\Delta x$, both sides grow simultaneously:

  • the change in width $u$ multiplied by the height $v$ gives $u'(x)v(x)\,\Delta x$,
  • the width $u$ multiplied by the change in height $v$ gives $u(x)v'(x)\,\Delta x$.

Leaving out either term ignores the growth of one of the two factors.

1. Identify the two factors and their derivatives

Write $f(x)=u(x)v(x)$ with:

  • $u(x)=x^2+1 \implies u'(x)=2x$,
  • $v(x)=e^x \implies v'(x)=e^x$.

2. Apply the product rule

Substitute $u,u',v,v'$ into $f'(x)=u'(x)v(x)+u(x)v'(x)$:

$$f'(x)=(2x)e^x+(x^2+1)e^x.$$

Factor out the common factor $e^x$ to write the derivative cleanly:

$$f'(x)=(x^2+2x+1)e^x=(x+1)^2e^x.$$

3. Compare with the incorrect formula and explain the missing term

The proposed guess was

$$f_{\text{wrong}}'(x)=(2x)e^x.$$

Because $\dfrac{d}{dx}(e^x)=e^x$, this mistake is doubly deceptive:

  1. A student who mistakenly thinks $(uv)'=u'v'$ computes $(x^2+1)'\cdot(e^x)'=(2x)e^x$.
  2. A student who differentiates the first factor $(x^2+1)$ and forgets to differentiate the second factor $e^x$ also gets $(2x)e^x$.

In both cases, the missing term is

$$u(x)v'(x)=(x^2+1)e^x,$$

which accounts for how fast $e^x$ changes while $x^2+1$ acts as the multiplier.

Quick verification

Let us test both formulas at $x=0$:

  • At $x=0$, $f(x)=(x^2+1)e^x$ has value $f(0)=1\cdot e^0=1$.
  • For small $x$, $x^2$ is negligible compared to $x$, so $f(x)=(1+x^2)e^x \approx 1\cdot(1+x)=1+x$, which has slope $1$ at $x=0$.
  • Our product-rule formula gives $f'(0)=(0+1)^2e^0=1$. ✓
  • The incorrect formula gives $f_{\text{wrong}}'(0)=2(0)e^0=0$, claiming a horizontal tangent at $x=0$ even though $f(0)=1$ and $f(0.1)=1.01\cdot e^{0.1}\approx 1.1162$ (slope $\approx 1.16$). ✓

A concrete example

Look at $x=-1$:

$$f'(-1)=(-1+1)^2e^{-1}=0.$$

Because $f'(x)=(x+1)^2e^x\ge0$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$, the function $f(x)=(x^2+1)e^x$ is strictly increasing everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$, pausing with a horizontal inflection tangent only at $x=-1$, not at $x=0$.

How I would explain this in class

Since $f(x)=(x^2+1)e^x$ is a product of two functions of $x$, we must use the product rule $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$. That gives $f'(x)=2xe^x+(x^2+1)e^x=(x+1)^2e^x$. Writing just $2xe^x$ is wrong because it misses the second term $(x^2+1)e^x$, which comes from keeping $(x^2+1)$ fixed and differentiating $e^x$.

Exercise 4. A Quotient and Domain Control

The question

For

$$f(x)=\frac{x^2+1}{x-1}$$

determine the domain, compute the derivative, simplify the result, and explain why the restriction $x\neq1$ remains important.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When a function has the form $f(x)=\dfrac{u(x)}{v(x)}$, we need two things:

  1. Domain control: the denominator cannot be zero ($v(x)\neq0$).
  2. The quotient rule: at every point where $u$ and $v$ are differentiable and $v(x)\neq0$,
$$\left(\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}\right)'=\frac{u'(x)v(x)-u(x)v'(x)}{(v(x))^2}.$$

Watch the order in the numerator: because of the minus sign, $u'v$ must come first and $uv'$ second (switching them flips the sign of the entire derivative).

1. Determine the domain

The numerator $x^2+1$ and denominator $x-1$ are polynomials defined for all real $x$, so the only restriction is that the denominator cannot vanish:

$$x-1\neq0 \iff x\neq1.$$

Thus the domain of $f$ is

$$D_f=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}=(-\infty,1)\cup(1,\infty).$$

2. Apply the quotient rule

Identify the numerator and denominator and differentiate each:

  • $u(x)=x^2+1 \implies u'(x)=2x$,
  • $v(x)=x-1 \implies v'(x)=1$.

Substitute into the quotient rule:

$$f'(x)=\frac{(2x)(x-1)-(x^2+1)(1)}{(x-1)^2}.$$

3. Simplify the result

Expand the numerator and combine like terms:

$$f'(x)=\frac{2x^2-2x-x^2-1}{(x-1)^2}=\frac{x^2-2x-1}{(x-1)^2}.$$

4. Why the restriction $x\neq1$ remains important

The restriction $x\neq1$ is crucial for three connected reasons:

  1. Algebraic: setting $x=1$ in $f'(x)$ produces $(1-1)^2=0$ in the denominator, so $f'(1)$ is undefined.
  2. Geometric: the line $x=1$ is a vertical asymptote of $f(x)$ ($\lim_{x\to1^-}f(x)=-\infty$ and $\lim_{x\to1^+}f(x)=+\infty$). There is no point on the graph at $x=1$, so no tangent line can exist there.
  3. Analytical: because $x=1$ splits the domain into two separate intervals $(-\infty,1)$ and $(1,\infty)$, any theorem about the sign of $f'(x)$ (such as monotonicity or the Mean Value Theorem) applies on each interval $(-\infty,1)$ and $(1,\infty)$ separately, never across the jump at $x=1$.

Quick verification

We can rewrite $f(x)$ by polynomial division for $x\neq1$:

$$f(x)=\frac{(x^2-1)+2}{x-1}=\frac{(x-1)(x+1)+2}{x-1}=x+1+\frac{2}{x-1}=x+1+2(x-1)^{-1}.$$

Differentiating term by term gives:

$$f'(x)=1+0-2(x-1)^{-2}=1-\frac{2}{(x-1)^2}=\frac{(x-1)^2-2}{(x-1)^2}=\frac{x^2-2x-1}{(x-1)^2}.\quad\checkmark$$

Both methods produce the exact same simplified formula.

A concrete example

At $x=2$:

$$f(2)=\frac{2^2+1}{2-1}=5,\qquad f'(2)=\frac{2^2-2(2)-1}{(2-1)^2}=\frac{4-4-1}{1}=-1.$$

At $x=0$:

$$f(0)=\frac{0^2+1}{0-1}=-1,\qquad f'(0)=\frac{0-0-1}{(0-1)^2}=-1.$$

Even though $f'(0)=-1<0$ and $f'(2)=-1<0$, notice that $f(0)=-1 < f(2)=5$! Why did the function value go up from $-1$ to $5$ if the derivative was negative at those points? Because the vertical asymptote at $x=1$ separates $x=0$ and $x=2$ into two disconnected branches.

How I would explain this in class

First, the denominator $x-1$ cannot be zero, so the domain is $x\neq1$. Next, using the quotient rule $\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$ with $u=x^2+1$ and $v=x-1$, we get $f'(x)=\frac{2x(x-1)-(x^2+1)\cdot1}{(x-1)^2}=\frac{x^2-2x-1}{(x-1)^2}$. We must keep the condition $x\neq1$ because the graph has a vertical asymptote there, splitting the function into two separate branches.

Exercise 5. The Chain Rule as Working Through Layers

The question

For

$$f(x)=(3x^2-2x+1)^5$$

identify the inner and outer functions, compute their derivatives, and combine the result according to the chain rule. Explain the origin of the additional factor.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When we evaluate $f(x)=(3x^2-2x+1)^5$ for a number $x$, we do it in two steps (layers):

  1. First compute the inside expression $u=g(x)=3x^2-2x+1$.
  2. Then raise that result to the $5$-th power: $y=h(u)=u^5$.

So $f(x)=h(g(x))$ is a composite function. The chain rule states that the derivative of a composition is the product of the derivatives of its layers:

$$f'(x)=h'(g(x))\cdot g'(x) \qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}.$$

1. Identify the inner and outer functions

Decompose $f(x)=(3x^2-2x+1)^5$ into its two layers:

  • Inner function: $u=g(x)=3x^2-2x+1$,
  • Outer function: $y=h(u)=u^5$.

2. Differentiate each layer separately

  • Derivative of the outer function with respect to $u$:
$$h'(u)=\frac{dy}{du}=5u^4.$$
  • Derivative of the inner function with respect to $x$:
$$g'(x)=\frac{du}{dx}=6x-2.$$

3. Combine according to the chain rule

Substitute $u=3x^2-2x+1$ back into $h'(u)$ and multiply by $g'(x)$:

$$f'(x)=5(3x^2-2x+1)^4\cdot(6x-2).$$

We can also factor $2$ out of $(6x-2)=2(3x-1)$ to write:

$$f'(x)=10(3x-1)(3x^2-2x+1)^4.$$

4. Explain the origin of the additional factor $(6x-2)$

Why isn't the derivative just $5(3x^2-2x+1)^4$?

Because $5u^4$ only measures how fast the output $y=u^5$ changes per unit change in $u$, not per unit change in $x$. When $x$ changes by a small amount $\Delta x$, the intermediate quantity $u=3x^2-2x+1$ does not change by $\Delta x$; it changes by approximately

$$\Delta u \approx g'(x)\,\Delta x = (6x-2)\,\Delta x.$$

Then the output $y=u^5$ changes by

$$\Delta y \approx 5u^4\,\Delta u \approx 5(3x^2-2x+1)^4(6x-2)\,\Delta x.$$

The factor $(6x-2)$ is the local magnification factor of the inner layer $u(x)$.

Quick verification

Check at $x=0$:

  • Using our chain rule formula:
$$f'(0)=5(0-0+1)^4(0-2)=5(1)^4(-2)=-10.$$
  • Alternatively, expand only the lowest-degree terms of $(1-2x+3x^2)^5$ near $x=0$ using the binomial theorem $(1+w)^5 = 1 + 5w + 10w^2 + \dots$:
$$f(x) = 1 + 5(-2x+3x^2) + \dots = 1 - 10x + O(x^2).$$

The coefficient of $x$ is $-10$, which must equal $f'(0)$. ✓

A concrete example

Look at $x=\dfrac{1}{3}$:

$$g'\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)=6\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)-2=0 \implies f'\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)=5\left(3\cdot\frac{1}{9}-\frac{2}{3}+1\right)^4\cdot 0=0.$$

Because the inner parabola $u=3x^2-2x+1$ has its vertex (horizontal tangent) at $x=\dfrac{1}{3}$, a tiny change in $x$ around $\dfrac{1}{3}$ produces zero first-order change in $u$, and therefore zero first-order change in $u^5$.

How I would explain this in class

We view $f(x)=(3x^2-2x+1)^5$ like peeling an onion from the outside in. The outer layer is a $5$-th power $u^5$, whose derivative is $5u^4=5(3x^2-2x+1)^4$, evaluated at the unchanged inner function. Then the chain rule tells us to multiply by the derivative of the inner layer $u=3x^2-2x+1$, which is $6x-2$, because $u$ itself is changing at rate $6x-2$ with respect to $x$.

Exercise 6. Product Rule and Trigonometry

The question

For

$$f(x)=x\sin x+\cos x$$

compute the derivative and simplify the result. Then compute $f'(0)$ and interpret the direction of the tangent line at that point.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

First, inspect the overall structure of $f(x)=x\sin x+\cos x$:

  • It is a sum of two terms: $x\sin x$ and $\cos x$.
  • The first term $x\sin x$ is a product of two functions of $x$, namely $u(x)=x$ and $v(x)=\sin x$, so it requires the product rule $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$.
  • We also need the basic trigonometric derivatives:
$$\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x)=\cos x,\qquad \frac{d}{dx}(\cos x)=-\sin x.$$

1. Differentiate the product term $x\sin x$

Apply the product rule to $x\sin x$ with $u(x)=x$ and $v(x)=\sin x$:

  • $u'(x)=1$,
  • $v'(x)=\cos x$.

Therefore,

$$\frac{d}{dx}(x\sin x)=1\cdot\sin x+x\cdot\cos x=\sin x+x\cos x.$$

2. Differentiate the second term and simplify $f'(x)$

The derivative of $\cos x$ is $-\sin x$. Adding the two parts together:

$$f'(x)=(\sin x+x\cos x)+(-\sin x)=x\cos x.$$

Notice the neat cancellation: the $+\sin x$ from the product rule and the $-\sin x$ from differentiating $\cos x$ cancel out completely, leaving the compact expression $f'(x)=x\cos x$.

3. Compute $f'(0)$ and interpret the tangent line

Substitute $x=0$ into $f'(x)=x\cos x$:

$$f'(0)=0\cdot\cos 0=0\cdot 1=0.$$

What does $f'(0)=0$ mean geometrically?

  • At $x=0$, the point on the graph is $(0,f(0))=(0,0\cdot\sin 0+\cos 0)=(0,1)$.
  • Since the slope is $f'(0)=0$, the tangent line to the graph at $(0,1)$ is horizontal (parallel to the $x$-axis), with equation $y=1$.

Quick verification

Let us check the behavior of $f(x)=x\sin x+\cos x$ near $x=0$:

  1. Symmetry check: $f(-x)=(-x)\sin(-x)+\cos(-x)=x\sin x+\cos x=f(x)$. Every differentiable even function is symmetric across the $y$-axis and therefore must have $f'(0)=0$. ✓
  2. Small-$x$ approximation: near $x=0$, $\sin x\approx x$ and $\cos x\approx 1-\frac{1}{2}x^2$, so
$$f(x)\approx x(x)+\left(1-\frac{1}{2}x^2\right)=1+\frac{1}{2}x^2.$$

The linear term in $x$ is $0\cdot x$, confirming $f'(0)=0$, and the parabola $1+\frac{1}{2}x^2$ has a horizontal tangent and local minimum at $(0,1)$. ✓

A concrete example

At $x=\dfrac{\pi}{3}$:

$$f'\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{\pi}{6}\approx 0.5236>0,$$

while at $x=-\dfrac{\pi}{3}$:

$$f'\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=-\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=-\frac{\pi}{6}\approx -0.5236<0.$$

As $x$ passes through $0$ from negative to positive values, $f'(x)=x\cos x$ changes sign from negative to positive, so $(0,1)$ is a local minimum where the curve levels off horizontally.

How I would explain this in class

To differentiate $f(x)=x\sin x+\cos x$, we use the product rule on $x\sin x$ to get $\sin x+x\cos x$, and then add the derivative of $\cos x$, which is $-\sin x$. The $\sin x$ and $-\sin x$ terms cancel out, leaving $f'(x)=x\cos x$. At $x=0$, $f'(0)=0\cdot\cos 0=0$, which tells us that the tangent line at $(0,1)$ is completely horizontal.

Exercise 7. A Trigonometric Composition

The question

For

$$f(x)=\sin(x^2+1)$$

compute the derivative. Explicitly identify the outer function, the inner function, and the derivative of the inner function. Finally, determine the sign of $f'(0)$.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Unlike $x\sin x$, the expression $\sin(x^2+1)$ is not a product ("$\sin$" by itself is not a number or factor; it is an operation applied to the angle $x^2+1$). Instead, $f(x)=\sin(x^2+1)$ is a composition $h(g(x))$:

  • first, $x$ is mapped to the angle $u=g(x)=x^2+1$,
  • second, the sine function is applied to $u$: $y=h(u)=\sin u$.

By the chain rule,

$$\frac{d}{dx}\big(\sin(g(x))\big)=\cos(g(x))\cdot g'(x).$$

1. Identify the outer function, inner function, and inner derivative

Break $f(x)=\sin(x^2+1)$ into its components:

  • Inner function: $u=g(x)=x^2+1$,
  • Outer function: $y=h(u)=\sin u$,
  • Derivative of the outer function: $h'(u)=\cos u=\cos(x^2+1)$,
  • Derivative of the inner function: $g'(x)=\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2+1)=2x$.

2. Compute the derivative $f'(x)$

Multiply the derivative of the outer function (evaluated at the inner function $x^2+1$) by the derivative of the inner function:

$$f'(x)=\cos(x^2+1)\cdot 2x=2x\cos(x^2+1).$$

(Writing the algebraic factor $2x$ in front of $\cos(x^2+1)$ avoids any ambiguity about whether $2x$ is inside or outside the cosine.)

3. Determine the sign of $f'(0)$

Substitute $x=0$ into $f'(x)=2x\cos(x^2+1)$:

$$f'(0)=2(0)\cos(0^2+1)=0\cdot\cos(1)=0.$$

Because $f'(0)=0$, the value is neither positive nor negative (its sign is zero). Even though $\cos(1)\approx 0.5403>0$, the factor $2x$ from the derivative of the inner function vanishes at $x=0$, making the tangent line horizontal at $(0,\sin 1)$.

Quick verification

Let us verify using two independent observations:

  1. Even symmetry: $f(-x)=\sin((-x)^2+1)=\sin(x^2+1)=f(x)$. Because $f$ is an even differentiable function, its graph is symmetric across the $y$-axis, so its slope at $x=0$ must be $f'(0)=0$. ✓
  2. Numerical difference quotient at $x=1$:
  • Formula value: $f'(1)=2(1)\cos(1^2+1)=2\cos(2)\approx 2(-0.416147)=-0.832294$.
  • Difference quotient with $h=0.0001$:
$$\frac{\sin(1.0001^2+1)-\sin(2)}{0.0001}=\frac{\sin(2.00020001)-\sin(2)}{0.0001}\approx -0.83252 \approx f'(1).\quad\checkmark$$

A concrete example

Compare $x=-0.5$, $x=0$, and $x=0.5$ (note that for $x\in[-0.5,0.5]$, the angle $x^2+1\in[1,1.25]$ radians lies in the first quadrant $(0,\frac{\pi}{2})$, so $\cos(x^2+1)>0$):

  • At $x=-0.5$: $f'(-0.5)=-1\cdot\cos(1.25)\approx -0.3153<0$ (decreasing),
  • At $x=0$: $f'(0)=0$ (horizontal tangent at the local minimum $y=\sin 1\approx 0.8415$),
  • At $x=0.5$: $f'(0.5)=1\cdot\cos(1.25)\approx +0.3153>0$ (increasing).

How I would explain this in class

$\sin(x^2+1)$ is a composition: the outer function is $\sin u$ and the inner function is $u=x^2+1$. By the chain rule, we differentiate the outer sine to get $\cos(x^2+1)$, keeping the inside untouched, and multiply by the derivative of the inside, which is $2x$. That gives $f'(x)=2x\cos(x^2+1)$. At $x=0$, the factor $2x$ equals $0$, so $f'(0)=0$ and the tangent line is horizontal.

Exercise 8. Two Exponential Functions

The question

For

$$f(x)=e^{2x-3}+5^x$$

compute the derivative. Explain why the factor $2$ appears in the first term and why $\ln 5$ appears in the second.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Both terms in $f(x)=e^{2x-3}+5^x$ are exponential functions, because the variable $x$ is in the exponent rather than the base. However, they differ from the standard exponential $e^x$ in two distinct ways:

  1. First term ($e^{2x-3}$): the base is the natural base $e$, where $\dfrac{d}{du}(e^u)=e^u$, but the exponent is a composite linear function $u(x)=2x-3$.
  2. Second term ($5^x$): the exponent is simply $x$, but the base is $a=5\neq e$, for which
$$\frac{d}{dx}(a^x)=a^x\ln a.$$

Interestingly, both extra factors can be understood through the chain rule once we rewrite $5^x=e^{x\ln 5}$.

1. Differentiate the first term $e^{2x-3}$

View $e^{2x-3}$ as a composition $e^{u(x)}$ with inner function $u(x)=2x-3$:

  • Derivative of outer function $e^u$: $e^u=e^{2x-3}$,
  • Derivative of inner function $u(x)=2x-3$: $u'(x)=2$.

By the chain rule:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{2x-3}\right)=e^{2x-3}\cdot 2=2e^{2x-3}.$$

2. Differentiate the second term $5^x$

Apply the general exponential derivative rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}(a^x)=a^x\ln a$ with $a=5$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(5^x\right)=5^x\ln 5.$$

3. Combine to get $f'(x)$

Adding the derivatives of the two terms gives:

$$f'(x)=2e^{2x-3}+5^x\ln 5.$$

4. Why $2$ appears in the first term and $\ln 5$ in the second

Both factors measure the rate of change of the natural-base exponent:

  • Why $2$ appears in $2e^{2x-3}$: The base is already $e$ (for which $\ln e=1$), so differentiating the outer exponential produces no logarithm factor. Instead, the exponent $u=2x-3$ changes twice as fast as $x$ ($\frac{du}{dx}=2$), so the chain rule multiplies by $2$.
  • Why $\ln 5$ appears in $5^x\ln 5$: Since $5=e^{\ln 5}$, we can rewrite $5^x$ in base $e$ as
$$5^x=\left(e^{\ln 5}\right)^x=e^{(\ln 5)x}.$$

Now apply the exact same chain rule as in the first term! The inner linear exponent is $v(x)=(\ln 5)x$, whose derivative with respect to $x$ is $v'(x)=\ln 5$. Thus:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(5^x\right)=\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{(\ln 5)x}\right)=e^{(\ln 5)x}\cdot\ln 5=5^x\ln 5.$$

So $\ln 5\approx 1.6094$ is the conversion factor that rescales base-$5$ growth into natural (base-$e$) instantaneous growth.

Quick verification

We can also rewrite the first term using exponent laws before differentiating:

$$e^{2x-3}=e^{-3}\cdot e^{2x}=e^{-3}\cdot\left(e^2\right)^x.$$

Viewing this as a constant $e^{-3}$ times an exponential with base $a=e^2$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{-3}(e^2)^x\right)=e^{-3}(e^2)^x\ln(e^2)=e^{-3}e^{2x}\cdot 2=2e^{2x-3}.\quad\checkmark$$

Both perspectives — chain rule on $e^{u(x)}$ and base-change rule $a^x\ln a$ — agree completely!

A concrete example

At $x=1.5$:

  • The first term has exponent $2(1.5)-3=0$, so $e^{2(1.5)-3}=e^0=1$, and its instantaneous rate of change is $2e^0=2$.
  • The second term has value $5^{1.5}=5\sqrt{5}\approx 11.1803$, and its instantaneous rate of change is $5^{1.5}\ln 5\approx 11.1803\times 1.6094\approx 17.994$.
  • In total, $f'(1.5)=2+5\sqrt{5}\ln 5\approx 19.994$.

How I would explain this in class

The derivative of $f(x)=e^{2x-3}+5^x$ is $f'(x)=2e^{2x-3}+5^x\ln 5$. In the first term, the base is $e$, so the exponential keeps its form and the chain rule multiplies by $(2x-3)'=2$. In the second term, the base is $5$ instead of $e$; since $5^x=e^{x\ln 5}$, differentiating brings down the constant factor $\ln 5$ from the exponent.

Exercise 9. A Logarithm and the Chain Rule

The question

For

$$f(x)=\ln(x^2+1)$$

compute the derivative. Then determine the sign of $f'(x)$ for $x<0$, $x=0$, and $x>0$, and describe qualitatively how the function behaves around zero.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

First, notice that $x^2+1\ge1>0$ for every real number $x$, so the domain of $f(x)=\ln(x^2+1)$ is the entire real line $\mathbb{R}$.

Second, $f(x)=\ln(g(x))$ is a composition of the natural logarithm $\ln u$ (whose derivative is $\frac{1}{u}$) with the inner polynomial $u=g(x)=x^2+1$. By the chain rule:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\big(\ln(g(x))\big)=\frac{1}{g(x)}\cdot g'(x)=\frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}.$$

Finally, the sign of $f'(x)$ tells us the monotonicity of $f$:

  • $f'(x)<0$ on an interval $\implies f$ is strictly decreasing there,
  • $f'(x)=0$ $\implies$ horizontal tangent (stationary point),
  • $f'(x)>0$ on an interval $\implies f$ is strictly increasing there.

1. Compute the derivative

Identify the layers of $f(x)=\ln(x^2+1)$:

  • Outer function: $h(u)=\ln u \implies h'(u)=\dfrac{1}{u}$,
  • Inner function: $u=g(x)=x^2+1 \implies g'(x)=2x$.

Multiply by the chain rule:

$$f'(x)=\frac{1}{x^2+1}\cdot 2x=\frac{2x}{x^2+1}.$$

2. Sign analysis of $f'(x)$

In the fraction $f'(x)=\dfrac{2x}{x^2+1}$, the denominator satisfies

$$x^2+1\ge1>0\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.$$

Because the denominator is always strictly positive, the sign of $f'(x)$ is determined entirely by the numerator $2x$:

  • For $x<0$: $2x<0$, so $f'(x)<0$ (negative).
  • For $x=0$: $2x=0$, so $f'(0)=0$ (zero).
  • For $x>0$: $2x>0$, so $f'(x)>0$ (positive).

3. Qualitative behavior of $f(x)$ around zero

  • As $x$ approaches $0$ from the left ($x<0$), $f'(x)<0$, so the graph slopes downward toward the origin.
  • At $x=0$, $f(0)=\ln(0^2+1)=\ln 1=0$ and $f'(0)=0$. The graph touches the origin $(0,0)$ with a horizontal tangent line ($y=0$, the $x$-axis).
  • As $x$ moves to the right of $0$ ($x>0$), $f'(x)>0$, so the graph turns upward and increases symmetrically ($f(-x)=f(x)$).
  • Therefore, $f(x)$ has a smooth U-shaped valley (global minimum) at $(0,0)$.

Quick verification

Let us check near $x=0$ using the standard approximation $\ln(1+u)\approx u$ for small $u$:

Setting $u=x^2$, for $x$ very close to $0$ we have:

$$f(x)=\ln(1+x^2)\approx x^2.$$

Near the origin, $f(x)=\ln(x^2+1)$ looks almost indistinguishable from the standard parabola $y=x^2$, whose derivative is $2x$ — which matches $\dfrac{2x}{x^2+1}\approx \dfrac{2x}{1}=2x$ when $x$ is small! ✓

A concrete example

Evaluate $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$ at $x=-1$, $0$, and $1$:

  • At $x=-1$: $f(-1)=\ln 2\approx 0.693$, and $f'(-1)=\dfrac{2(-1)}{(-1)^2+1}=-1<0$.
  • At $x=0$: $f(0)=\ln 1=0$, and $f'(0)=0$.
  • At $x=1$: $f(1)=\ln 2\approx 0.693$, and $f'(1)=\dfrac{2(1)}{1^2+1}=+1>0$.

The function drops from $\ln 2$ at $x=-1$ down to $0$ at $x=0$, and rises symmetrically back to $\ln 2$ at $x=1$.

How I would explain this in class

By the chain rule for logarithms, $(\ln g(x))'=\frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}$, so $f'(x)=\frac{2x}{x^2+1}$. Since the denominator $x^2+1$ is always positive, the sign of $f'(x)$ is the same as the sign of $2x$: negative for $x<0$, zero at $x=0$, and positive for $x>0$. This means the function decreases on $(-\infty,0)$, hits a minimum with a horizontal tangent at $(0,0)$, and increases on $(0,\infty)$.

Exercise 10. The Tangent Line as an Application of the Derivative

The question

For

$$f(x)=x^2-3x+1$$

find the equation of the tangent line at the point with $x=2$. Give the point of tangency, the value $f'(2)$, the equation of the line, and verify that the point lies on the obtained tangent line.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

To write the equation of a line, we need a point $(x_0,y_0)$ on the line and its slope $m$. For the tangent line to the graph $y=f(x)$ at $x_0$:

  1. Point of tangency: $(x_0,y_0)=(x_0,f(x_0))$ — obtained by plugging $x_0$ into the original function $f$.
  2. Slope of the tangent line: $m=f'(x_0)$ — obtained by plugging $x_0$ into the derivative $f'$.
  3. Point-slope equation of the tangent line:
$$y-f(x_0)=f'(x_0)(x-x_0) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0).$$

(A common mistake is plugging $f'(x)$ instead of the constant number $f'(x_0)$ into the line equation — the slope $m=f'(2)$ must be a constant number so that the equation represents a straight line.)

1. Find the point of tangency

Evaluate the function $f(x)=x^2-3x+1$ at $x_0=2$:

$$f(2)=2^2-3(2)+1=4-6+1=-1.$$

So the point of tangency is

$$P=(2,-1).$$

2. Compute the derivative and the slope $f'(2)$

Differentiate $f(x)=x^2-3x+1$ using the power rule:

$$f'(x)=2x-3.$$

Evaluate $f'(x)$ at $x_0=2$ to get the slope of the tangent line:

$$m=f'(2)=2(2)-3=4-3=1.$$

3. Write the equation of the tangent line

Substitute $(x_0,y_0)=(2,-1)$ and $m=1$ into the tangent line formula $y-y_0=m(x-x_0)$:

$$y-(-1)=1\cdot(x-2)$$
$$y+1=x-2$$
$$y=x-3.$$

4. Verify that the point of tangency lies on the tangent line

Substitute $x=2$ into the tangent line equation $y=x-3$:

$$y(2)=2-3=-1=f(2).\quad\checkmark$$

Thus the point $P=(2,-1)$ lies on the line $y=x-3$.

Quick verification

For a parabola, a line is tangent to the graph if and only if the system of equations has a double root (zero discriminant) at the point of tangency. Let us intersect $y=x^2-3x+1$ and $y=x-3$:

$$x^2-3x+1=x-3 \iff x^2-4x+4=0 \iff (x-2)^2=0.$$

Since $(x-2)^2=0$ has a double root at $x=2$, the line $y=x-3$ touches the parabola at $x=2$ without crossing it and stays strictly below the parabola ($x^2-3x+1-(x-3)=(x-2)^2\ge0$) everywhere else. ✓

A concrete example

Use the tangent line $L(x)=x-3$ to approximate $f(2.1)$:

  • Tangent line approximation: $L(2.1)=2.1-3=-0.9$.
  • Exact function value: $f(2.1)=(2.1)^2-3(2.1)+1=4.41-6.3+1=-0.89$.
  • The discrepancy is only $0.01=(2.1-2)^2$, showing that the tangent line is the best linear approximation to $f(x)$ near $x=2$.

How I would explain this in class

To find the tangent line at $x=2$, we need two numbers: the $y$-coordinate and the slope. First, $f(2)=2^2-3(2)+1=-1$, so the point of tangency is $(2,-1)$. Second, the derivative is $f'(x)=2x-3$, so the slope at $x=2$ is $f'(2)=2(2)-3=1$. Using point-slope form $y-(-1)=1(x-2)$, we get $y=x-3$, and plugging in $x=2$ gives $y=2-3=-1$, confirming the point is on the line.

Exercise 11. Derivative from the Definition

The question

Using only

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h},$$

find the derivative of $f(x)=x^2$. Compare the result with the power rule and indicate where the limit is used in the calculation.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

For a nonzero step $h\neq0$, the difference quotient

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

is the slope of the secant line passing through the two points $(x,f(x))$ and $(x+h,f(x+h))$ on the graph. We cannot simply plug $h=0$ into this fraction at the start because that gives the indeterminate form $\dfrac{0}{0}$.

Instead, we must:

  1. expand $f(x+h)-f(x)$ algebraically,
  2. factor out $h$ in the numerator and cancel it with the $h$ in the denominator (valid because $h\neq0$ inside the limit),
  3. take the limit as $h\to0$ once the division-by-zero obstacle has been removed.

1. Set up and expand the difference quotient

For $f(x)=x^2$, evaluate $f(x+h)$ by replacing $x$ with $(x+h)$:

$$f(x+h)=(x+h)^2=x^2+2xh+h^2.$$

Subtract $f(x)=x^2$:

$$f(x+h)-f(x)=(x^2+2xh+h^2)-x^2=2xh+h^2.$$

Divide by $h$ (for $h\neq0$):

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}.$$

2. Factor and cancel $h$

Factor $h$ out of the numerator:

$$\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}=\frac{h(2x+h)}{h}=2x+h \qquad (h\neq0).$$

Geometrically, $2x+h$ is the exact slope of the secant line between $x$ and $x+h$.

3. Take the limit as $h\to0$ (and indicate where it is used)

Now apply the limit as $h\to0$:

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h)=2x+0=2x.$$

Where is the limit used?

  • In steps 1 and 2, we used the fact that the limit as $h\to0$ examines $h\neq0$, which allowed us to cancel $\frac{h}{h}=1$.
  • In the final step $\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h)=2x$, the limit is used to make the remaining increment $h$ vanish (by continuity of the linear function $h\mapsto 2x+h$ at $h=0$).

4. Compare with the power rule

The power rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n)=nx^{n-1}$ for $n=2$ gives

$$\frac{d}{dx}(x^2)=2x^{2-1}=2x.$$

The results are identical! In fact, the power rule is not an arbitrary axiom — it is a shortcut obtained by performing this exact binomial expansion and limit once and for all for general $n$:

$$(x+h)^n-x^n=nx^{n-1}h+\binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h^2+\dots+h^n \implies \lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^n-x^n}{h}=nx^{n-1}.$$

Quick verification

Think of $f(x)=x^2$ as the area of a square of side length $x$. If we increase the side length from $x$ to $x+h$:

  • The square gains two rectangular strips of dimensions $x\times h$ (total area $2xh$) and one tiny corner square of dimensions $h\times h$ (area $h^2$).
  • Total area change: $\Delta A = 2xh+h^2$.
  • Rate of change per unit of $h$: $\dfrac{\Delta A}{h}=2x+h$.
  • As the strip thickness $h\to0$, the tiny corner contributes $h\to0$, leaving the two side edges of total length $2x$. ✓

A concrete example

At $x=3$, our derivative is $f'(3)=2(3)=6$, and the secant slope formula is $2(3)+h=6+h$:

  • For $h=0.1$: secant slope between $3$ and $3.1$ is $\dfrac{3.1^2-3^2}{0.1}=\dfrac{9.61-9}{0.1}=6.1=6+0.1$.
  • For $h=0.01$: secant slope is $6.01$.
  • For $h=0.001$: secant slope is $6.001$.
  • In the limit as $h\to0$, the extra $+h$ disappears and we get the exact tangent slope $6$.

How I would explain this in class

To find the derivative of $x^2$ from the definition, we write the difference quotient $\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}$. Expanding $(x+h)^2=x^2+2xh+h^2$ cancels the $x^2$ terms and leaves $\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}$. Since $h\neq0$ before taking the limit, we cancel $h$ to get $2x+h$. Finally, taking the limit as $h\to0$ makes the $+h$ term disappear, giving $2x$, which matches the power rule $2x^{2-1}=2x$.

Exercise 12. Several Rules at Once

The question

For

$$f(x)=(x^2+1)^3e^x$$

compute the derivative. Before calculating, write down a plan: which rule applies to the whole expression and which rule applies to one of its factors. Finally, organize the result by factoring out common factors.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When an expression combines several operations, we always differentiate from the outermost operation inward:

  1. Top-level structure (the whole expression): $f(x)=\underbrace{(x^2+1)^3}_{u(x)}\cdot\underbrace{e^x}_{v(x)}$ is a product of two factors. Therefore, the product rule $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$ governs the overall derivative.
  2. Sub-problem (the first factor): to find $u'(x)$ for $u(x)=(x^2+1)^3$, notice that $u(x)$ is a composition (an inner polynomial $x^2+1$ raised to the $3$-rd power). Therefore, we must apply the chain rule inside the first step of the product rule.

1. Write the differentiation plan

  • Step A (Outer rule — Product Rule): Write $f(x)=u(x)v(x)$ with $u(x)=(x^2+1)^3$ and $v(x)=e^x$, so
$$f'(x)=u'(x)v(x)+u(x)v'(x).$$
  • Step B (Inner rule — Chain Rule on $u(x)$): Differentiate $u(x)=(x^2+1)^3$ by differentiating the outer cube $w^3\mapsto 3w^2$ and multiplying by the derivative of the inner polynomial $w=x^2+1\mapsto 2x$.
  • Step C (Algebraic cleanup): Factor out the greatest common factors $(x^2+1)^2$ and $e^x$.

2. Differentiate each factor

For the first factor $u(x)=(x^2+1)^3$, apply the chain rule:

$$u'(x)=3(x^2+1)^2\cdot\frac{d}{dx}(x^2+1)=3(x^2+1)^2\cdot(2x)=6x(x^2+1)^2.$$

For the second factor $v(x)=e^x$:

$$v'(x)=e^x.$$

3. Combine with the product rule

Substitute $u,u',v,v'$ into $f'(x)=u'(x)v(x)+u(x)v'(x)$:

$$f'(x)=6x(x^2+1)^2e^x+(x^2+1)^3e^x.$$

4. Factor out common factors

Both terms contain $(x^2+1)^2$ and $e^x$. Factor out $(x^2+1)^2e^x$:

$$f'(x)=(x^2+1)^2e^x\big[6x+(x^2+1)\big]=(x^2+1)^2(x^2+6x+1)e^x.$$

Why is this factored form much better than the unfactored sum?

  • We can immediately see the sign of $f'(x)$: since $(x^2+1)^2>0$ and $e^x>0$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$, the zeros and sign changes of $f'(x)$ come solely from the quadratic factor $x^2+6x+1$.

Quick verification

Let us check at $x=0$ and $x=1$:

  • At $x=0$:
  • Factored formula: $f'(0)=(0+1)^2(0+0+1)e^0=1\cdot1\cdot1=1$.
  • Small-$x$ expansion of $f(x)=(1+x^2)^3e^x$: since $(1+x^2)^3=1+3x^2+\dots$ has no linear $x$ term, near $x=0$ we have $f(x)\approx 1\cdot(1+x)=1+x$, so $f'(0)=1$. ✓
  • At $x=1$:
  • Unfactored sum: $6(1)(2)^2e^1+(2)^3e^1=24e+8e=32e$.
  • Factored formula: $(1+1)^2(1+6+1)e^1=4\cdot8\cdot e=32e$. ✓

A concrete example

To find the critical points of $f(x)=(x^2+1)^3e^x$ (where the tangent line is horizontal, $f'(x)=0$), our factored form makes the task effortless:

$$(x^2+1)^2(x^2+6x+1)e^x=0 \iff x^2+6x+1=0 \iff x=-3\pm\sqrt{9-1}=-3\pm2\sqrt{2}.$$

Without factoring out $(x^2+1)^2e^x$, solving $6x(x^2+1)^2e^x+(x^2+1)^3e^x=0$ would look like a degree-$6$ equation!

How I would explain this in class

Before differentiating $f(x)=(x^2+1)^3e^x$, we look at its overall structure: it is a product of $(x^2+1)^3$ and $e^x$, so we start with the product rule. When we need the derivative of the first factor $(x^2+1)^3$, we use the chain rule to get $3(x^2+1)^2\cdot 2x=6x(x^2+1)^2$. Putting the product rule together gives $6x(x^2+1)^2e^x+(x^2+1)^3e^x$, and factoring out $(x^2+1)^2e^x$ simplifies the answer to $(x^2+1)^2(x^2+6x+1)e^x$.

Exercise 13. A Multi-Layer Composition

The question

For

$$f(x)=e^{\sin(x^2)}$$

compute the derivative. First write the layers

$$x\longrightarrow x^2\longrightarrow \sin(x^2)\longrightarrow e^{\sin(x^2)}$$

and explain why differentiation moves through them in the reverse order.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A three-layer composition $f(x)=F(G(H(x)))$ works like a three-stage pipeline:

  • Evaluation starts at the innermost input $x$ and applies $H$, then $G$, then $F$ moving inside-out.
  • Differentiation by the chain rule unwraps the layers moving outside-in (in the reverse order of evaluation), multiplying the derivative of each layer evaluated at everything inside it:
$$f'(x)=F'\big(G(H(x))\big)\cdot G'\big(H(x)\big)\cdot H'(x).$$

In Leibniz notation, if $u=x^2$, $v=\sin u$, and $y=e^v$, then

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{dv}\cdot\frac{dv}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}.$$

1. Write the layers of evaluation

When we plug a number $x$ into $f(x)=e^{\sin(x^2)}$, we compute three intermediate steps in order from inside to outside:

$$x \xrightarrow{\text{square}} u=x^2 \xrightarrow{\text{sine}} v=\sin(x^2) \xrightarrow{\text{exponential}} y=e^{\sin(x^2)}.$$
  • Layer 1 (innermost): $u=H(x)=x^2$,
  • Layer 2 (middle): $v=G(u)=\sin u=\sin(x^2)$,
  • Layer 3 (outermost): $y=F(v)=e^v=e^{\sin(x^2)}$.

2. Differentiate layer by layer in reverse order

Unwrap the composition from the outermost layer to the innermost layer:

  1. Outermost layer ($e^v$): differentiate $e^v$ with respect to its argument $v=\sin(x^2)$:
$$\frac{dy}{dv}=e^v=e^{\sin(x^2)}.$$
  1. Middle layer ($\sin u$): differentiate $\sin u$ with respect to its argument $u=x^2$:
$$\frac{dv}{du}=\cos u=\cos(x^2).$$
  1. Innermost layer ($x^2$): differentiate $x^2$ with respect to $x$:
$$\frac{du}{dx}=2x.$$

3. Multiply the factors to obtain $f'(x)$

Multiply the three derivatives together:

$$f'(x)=e^{\sin(x^2)}\cdot\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x=2x\cos(x^2)e^{\sin(x^2)}.$$

4. Why differentiation moves in the reverse order of evaluation

Think of wrapping and unwrapping a gift box:

  • When evaluating $f(x)$, $x$ is placed inside the squaring box $x^2$, which is placed inside the $\sin(\cdot)$ box, which is finally placed inside the $e^{(\cdot)}$ box.
  • When differentiating $f(x)$, we ask how the final output $y=e^{\sin(x^2)}$ responds to a change in $x$. At the top level, $f(x)$ is an exponential function $e^{(\text{something})}$, so we must first open the outermost $e^{(\cdot)}$ box while keeping its contents $\sin(x^2)$ intact; only then does the chain rule ask us to differentiate what was inside ($\sin(x^2)$), which in turn requires opening the middle $\sin(\cdot)$ box before finally reaching the innermost $x^2$ box.

(Note: because multiplication of real numbers is commutative, the final product $e^{\sin(x^2)}\cdot\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x=2x\cos(x^2)e^{\sin(x^2)}$ can be written in any order, even though the logical process of peeling off layers proceeds from outside to inside.)

Quick verification

Let us approximate $f(x)=e^{\sin(x^2)}$ near $x=0$ using $\sin u\approx u$ and $e^v\approx 1+v$:

$$f(x)=e^{\sin(x^2)}\approx e^{x^2}\approx 1+x^2\quad\text{for }x\text{ near }0.$$

Differentiating $1+x^2$ gives $2x$. Now check our exact formula as $x\to0$:

$$f'(x)=2x\cos(x^2)e^{\sin(x^2)}\approx 2x\cdot\cos(0)\cdot e^0=2x\cdot1\cdot1=2x.\quad\checkmark$$

Both the small-$x$ behavior and the exact derivative formula match.

A concrete example

Evaluate $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$ at $x=\sqrt{\pi}\approx 1.7725$:

  • Innermost layer: $u=(\sqrt{\pi})^2=\pi$,
  • Middle layer: $v=\sin(\pi)=0$,
  • Outermost layer: $f(\sqrt{\pi})=e^0=1$.
  • Derivative at $x=\sqrt{\pi}$:
$$f'(\sqrt{\pi})=2\sqrt{\pi}\cos(\pi)e^{\sin(\pi)}=2\sqrt{\pi}(-1)(1)=-2\sqrt{\pi}\approx -3.5449.$$

Because $\cos(\pi)=-1<0$, the function is decreasing steeply as $x$ passes through $\sqrt{\pi}$.

How I would explain this in class

Evaluating $f(x)=e^{\sin(x^2)}$ goes from inside to outside: $x\to x^2\to\sin(x^2)\to e^{\sin(x^2)}$. Differentiating works in reverse, like peeling layers off from the outside in: first differentiate the outer exponential to get $e^{\sin(x^2)}$, then multiply by the derivative of the middle sine layer, $\cos(x^2)$, and finally multiply by the derivative of the inner square, $2x$, giving $f'(x)=2x\cos(x^2)e^{\sin(x^2)}$.

Exercise 14. Continuity and Differentiability of a Piecewise Function

The question

Investigate the behavior at $x=0$ of

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2,&x\ge0,\\ax,&x<0.\end{cases}$$

For which values of $a$ is the function continuous? For which values do the one-sided derivatives exist and agree? For which value of $a$ is the function differentiable? Explain why continuity alone is not sufficient.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When a function is glued together from two formulas at a boundary point $x=0$, we must check two separate conditions in order:

  1. Continuity at $x=0$ (do the two pieces meet without a jump?):
$$\lim_{x\to0^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)=f(0).$$
  1. Differentiability at $x=0$ (do the two pieces meet smoothly with the same slope, without a sharp corner?): the left-hand derivative $f'_-(0)$ and the right-hand derivative $f'_+(0)$ must both exist and be equal:
$$f'_-(0)=\lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{f(0+h)-f(0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{f(0+h)-f(0)}{h}=f'_+(0).$$

1. Continuity at $x=0$

Compute the value at $x=0$ and the two one-sided limits:

  • Value at $x=0$ (using the $x\ge0$ branch): $f(0)=0^2=0$.
  • Right-hand limit:
$$\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)=\lim_{x\to0^+}x^2=0.$$
  • Left-hand limit:
$$\lim_{x\to0^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to0^-}(ax)=a\cdot0=0\quad\text{for every }a\in\mathbb{R}.$$

Since $\lim_{x\to0^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)=f(0)=0$ regardless of $a$, the function $f$ is continuous at $x=0$ for all real values $a\in\mathbb{R}$.

2. One-sided derivatives at $x=0$

Now compute the right-hand and left-hand derivatives at $x=0$ using $f(0)=0$:

  • Right-hand derivative ($h\to0^+$, so $h>0$ and $f(h)=h^2$):
$$f'_+(0)=\lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{h^2-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0^+}h=0.$$
  • Left-hand derivative ($h\to0^-$, so $h<0$ and $f(h)=ah$):
$$f'_-(0)=\lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{ah-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0^-}a=a.$$

Thus:

  • Both one-sided derivatives $f'_-(0)=a$ and $f'_+(0)=0$ exist for every $a\in\mathbb{R}$.
  • They agree ($f'_-(0)=f'_+(0)$) if and only if
$$a=0.$$

3. Differentiability at $x=0$

A function is differentiable at $x=0$ if and only if its left-hand and right-hand derivatives exist and are equal. Therefore, $f$ is differentiable at $x=0$ if and only if $a=0$ (in which case $f'(0)=0$).

4. Why continuity alone is not sufficient

  • Continuity only requires the graphs of the two pieces $y=ax$ (for $x<0$) and $y=x^2$ (for $x\ge0$) to connect at the same point $(0,0)$. Since both $ax$ and $x^2$ equal $0$ at $x=0$, the pieces always touch at the origin.
  • Differentiability requires a unique tangent line at $(0,0)$. The parabola $y=x^2$ arrives at the origin horizontally with slope $f'_+(0)=0$, whereas the line $y=ax$ arrives at the origin with slope $f'_-(0)=a$. If $a\neq0$, the graph has a sharp corner (kink) at $(0,0)$ where the slope abruptly jumps from $a$ to $0$, so no single tangent line exists.

Quick verification

Check the formula for $f'(x)$ away from $x=0$:

$$f'(x)=\begin{cases}2x,&x>0,\\a,&x<0.\end{cases}$$

As $x\to0^+$, the slope $2x\to0$. As $x\to0^-$, the slope is constantly $a$. The slope transitions without a jump across $x=0$ if and only if $a=0$. ✓

A concrete example

Compare $a=1$ and $a=0$:

  • If $a=1$: $f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2,&x\ge0,\\x,&x<0.\end{cases}$ The graph is continuous at $(0,0)$, but coming from the left along the $45^\circ$ line $y=x$ the slope is $1$, and at $(0,0)$ it suddenly bends into the flat bottom of $y=x^2$ with slope $0$. This sharp corner at $(0,0)$ is not differentiable.
  • If $a=0$: $f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2,&x\ge0,\\0,&x<0.\end{cases}$ Coming from the left along the horizontal line $y=0$ (slope $0$), the graph merges smoothly into the parabola $y=x^2$ (whose tangent at $x=0$ also has slope $0$), so $f'(0)=0$ exists.

How I would explain this in class

For any real number $a$, both $ax$ and $x^2$ approach $0$ as $x\to0$, so $f$ is continuous at $x=0$ for all $a\in\mathbb{R}$. However, from the right the parabola $x^2$ has slope $f'_+(0)=0$, while from the left the line $ax$ has slope $f'_-(0)=a$. The two one-sided derivatives agree only when $a=0$. Continuity only prevents a jump at $(0,0)$, whereas differentiability also prevents a sharp corner, which is why we need $a=0$.

Exercise 15. The Difference Quotient as a Check of the Derivative

The question

Choose one function from the problem set and a point $x_0$ at which the derivative exists. Compute $f'(x_0)$ exactly, and then calculate

$$\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}$$

for $h=1$, $0.1$, and $0.01$. Compare the values with $f'(x_0)$ and explain why the difference quotient approaching a single number is consistent with the definition of the derivative, while a few computations do not replace a limit argument.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

By definition, the derivative at $x_0$ is the limit of the difference quotient:

$$f'(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}.$$

For any specific nonzero number $h$, the difference quotient $\dfrac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}$ is the secant slope between $x_0$ and $x_0+h$. Evaluating this ratio for smaller and smaller steps ($h=1, 0.1, 0.01$) lets us numerically observe the secant slopes converging toward the tangent slope $f'(x_0)$ — a powerful sanity check on our derivative formula, though not a mathematical proof.

1. Choose a function and a point, and compute $f'(x_0)$ exactly

Let us choose the polynomial from Exercise 10 (and Exercise 11):

$$f(x)=x^2-3x+1 \qquad\text{at}\qquad x_0=2.$$

First compute the exact value and exact derivative at $x_0=2$:

  • Function value: $f(2)=2^2-3(2)+1=4-6+1=-1$.
  • Derivative formula: $f'(x)=2x-3$.
  • Exact derivative at $x_0=2$:
$$f'(2)=2(2)-3=1.$$

2. Compute the difference quotient for $h=1$, $0.1$, and $0.01$

First let us simplify $f(2+h)$ for a general step $h$:

$$f(2+h)=(2+h)^2-3(2+h)+1=(4+4h+h^2)-(6+3h)+1=-1+h+h^2.$$

Therefore, for any $h\neq0$:

$$\frac{f(2+h)-f(2)}{h}=\frac{(-1+h+h^2)-(-1)}{h}=\frac{h+h^2}{h}=1+h.$$

Now evaluate for the three requested values of $h$:

  1. For $h=1$ ($x_0+h=3$, where $f(3)=3^2-3(3)+1=1$):
$$\frac{f(3)-f(2)}{1}=\frac{1-(-1)}{1}=2=1+1.$$
  1. For $h=0.1$ ($x_0+h=2.1$, where $f(2.1)=2.1^2-3(2.1)+1=-0.89$):
$$\frac{f(2.1)-f(2)}{0.1}=\frac{-0.89-(-1)}{0.1}=\frac{0.11}{0.1}=1.1=1+0.1.$$
  1. For $h=0.01$ ($x_0+h=2.01$, where $f(2.01)=2.01^2-3(2.01)+1=-0.9899$):
$$\frac{f(2.01)-f(2)}{0.01}=\frac{-0.9899-(-1)}{0.01}=\frac{0.0101}{0.01}=1.01=1+0.01.$$

3. Compare the values with $f'(2)=1$

$1$$3$$2.00$$1$$1.00$
$0.1$$2.1$$1.10$$1$$0.10$
$0.01$$2.01$$1.01$$1$$0.01$

As $h$ shrinks by a factor of $10$ each time ($1\to 0.1\to 0.01$), the difference quotient $2.00\to 1.10\to 1.01$ gets steadily closer to $f'(2)=1$, and the discrepancy equals $h$.

4. Consistency vs. proof: why a few values do not replace a limit argument

  • Why it is consistent with the definition: If $f'(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}$ exists, then for small values of $h$ the difference quotient must lie close to $f'(x_0)$. Seeing $2\to 1.1\to 1.01$ approach $1$ confirms that we did not make a sign or arithmetic error when computing $f'(2)=1$.
  • Why three values do not prove the limit exists:
  1. Checking only $h=1, 0.1, 0.01$ tests only three positive numbers ($h>0$). It never checks negative steps $h<0$ (such as $h=-0.01$, which could approach a completely different left-hand slope if the function had a corner like in Exercise 14).
  2. Even for $h>0$, a function could behave nicely at $h=1, 0.1, 0.01$ and then oscillate wildly or jump at $h=0.0001$.
  3. Only the algebraic limit argument $\lim_{h\to0}(1+h)=1$ proves that the difference quotient converges to $1$ for every way $h$ can approach $0$ ($h\to0^+$ and $h\to0^-$).

Quick verification

Let us also check negative steps $h=-0.1$ and $h=-0.01$ at $x_0=2$:

  • For $h=-0.1$: $\dfrac{f(1.9)-f(2)}{-0.1}=\dfrac{-1.09-(-1)}{-0.1}=\dfrac{-0.09}{-0.1}=0.90=1-0.1$.
  • For $h=-0.01$: $\dfrac{f(1.99)-f(2)}{-0.01}=\dfrac{-1.0099-(-1)}{-0.01}=\dfrac{-0.0099}{-0.01}=0.99=1-0.01$.

From the left ($0.90\to 0.99$) and from the right ($1.10\to 1.01$), the secant slopes sandwich the exact derivative $f'(2)=1$. ✓

A concrete example

Why can testing only $h=1, 0.1, 0.01$ be fooled? Consider two examples:

  1. One-sided trap: For $g(x)=|x-2|+(x-2)$ at $x_0=2$, testing $h=1, 0.1, 0.01$ gives difference quotients $2, 2, 2$, suggesting $g'(2)=2$. Yet for $h=-0.01$, the difference quotient is $0$, so $g'(2)$ does not exist at all!
  2. Tiny-scale trap: For $k(x)=x^2-3x+1+\sin(100\pi x)$ at $x_0=2$, plugging in $h=1, 0.1, 0.01$ makes $\sin(100\pi(2+h))=\sin(200\pi+100\pi h)=0$ at all three points, giving the exact same table $2.00, 1.10, 1.01$, even though the actual derivative at $x=2$ is $1+100\pi\approx 315.16$!

How I would explain this in class

Choosing $f(x)=x^2-3x+1$ at $x_0=2$, the power rule gives the exact derivative $f'(2)=2(2)-3=1$. Computing the difference quotient $\frac{f(2+h)-f(2)}{h}=1+h$ for $h=1, 0.1, 0.01$ gives $2, 1.1, 1.01$, which clearly approaches $f'(2)=1$. This is a great numerical check that our derivative formula is right, but three sample points cannot replace a limit proof because they only test three positive values of $h$ and cannot rule out corners for $h<0$ or hidden oscillations at smaller scales.
