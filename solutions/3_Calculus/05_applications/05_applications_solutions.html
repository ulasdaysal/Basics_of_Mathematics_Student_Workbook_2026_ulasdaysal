Exercise 1. Motion — from Position to Velocity

The question

The position of a point along an axis is given by

$$s(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t+2,\qquad t\ge0.$$
  1. Determine the velocity and acceleration.
  2. Find the times at which the point stops.
  3. Determine on which intervals the point moves in the positive direction of the axis and on which intervals it moves in the negative direction.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

In kinematics, derivatives measure instantaneous rates of change with respect to time $t$:

  • Velocity $v(t)=s'(t)$ is the rate of change of position. Its sign tells us the direction of motion:
  • $v(t)>0$: the point moves in the positive direction ($s(t)$ increases),
  • $v(t)<0$: the point moves in the negative direction ($s(t)$ decreases),
  • $v(t)=0$: the point is instantaneously at rest (it stops and may reverse direction).
  • Acceleration $a(t)=v'(t)=s''(t)$ is the rate of change of velocity.

1. Velocity and acceleration

Differentiating the position function $s(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t+2$ term by term gives the velocity:

$$v(t)=s'(t)=3t^2-12t+9.$$

Factoring out $3$ makes the algebraic structure immediately visible:

$$v(t)=3(t^2-4t+3)=3(t-1)(t-3).$$

Differentiating $v(t)$ once more gives the acceleration:

$$a(t)=v'(t)=s''(t)=6t-12=6(t-2).$$

2. Times at which the point stops

The point stops when its instantaneous velocity is zero:

$$v(t)=0 \iff 3(t-1)(t-3)=0.$$

Since $t\ge0$, both roots lie in the physical domain:

$$t_1=1,\qquad t_2=3.$$

At these two moments, the point reaches local extrema of position:

  • At $t=1$: $s(1)=1^3-6(1)^2+9(1)+2=6$ (a local turnaround point),
  • At $t=3$: $s(3)=3^3-6(3)^2+9(3)+2=27-54+27+2=2$ (a second turnaround point).

3. Direction of motion on time intervals

We examine the sign of $v(t)=3(t-1)(t-3)$ on $[0,\infty)$:

  • On $[0,1)$: both $(t-1)<0$ and $(t-3)<0$, so their product is positive and $v(t)>0$.
  • On $(1,3)$: $(t-1)>0$ while $(t-3)<0$, so $v(t)<0$.
  • On $(3,\infty)$: both $(t-1)>0$ and $(t-3)>0$, so $v(t)>0$.

Therefore:

  • The point moves in the positive direction on $[0,1)$ and on $(3,\infty)$.
  • The point moves in the negative direction on $(1,3)$.

4. Physical trajectory summary

Starting at $s(0)=2$, the point moves forward to $s(1)=6$, stops at $t=1$, reverses direction and moves backward to $s(3)=2$, stops again at $t=3$, and then moves forward indefinitely for $t>3$. Notice that at $t=2$, $a(2)=0$ and $v(2)=-3$: this is the moment of maximum backward speed.

Quick verification

Let us check the position values and test velocities in each interval:

  • At $t=0$: $s(0)=2$, and $v(0)=9>0$ (moving forward from $2$ to $s(1)=6$). ✓
  • At $t=2\in(1,3)$: $v(2)=3(2-1)(2-3)=-3<0$, and indeed $s(t)$ drops from $s(1)=6$ to $s(3)=2$. ✓
  • At $t=4\in(3,\infty)$: $v(4)=3(4-1)(4-3)=9>0$, and $s(4)=64-96+36+2=6>s(3)$. ✓

A concrete example

Suppose $s(t)$ is measured in meters and $t$ in seconds. If we only look at the net displacement between $t=0$ and $t=3$, we see

$$\Delta s = s(3)-s(0) = 2-2 = 0\text{ m}.$$

However, the point was not standing still! From $t=0$ to $t=1$ it traveled $|6-2|=4\text{ m}$ forward, and from $t=1$ to $t=3$ it traveled $|2-6|=4\text{ m}$ backward, for a total distance traveled of $4+4=8\text{ m}$. Tracking the sign of $v(t)$ is what lets us distinguish net displacement from actual motion.

How I would explain this in class

To study motion from a position formula $s(t)$, we differentiate once to get velocity $v(t)=3t^2-12t+9=3(t-1)(t-3)$ and a second time to get acceleration $a(t)=6t-12$. Setting $v(t)=0$ shows the object stops at $t=1$ and $t=3$. Because $v(t)$ is a parabola opening upward, the velocity is positive on $[0,1)$ and $(3,\infty)$, meaning forward motion, and negative on $(1,3)$, meaning the object backs up between $t=1$ and $t=3$.

Exercise 2. Maximum Area of a Rectangle

The question

A rectangle has perimeter $40$.

  1. Write its area as a function of the length of one side.
  2. Determine the domain of this function resulting from the geometry of the problem.
  3. Find the dimensions that give the maximum area.
  4. Justify that the point found actually gives a maximum.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A rectangle has two independent side lengths $x$ and $y$, so its area $A=xy$ initially depends on two variables. However, the fixed perimeter $2x+2y=40$ acts as a constraint equation. Using the constraint to express $y$ in terms of $x$ reduces the problem to a single-variable function $A(x)$ on an open interval, which we can optimize using the derivative.

1. Area as a function of one side

Let $x$ and $y$ denote the lengths of the adjacent sides of the rectangle. The perimeter constraint is

$$2x+2y=40 \implies x+y=20 \implies y=20-x.$$

Substituting $y=20-x$ into the area formula $A=xy$ gives the objective function of a single variable:

$$A(x)=x(20-x)=20x-x^2.$$

2. Geometric domain of the function

For a non-degenerate rectangle to exist, both side lengths must be strictly positive:

$$x>0 \quad\text{and}\quad y=20-x>0 \implies x<20.$$

Thus, the natural geometric domain of $A(x)$ is the open interval

$$D=(0,20).$$

3. Finding the dimensions that maximize the area

We differentiate $A(x)=20x-x^2$ with respect to $x$:

$$A'(x)=20-2x.$$

Setting the derivative equal to zero gives the critical point:

$$A'(x)=0 \iff 20-2x=0 \iff x=10.$$

Notice that $x=10$ lies inside the domain $D=(0,20)$. The corresponding second side is

$$y=20-10=10.$$

Thus, the optimal dimensions are $10\times 10$ (a square), yielding a maximum area of

$$A(10)=10\cdot 10=100.$$

4. Justification that the critical point gives a maximum

We can justify that $x=10$ is a global maximum on $(0,20)$ in three complementary ways:

  • First derivative sign test: For $x\in(0,10)$, we have $A'(x)=20-2x>0$, so $A(x)$ is strictly increasing. For $x\in(10,20)$, we have $A'(x)=20-2x<0$, so $A(x)$ is strictly decreasing. Therefore, $x=10$ is the unique global maximum on $(0,20)$.
  • Second derivative test: $A''(x)=-2<0$ everywhere, so $A(x)$ is strictly concave down and its stationary point is a global maximum.
  • Completing the square: $A(x)=100-(x-10)^2\le 100$, with equality if and only if $x=10$.

Quick verification

Let us compare $A(10)=100$ with nearby and extreme rectangles of perimeter $40$:

  • If $x=9$, then $y=11$ and $A(9)=9\cdot 11=99 < 100$. ✓
  • If $x=11$, then $y=9$ and $A(11)=11\cdot 9=99 < 100$. ✓
  • Near the endpoints: as $x\to 0^+$ or $x\to 20^-$, $A(x)\to 0$. ✓

A concrete example

Suppose you have $40\text{ m}$ of fencing to enclose a rectangular garden.

  • A long, narrow strip of dimensions $2\text{ m}\times 18\text{ m}$ uses all $40\text{ m}$ of fence ($2(2+18)=40$) but encloses only $36\text{ m}^2$.
  • A $5\text{ m}\times 15\text{ m}$ rectangle encloses $75\text{ m}^2$.
  • Balancing the sides equally to $10\text{ m}\times 10\text{ m}$ encloses $100\text{ m}^2$, illustrating the classic isoperimetric principle: among all rectangles of a fixed perimeter, the square encloses the largest area.

How I would explain this in class

Since the perimeter is $40$, half the perimeter is $x+y=20$, so the second side is $y=20-x$. Both sides must be positive, which restricts $x$ to $(0,20)$. The area function is $A(x)=x(20-x)=20x-x^2$. Its derivative $A'(x)=20-2x$ equals zero at $x=10$, where $y=10$ as well. Because $A'(x)$ switches from positive to negative at $x=10$ (and $A''(x)=-2<0$), the $10\times 10$ square gives the maximum area of $100$.

Exercise 3. A Box Made from Cardboard

The question

From a $30\text{ cm}\times 40\text{ cm}$ sheet of cardboard, squares of side length $x$ are cut from the four corners and the resulting flaps are folded up to form an open rectangular box.

Write the volume as a function of $x$, determine the physically meaningful domain, and find the value of $x$ for which the volume is greatest.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When we cut a square of side $x$ from each corner of a $30\text{ cm}\times 40\text{ cm}$ rectangle and fold up the four flaps:

  • Each original side loses $2x$ of length (one cut of length $x$ at each end), so the base of the box has dimensions $(30-2x)\text{ cm}$ by $(40-2x)\text{ cm}$.
  • The folded flaps become the vertical walls, so the height of the box is $x\text{ cm}$.

Multiplying length, width, and height produces a cubic polynomial $V(x)$ that we maximize over the interval where all three dimensions are strictly positive.

1. Volume as a function of $x$

The volume of the open box is the product of its base dimensions and height:

$$V(x)=x(30-2x)(40-2x).$$

Expanding the product so we can differentiate easily:

$$V(x)=x(1200-140x+4x^2)=4x^3-140x^2+1200x\quad [\text{cm}^3].$$

2. Physically meaningful domain

All three dimensions of the box must be strictly positive:

$$x>0,\qquad 30-2x>0 \implies x<15,\qquad 40-2x>0 \implies x<20.$$

The shorter side of the cardboard ($30\text{ cm}$) is the binding constraint: once $x\ge 15\text{ cm}$, the two corner cuts meet in the middle of the $30\text{ cm}$ side and no base remains. Thus, the physical domain is

$$D=(0,15)\quad [\text{cm}].$$

3. Finding the critical points

Differentiating $V(x)=4x^3-140x^2+1200x$ with respect to $x$ gives

$$V'(x)=12x^2-280x+1200=4(3x^2-70x+300).$$

Setting $V'(x)=0$ yields the quadratic equation

$$3x^2-70x+300=0.$$

The discriminant is

$$\Delta=(-70)^2-4\cdot 3\cdot 300=4900-3600=1300=100\cdot 13,$$

so $\sqrt{\Delta}=10\sqrt{13}$. The two roots are

$$x_{1,2}=\frac{70\pm 10\sqrt{13}}{6}=\frac{35\pm 5\sqrt{13}}{3}.$$

4. Selecting and justifying the maximum in the domain

Since $\sqrt{13}\approx 3.6056$, the two critical points are approximately

$$x_1=\frac{35-5\sqrt{13}}{3}\approx 5.66\text{ cm},\qquad x_2=\frac{35+5\sqrt{13}}{3}\approx 17.68\text{ cm}.$$

Because $x_2\approx 17.68\notin(0,15)$, the only critical point inside our physical domain $D=(0,15)$ is

$$x^*=\frac{35-5\sqrt{13}}{3}\approx 5.66\text{ cm}.$$

Since $V'(x)=4(3x^2-70x+300)$ is an upward-opening parabola with roots $x_1\approx 5.66$ and $x_2\approx 17.68$:

  • For $x\in(0,x^*)$, $V'(x)>0$, so $V(x)$ is strictly increasing.
  • For $x\in(x^*,15)$, $V'(x)<0$, so $V(x)$ is strictly decreasing.

Therefore, $x^*=\dfrac{35-5\sqrt{13}}{3}\approx 5.66\text{ cm}$ gives the global maximum volume on $(0,15)$.

Quick verification

Let us evaluate the second derivative and check volume values around $x^*\approx 5.66\text{ cm}$:

  • Second derivative test: $V''(x)=24x-280$. At $x^*=\frac{35-5\sqrt{13}}{3}$, we have $V''(x^*)=8(35-5\sqrt{13})-280=-40\sqrt{13}<0$, confirming a local maximum. ✓
  • Boundary behavior: $\lim_{x\to 0^+}V(x)=0$ and $\lim_{x\to 15^-}V(x)=0$, while $V(x^*)>0$. ✓

A concrete example

Let us compare the volume at $x^*\approx 5.66\text{ cm}$ with nearby integer cuts:

  • For $x=5\text{ cm}$: base is $20\text{ cm}\times 30\text{ cm}$, so $V(5)=5\cdot 20\cdot 30=3000\text{ cm}^3$.
  • For $x=6\text{ cm}$: base is $18\text{ cm}\times 28\text{ cm}$, so $V(6)=6\cdot 18\cdot 28=3024\text{ cm}^3$.
  • For $x^*\approx 5.66\text{ cm}$: base is $18.69\text{ cm}\times 28.69\text{ cm}$, giving the maximum volume $V(x^*)\approx 3032.3\text{ cm}^3$.

Both shallow cuts ($x=5$) and deeper cuts ($x=6$) produce smaller volumes than the optimal cut $x^*\approx 5.66\text{ cm}$.

How I would explain this in class

Cutting squares of side $x$ from the four corners reduces the cardboard's sides from $30$ and $40$ to $30-2x$ and $40-2x$, while folding up the flaps gives height $x$. For all three dimensions to be positive, $x$ must lie in $(0,15)$. Expanding $V(x)=x(30-2x)(40-2x)=4x^3-140x^2+1200x$ and solving $V'(x)=0$ gives two roots, $(35\pm 5\sqrt{13})/3$. Only $x=(35-5\sqrt{13})/3\approx 5.66\text{ cm}$ fits in $(0,15)$, and because $V'(x)$ changes sign from $+$ to $-$ there, it maximizes the volume.

Exercise 4. Area Between Curves

The question

Find the area of the region bounded by the graphs

$$y=x \quad\text{and}\quad y=x^2.$$

First find the intersection points and justify which function is greater on the relevant interval.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

The area of a planar region bounded vertically between two continuous curves $y=f(x)$ and $y=g(x)$ on an interval $[a,b]$ is obtained by integrating the vertical height of the region:

$$\text{Area}=\int_a^b \bigl(y_{\text{upper}}(x)-y_{\text{lower}}(x)\bigr)\,dx.$$

Before setting up the integral, we must:

  1. Solve $f(x)=g(x)$ to find the horizontal limits of integration $a$ and $b$.
  2. Determine which curve lies on top ($y_{\text{upper}}\ge y_{\text{lower}}$) on $[a,b]$ so that the integrand represents a non-negative height.

1. Finding the intersection points

We set the two formulas equal to each other:

$$x^2=x \iff x^2-x=0 \iff x(x-1)=0.$$

Thus, the graphs intersect at

$$x_1=0 \quad\text{and}\quad x_2=1,$$

which correspond to the points $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ in the plane. The bounded region therefore spans the interval $[0,1]$.

2. Determining which function is greater on $[0,1]$

On the open interval $(0,1)$, we have $0<x<1$. Multiplying the inequality $x<1$ by the positive number $x$ gives

$$x^2 < x \qquad\text{for all } x\in(0,1).$$

Equivalently, $x-x^2=x(1-x)>0$ on $(0,1)$ because both factors $x$ and $1-x$ are positive. Hence, the line $y=x$ is the upper curve and the parabola $y=x^2$ is the lower curve on $[0,1]$.

3. Setting up and computing the area integral

Integrating the vertical slice height $(x-x^2)$ from $x=0$ to $x=1$ gives

$$\text{Area}=\int_0^1 (x-x^2)\,dx.$$

Using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$\text{Area}=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)-(0-0)=\frac{1}{6}.$$

4. Geometric decomposition of the result

We can interpret the integral $\int_0^1 x\,dx - \int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{6}$ directly:

  • The region under the line $y=x$ on $[0,1]$ is a right triangle of base $1$ and height $1$, whose area is $\frac{1}{2}$.
  • The region under the parabola $y=x^2$ on $[0,1]$ has area $\frac{1}{3}$ (a classic result known since Archimedes).
  • Removing the parabolic region from the right triangle leaves the lens-shaped region of area $\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{6}$.

Quick verification

Let us verify the inequality and the magnitude of the area:

  • Midpoint check at $x=\frac{1}{2}$: the line height is $y=\frac{1}{2}=0.5$, while the parabola height is $y=\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\bigr)^2=0.25<0.5$, confirming $x>x^2$ on $(0,1)$. ✓
  • Bounding box check: the entire region fits inside the right triangle of area $\frac{1}{2}$, and its maximum vertical thickness (attained at $x=\frac{1}{2}$) is $\frac{1}{4}$. Thus $0<\text{Area}<\frac{1}{2}$, and $\frac{1}{6}\approx 0.1667$ fits this bound cleanly. ✓

A concrete example

If we forgot to check which curve was on top and mistakenly integrated $\int_0^1 (x^2-x)\,dx$, we would get $-\frac{1}{6}$, which is negative and cannot be a geometric area. Even worse, if two curves cross inside an interval—for instance $y=x$ and $y=x^3$ on $[-1,1]$, which cross at $x=-1,0,1$—integrating $\int_{-1}^1 (x-x^3)\,dx=0$ cancels the two lobes completely! Checking intersections and ordering first prevents signed cancellation.

How I would explain this in class

First we find where the curves meet by solving $x=x^2$, which gives $x=0$ and $x=1$. Between $0$ and $1$, squaring a fraction makes it smaller, so the line $y=x$ lies above the parabola $y=x^2$. We integrate top minus bottom: $\int_0^1 (x-x^2)\,dx = \bigl[\frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}\bigr]_0^1 = \frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$.

Exercise 5. Distance from Velocity

The question

The velocity of a car during the first five seconds is described by the function

$$v(t)=4t+2\quad [\text{m/s}].$$

Compute the distance traveled and explain why the integral of velocity gives the change in position.

Then answer what would have to be changed in the reasoning if the velocity also took negative values.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Position $s(t)$ and velocity $v(t)$ are linked by differentiation: $s'(t)=v(t)$, which means $s(t)$ is an antiderivative of $v(t)$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, integrating the rate of change $v(t)$ over $[0,5]$ accumulates all infinitesimal displacements $ds=v(t)\,dt$:

$$\int_0^5 v(t)\,dt = s(5)-s(0) = \Delta s.$$

When $v(t)\ge 0$ on the entire interval, the car never reverses direction, so the net change in position (displacement) equals the total distance traveled.

1. Checking the sign of velocity and computing the distance

For $t\in[0,5]$, the velocity is

$$v(t)=4t+2 \ge 2 > 0.$$

Since the car moves strictly forward throughout $[0,5]$, the distance traveled equals the definite integral of $v(t)$:

$$d=\int_0^5 (4t+2)\,dt = \left[2t^2+2t\right]_0^5 = \bigl(2(5)^2+2(5)\bigr) - 0 = 50+10 = 60\text{ m}.$$

2. Why the integral of velocity gives the change in position

Divide the time interval $[a,b]$ into tiny subintervals of duration $\Delta t_i$. On each short subinterval, the velocity is nearly constant at $v(t_i)$, so the small change in position is approximately

$$\Delta s_i \approx v(t_i)\,\Delta t_i.$$

Summing these increments over the whole trip gives the Riemann sum $\sum v(t_i)\,\Delta t_i$, which in the limit $\Delta t_i\to 0$ becomes $\int_a^b v(t)\,dt$. At the same time, the sum of all small position changes telescopes to the total change in position $s(b)-s(a)$ by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

3. What changes if velocity takes negative values?

If $v(t)<0$ on part of the interval, the car is moving backward during that time:

  • The ordinary integral $\int_a^b v(t)\,dt = s(b)-s(a)$ computes the net displacement (forward distance minus backward distance).
  • To compute the actual total distance traveled (what the odometer measures), we must integrate the speed $|v(t)|$:
$$d_{\text{total}} = \int_a^b |v(t)|\,dt.$$

In practice, we find the times where $v(t)=0$, split $[a,b]$ into subintervals where $v(t)$ has a constant sign, and integrate $+v(t)$ where $v(t)\ge 0$ and $-v(t)$ where $v(t)<0$.

4. Kinematics and geometric interpretation

Notice that $v(t)=4t+2$ describes motion with constant acceleration $a=v'(t)=4\text{ m/s}^2$ and initial velocity $v_0=2\text{ m/s}$. Under the velocity-time graph, the region on $[0,5]$ is a trapezoid of width $5\text{ s}$ and parallel heights $v(0)=2\text{ m/s}$ and $v(5)=22\text{ m/s}$.

Quick verification

We can check $d=60\text{ m}$ in two independent ways:

  • Area of a trapezoid: $\text{Area}=\frac{v(0)+v(5)}{2}\cdot 5 = \frac{2+22}{2}\cdot 5 = 12\cdot 5 = 60\text{ m}$. ✓
  • Physics formula for uniform acceleration: $d=v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2 = 2(5) + \frac{1}{2}(4)(5)^2 = 10 + 50 = 60\text{ m}$. ✓

A concrete example

Suppose instead that a car had velocity $v(t)=2t-4\text{ m/s}$ on $[0,5]$. Here $v(t)<0$ on $[0,2)$ and $v(t)>0$ on $(2,5]$.

  • The net displacement is $\int_0^5 (2t-4)\,dt = [t^2-4t]_0^5 = 25-20 = 5\text{ m}$.
  • The total distance traveled splits at $t=2$:
$$d = \int_0^2 -(2t-4)\,dt + \int_2^5 (2t-4)\,dt = 4 + 9 = 13\text{ m}.$$

The car backed up $4\text{ m}$ during the first $2\text{ s}$ and then drove $9\text{ m}$ forward, traveling $13\text{ m}$ in total to end up $5\text{ m}$ ahead of its starting point.

How I would explain this in class

Because velocity is the derivative of position ($s'(t)=v(t)$), integrating velocity over $[0,5]$ sums all the tiny position changes $v(t)\,dt$ to give the net change in position $s(5)-s(0)$. Since $v(t)=4t+2$ is always positive on $[0,5]$, the car only moves forward, and $\int_0^5 (4t+2)\,dt = [2t^2+2t]_0^5 = 60\text{ m}$ is the exact distance traveled. If $v(t)$ became negative, the plain integral would subtract backward motion, so we would have to integrate the speed $|v(t)|$ by splitting the integral at the roots of $v(t)=0$.

Exercise 6. Average Value of a Function

The question

Compute the average value of the function

$$f(x)=x^2$$

on the interval $[0,3]$.

Find a point $c\in[0,3]$ for which $f(c)$ is equal to this average value, and interpret the result geometrically.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

To find the average of finitely many numbers, we add them up and divide by how many there are. For a continuous function $f(x)$ on an interval $[a,b]$, the definite integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ acts as the continuous sum of values, and the interval length $b-a$ acts as the count:

$$f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.$$

By the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals, a continuous function on $[a,b]$ always attains its average value at least once inside the interval: there exists $c\in[a,b]$ such that $f(c)=f_{\text{avg}}$.

1. Computing the average value on $[0,3]$

Applying the average value formula with $a=0$, $b=3$, and $f(x)=x^2$:

$$f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{3-0}\int_0^3 x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{3}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^3.$$

Evaluating at the endpoints gives

$$f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{27}{3} = \frac{9}{3} = 3.$$

2. Finding the point $c\in[0,3]$ where $f(c)=f_{\text{avg}}$

We look for $c\in[0,3]$ such that

$$f(c)=3 \iff c^2=3 \implies c=\pm\sqrt{3}.$$

Since $c$ must belong to the interval $[0,3]$, we select the positive root:

$$c=\sqrt{3}\approx 1.732.$$

3. Geometric interpretation (equal-area rectangle)

Rewriting the average value equation as

$$\int_0^3 x^2\,dx = f(c)\cdot(3-0) = 3\cdot 3 = 9$$

reveals its geometric meaning:

  • The curved region under the parabola $y=x^2$ over $[0,3]$ has area $9$.
  • A rectangle with the same base $[0,3]$ (width $3$) and constant height $y=f_{\text{avg}}=3$ (attained by the graph at $x=c=\sqrt{3}$) also has area $3\times 3=9$.

If the area under $y=x^2$ were made of water and allowed to settle into a flat horizontal level over $[0,3]$, the water depth would stabilize at $y=3$.

4. Why $c=\sqrt{3}$ is greater than the midpoint $1.5$

Notice that the average of the endpoint values is $\frac{f(0)+f(3)}{2}=\frac{0+9}{2}=4.5$, whereas the true integral average is $3$. Because $f(x)=x^2$ is convex (concave up), it stays below the secant line connecting $(0,0)$ and $(3,9)$, pulling the true average down to $3$ and shifting $c=\sqrt{3}\approx 1.732$ to the right of the interval's midpoint $1.5$.

Quick verification

Let us verify the balance of areas above and below the horizontal line $y=3$:

  • From $x=0$ to $x=\sqrt{3}$, the line $y=3$ lies above $y=x^2$. The "missing" area under the line is $$\int_0^{\sqrt{3}} (3-x^2)\,dx = \left[3x-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^{\sqrt{3}} = 3\sqrt{3}-\sqrt{3} = 2\sqrt{3}.$$
  • From $x=\sqrt{3}$ to $x=3$, the parabola $y=x^2$ rises above $y=3$. The "excess" area above the line is $$\int_{\sqrt{3}}^3 (x^2-3)\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}-3x\right]_{\sqrt{3}}^3 = (9-9) - (\sqrt{3}-3\sqrt{3}) = 2\sqrt{3}.$$

The excess peak on $[\sqrt{3},3]$ fills the deficit on $[0,\sqrt{3}]$ with exact area $2\sqrt{3}$. ✓

A concrete example

Imagine a square plate whose local mass density varies along its length $x\in[0,3]\text{ m}$ as $\rho(x)=x^2\text{ kg/m}$. The total mass of the $3\text{ m}$ rod is $\int_0^3 x^2\,dx = 9\text{ kg}$. Replacing this non-uniform rod with a uniform $3\text{ m}$ rod of the same total mass requires a constant linear density of $\rho_{\text{avg}}=9/3=3\text{ kg/m}$, which matches the original rod's density at the exact cross-section $x=\sqrt{3}\text{ m}$.

How I would explain this in class

To average $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,3]$, we divide the area under the graph by the interval width: $\frac{1}{3-0}\int_0^3 x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{3}\cdot 9 = 3$. Setting $f(c)=c^2=3$ on $[0,3]$ gives $c=\sqrt{3}\approx 1.732$. Geometrically, a rectangle of width $3$ and height $f(\sqrt{3})=3$ has area $9$, which is identical to the area under the parabola $y=x^2$ on $[0,3]$—the curved peak above $y=3$ on $[\sqrt{3},3]$ exactly fills the gap below $y=3$ on $[0,\sqrt{3}]$.

Exercise 7. Exponential Growth

The question

Assume the population model

$$N(t)=N_0 e^{kt},\qquad k>0.$$
  1. Verify by differentiation that the model satisfies the relation $N'(t)=kN(t)$.
  2. Find the population doubling time.
  3. Explain why the doubling time does not depend on $N_0$.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Many biological, physical, and financial processes grow at a rate directly proportional to their current size: twice as many individuals produce twice as many offspring per unit time. The mathematical function whose derivative is a constant multiple of itself is the exponential function $e^{kt}$, differentiated via the chain rule: $\frac{d}{dt}(e^{kt})=k e^{kt}$.

1. Verifying the differential relation $N'(t)=kN(t)$

Differentiating $N(t)=N_0 e^{kt}$ with respect to $t$ (treating the initial size $N_0$ and growth constant $k>0$ as constants) and applying the chain rule to $e^{kt}$ gives:

$$N'(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(N_0 e^{kt}\bigr) = N_0 \cdot \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(e^{kt}\bigr) = N_0 \cdot k e^{kt} = k\bigl(N_0 e^{kt}\bigr) = k N(t).$$

Thus, at every instant $t$, the rate of growth $N'(t)$ equals the relative growth rate $k$ times the current population $N(t)$.

2. Finding the population doubling time

The doubling time $T_{\text{double}}$ is the time $t$ needed for the population to reach twice its initial value, i.e., $N(t)=2N_0$:

$$N_0 e^{k T_{\text{double}}} = 2N_0.$$

Assuming $N_0>0$, we divide both sides by $N_0$:

$$e^{k T_{\text{double}}} = 2.$$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides and dividing by $k>0$ yields:

$$k T_{\text{double}} = \ln 2 \implies T_{\text{double}} = \frac{\ln 2}{k}.$$

3. Why the doubling time does not depend on $N_0$

There are two complementary ways to understand why $N_0$ cancels out:

  • Algebraic scaling: $N_0$ is a multiplicative scale factor in $N(t)=N_0 e^{kt}$. Asking when $N(t)$ reaches $2N_0$ is asking when the growth factor $N(t)/N_0 = e^{kt}$ reaches $2$, which depends only on $k$.
  • Proportionality of growth: Because $N'(t)=kN(t)$, each individual (or unit of population) reproduces at the same average rate $k$ independently of how many other individuals exist. A population that is $10$ times larger has $10$ times as much absolute growth $N'(t)$ to achieve before doubling, and it also has $10$ times as many individuals contributing to that growth, so the two factors of $10$ cancel completely.

4. Extending to any interval $[t_0, t_0 + T_{\text{double}}]$

Not only does the initial value $N_0$ not matter, the starting time $t_0$ does not matter either:

$$N(t_0 + T_{\text{double}}) = N_0 e^{k(t_0 + \frac{\ln 2}{k})} = N_0 e^{kt_0} e^{\ln 2} = 2 N(t_0).$$

In exponential growth, the population doubles over every time window of length $\frac{\ln 2}{k}$.

Quick verification

Let us substitute $t=T_{\text{double}}=\frac{\ln 2}{k}$ back into $N(t)$:

$$N\left(\frac{\ln 2}{k}\right) = N_0 e^{k\cdot\frac{\ln 2}{k}} = N_0 e^{\ln 2} = 2N_0. \quad \checkmark$$

Similarly, after $m$ doubling periods ($t=m\frac{\ln 2}{k}$), $N(t)=N_0 e^{m\ln 2}=2^m N_0$. ✓

A concrete example

Suppose a bacterial colony grows with $k=0.2\text{ h}^{-1}$ ($20\%$ continuous relative growth per hour). Its doubling time is

$$T_{\text{double}} = \frac{\ln 2}{0.2} \approx \frac{0.6931}{0.2} \approx 3.47\text{ hours}.$$

Whether a dish starts with $N_0=100$ bacteria (growing to $200$) or $N_0=1\,000\,000$ bacteria (growing to $2\,000\,000$), both dishes double in exactly $3.47\text{ hours}$.

How I would explain this in class

By the chain rule, differentiating $N(t)=N_0 e^{kt}$ pulls down a factor of $k$, giving $N'(t)=k N_0 e^{kt}=k N(t)$. To find when the population doubles, we solve $N_0 e^{kt}=2N_0$. Dividing both sides by $N_0$ immediately eliminates $N_0$, leaving $e^{kt}=2$, so $t=\frac{\ln 2}{k}$. Physically, a larger starting population needs a larger absolute increase to double, but its growth rate $N'(t)=kN(t)$ is proportionally larger by the exact same factor, so the time required to double is always constant.

Exercise 8. Number of Requests Handled by a Server

The question

The rate at which requests arrive at a server during the first $5$ minutes is described by the function

$$r(t)=100+20t\quad [\text{requests/min}],\qquad 0\le t\le 5.$$
  1. Compute the total number of requests that arrived during this time.
  2. Compute the average rate of incoming requests over this interval.
  3. Compare the average rate with the values $r(0)$ and $r(5)$.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When we are given a time-varying rate of flow $r(t)$ (in requests per minute), multiplying rate by a tiny time increment $dt$ gives the number of requests $dQ=r(t)\,dt$ arriving during that instant. Integrating $r(t)$ over $[0,5]$ accumulates all incoming requests, and dividing that total by the $5$-minute duration yields the average arrival rate.

1. Total number of requests in the first 5 minutes

The total number of requests $Q$ arriving on $[0,5]$ is the definite integral of the arrival rate $r(t)$:

$$Q = \int_0^5 r(t)\,dt = \int_0^5 (100+20t)\,dt.$$

Computing the antiderivative and evaluating at the limits:

$$Q = \left[100t+10t^2\right]_0^5 = \bigl(100(5)+10(5)^2\bigr) - 0 = 500 + 250 = 750\text{ requests}.$$

2. Average rate of incoming requests over $[0,5]$

By the integral average value formula on $[0,5]$, the average arrival rate $r_{\text{avg}}$ is the total number of requests divided by the elapsed time:

$$r_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{5-0}\int_0^5 r(t)\,dt = \frac{750}{5} = 150\text{ requests/min}.$$

3. Comparing $r_{\text{avg}}$ with $r(0)$ and $r(5)$

Let us evaluate the arrival rate at the endpoints of the interval:

$$r(0) = 100+20(0) = 100\text{ requests/min},$$
$$r(5) = 100+20(5) = 200\text{ requests/min}.$$

Comparing the three quantities:

$$r_{\text{avg}} = 150 = \frac{100+200}{2} = \frac{r(0)+r(5)}{2},$$

and $r_{\text{avg}}$ is attained exactly at the midpoint $t=2.5\text{ min}$: $r(2.5)=100+20(2.5)=150\text{ requests/min}$.

4. Why the average equals the arithmetic mean of the endpoints here

Because $r(t)=100+20t$ is a linear function, the rate increases at a constant pace ($20\text{ requests/min}^2$). Every time interval $[0, 2.5]$ with rate below $150$ is symmetrically balanced by a corresponding time interval $[2.5, 5]$ with rate above $150$. For any linear function on $[a,b]$, the integral average always coincides with the midpoint value $r\bigl(\frac{a+b}{2}\bigr)$ and the endpoint average $\frac{r(a)+r(b)}{2}$.

Quick verification

We can verify the integral geometrically using the area of a trapezoid under $r(t)$ on $[0,5]$:

  • Base width: $\Delta t = 5\text{ min}$.
  • Parallel heights: $r(0)=100$ and $r(5)=200$.
  • Area: $\frac{100+200}{2}\cdot 5 = 150\cdot 5 = 750\text{ requests}$. ✓

If a second server received a constant stream of $150\text{ requests/min}$ for $5$ minutes, it would handle $150\times 5 = 750\text{ requests}$—the exact same total load. ✓

A concrete example

In capacity planning for a web service, engineers need both numbers:

  • The average rate $r_{\text{avg}}=150\text{ req/min}$ tells us the total logging storage or billing volume over the $5$-minute window ($750$ requests).
  • The peak rate $r(5)=200\text{ req/min}$ tells us the minimum throughput capacity the server CPU must have at $t=5$ so that requests do not queue up or time out.

How I would explain this in class

Integrating the arrival rate $r(t)=100+20t$ from $t=0$ to $t=5$ sums up the requests over time: $\int_0^5 (100+20t)\,dt = [100t+10t^2]_0^5 = 750$ requests. Dividing $750$ requests by $5$ minutes gives an average rate of $150\text{ requests/min}$. Since $r(0)=100$ and $r(5)=200$, the average rate $150$ is the exact halfway point $\frac{r(0)+r(5)}{2}$ because $r(t)$ is linear.

Exercise 9. Minimizing the Material Used for a Can

The question

A closed right circular cylinder has a fixed volume $V>0$.

  1. Write the volume constraint.
  2. Eliminate one of the variables.
  3. Express the total surface area as a function of the radius $r$.
  4. Find the relation between the height $h$ and the radius $r$ for which the material usage (surface area) is minimal, and interpret the obtained relation geometrically.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A closed cylindrical can of base radius $r>0$ and height $h>0$ consists of:

  • Two circular bases (top and bottom), each of area $\pi r^2$,
  • A lateral wall, which unrolls into a rectangle of circumference $2\pi r$ and height $h$, having area $2\pi rh$.

Thus the total surface area is $S=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh$. Because the volume $V=\pi r^2 h$ is fixed, choosing $r$ automatically determines $h$. Eliminating $h$ gives a single-variable function $S(r)$ on $(0,\infty)$ that balances wide, flat cans (huge base area) against tall, thin cans (huge lateral area).

1. Writing the volume constraint

The volume of a cylinder with base radius $r>0$ and height $h>0$ is

$$V = \pi r^2 h.$$

2. Eliminating one of the variables

Since $r>0$, we can solve the volume constraint easily for the first-power variable $h$:

$$h = \frac{V}{\pi r^2}.$$

3. Expressing the surface area as a function of $r$

The total surface area of the closed cylinder is

$$S(r,h) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh.$$

Substituting $h=\dfrac{V}{\pi r^2}$ into this expression eliminates $h$:

$$S(r) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r\left(\frac{V}{\pi r^2}\right) = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2V}{r},\qquad r\in(0,\infty).$$

4. Finding the optimal relation between $h$ and $r$ and its geometric meaning

We differentiate $S(r)=2\pi r^2 + 2Vr^{-1}$ with respect to $r$:

$$S'(r) = 4\pi r - \frac{2V}{r^2} = \frac{4\pi r^3 - 2V}{r^2}.$$

Setting $S'(r)=0$ gives

$$4\pi r^3 - 2V = 0 \iff 2\pi r^3 = V \implies r = \sqrt[3]{\frac{V}{2\pi}}.$$

To find the direct relation between $h$ and $r$ at the optimum without carrying cube roots, we substitute $V=\pi r^2 h$ back into the optimality condition $2\pi r^3 = V$:

$$2\pi r^3 = \pi r^2 h \implies h = 2r.$$

To verify that this critical point is a global minimum on $(0,\infty)$, compute the second derivative:

$$S''(r) = 4\pi + \frac{4V}{r^3} > 0 \qquad\text{for all } r>0.$$

Since $S''(r)>0$ everywhere on $(0,\infty)$, $S(r)$ is strictly convex and $h=2r$ gives the unique global minimum.

Geometric interpretation: Since $2r=d$ is the diameter of the base, the relation $h=2r$ means the cylinder's height equals its diameter. If you slice the optimal can vertically through its central axis, the cross-section is a square of side $2r\times 2r$ (and the can fits snugly inside a cube of side $2r$).

Quick verification

Let us check the sign of $S'(r)=\frac{2(2\pi r^3-V)}{r^2}$ around $r^*=\sqrt[3]{\frac{V}{2\pi}}$ and check the surface area split:

  • For $0<r<r^*$, $2\pi r^3<V$, so $S'(r)<0$ ($S$ decreases). For $r>r^*$, $2\pi r^3>V$, so $S'(r)>0$ ($S$ increases). ✓
  • At $h=2r$, the two bases have area $2\pi r^2$ and the lateral wall has area $2\pi r(2r)=4\pi r^2$, so the lateral wall uses exactly twice the material of the two lids combined, giving $S_{\min}=6\pi r^2$. ✓

A concrete example

Suppose a beverage company designs a closed cylindrical can of volume $V=1000\text{ cm}^3$ ($1\text{ liter}$):

  • Optimal square-profile can ($h=2r$): $r=\sqrt[3]{\frac{1000}{2\pi}}\approx 5.42\text{ cm}$ and $h\approx 10.84\text{ cm}$, which requires $S\approx 553.6\text{ cm}^2$ of aluminum.
  • Tall, slim can ($r=3\text{ cm}$): $h=\frac{1000}{9\pi}\approx 35.37\text{ cm}$, requiring $S=18\pi+\frac{2000}{3}\approx 723.2\text{ cm}^2$ ($30.6\%$ more metal!).
  • Wide, flat tin ($r=10\text{ cm}$): $h=\frac{1000}{100\pi}\approx 3.18\text{ cm}$, requiring $S=200\pi+200\approx 828.3\text{ cm}^2$ ($49.6\%$ more metal!).

How I would explain this in class

A closed cylinder has volume $V=\pi r^2 h$ and surface area $S=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh$ (two circular lids plus the side wall). Solving the volume equation for $h=\frac{V}{\pi r^2}$ and plugging it into $S$ gives $S(r)=2\pi r^2+\frac{2V}{r}$ for $r>0$. Differentiating gives $S'(r)=4\pi r-\frac{2V}{r^2}=0$, so $V=2\pi r^3$. Replacing $V$ with $\pi r^2 h$ yields $\pi r^2 h = 2\pi r^3$, which simplifies to $h=2r$. Because $S''(r)=4\pi+\frac{4V}{r^3}>0$, this is a minimum: the most material-efficient closed can has height equal to its base diameter, so its vertical cross-section is a square.

Exercise 10. A Cable Through Water and Land

The question

Point $A$ lies on one bank of a straight river of width $100\text{ m}$, and point $B$ lies on the opposite bank, $500\text{ m}$ along the bank from the point $O$ directly opposite $A$.

The cost per meter of laying cable underwater is twice the cost per meter of laying cable on land.

Build a cost function depending on the point where the cable comes out of the water, determine the domain, and find the solution that minimizes the cost.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Let $O$ be the point on the opposite bank directly across from $A$, so segment $AO\perp OB$ with $|AO|=100\text{ m}$ and $|OB|=500\text{ m}$. Suppose the cable runs straight underwater from $A$ to a landing point $P$ on segment $OB$ at distance $x=|OP|$ from $O$, and then runs straight along the bank from $P$ to $B$:

  • Underwater length $|AP|=\sqrt{100^2+x^2}$ (by the Pythagorean theorem on right triangle $AOP$),
  • Land length $|PB|=500-x$.

Increasing $x$ shortens the cheap land route at unit rate $1$, while lengthening the expensive underwater diagonal at rate $\frac{x}{\sqrt{100^2+x^2}}$. The optimal landing point occurs where the marginal land savings exactly match the marginal underwater expense.

1. Building the cost function and determining the domain

Let $c>0$ be the cost per meter of cable on land; then the cost per meter underwater is $2c$. If the cable exits the water at distance $x\text{ m}$ downstream from $O$ toward $B$:

  • Underwater distance: $\sqrt{x^2+100^2}=\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}\text{ m}$, costing $2c\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}$.
  • Land distance: $(500-x)\text{ m}$, costing $c(500-x)$.

Thus the total cost function is

$$C(x) = c\left(2\sqrt{x^2+10\,000} + 500 - x\right).$$

Since $P$ lies on the segment $OB$ between $O$ ($x=0$, crossing perpendicular to the river) and $B$ ($x=500$, going entirely underwater), the natural geometric domain is the closed interval

$$D = [0,500]\quad [\text{m}].$$

(Any point with $x<0$ or $x>500$ would strictly increase both the underwater and land distances compared to $x=0$ or $x=500$.)

2. Differentiating and finding the critical point

Differentiating $C(x)$ with respect to $x$ on $(0,500)$ gives:

$$C'(x) = c\left(\frac{2x}{\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}} - 1\right).$$

Setting $C'(x)=0$:

$$\frac{2x}{\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}} = 1 \iff 2x = \sqrt{x^2+10\,000}.$$

Since $x\in[0,500]$ is non-negative, squaring both sides is an equivalent transformation:

$$4x^2 = x^2 + 10\,000 \iff 3x^2 = 10\,000 \implies x^* = \frac{100}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{100\sqrt{3}}{3} \approx 57.74\text{ m}.$$

Clearly $x^*\approx 57.74\text{ m}$ lies inside the domain $[0,500]$.

3. Verifying that $x^*$ minimizes the cost

Let us check the sign of $C'(x) = c\left(\frac{2x-\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}}{\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}}\right)$:

  • For $x\in\bigl[0,\frac{100}{\sqrt{3}}\bigr)$, we have $4x^2<x^2+10\,000$, so $2x<\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}$ and $C'(x)<0$ ($C$ is strictly decreasing).
  • For $x\in\bigl(\frac{100}{\sqrt{3}},500\bigr]$, we have $4x^2>x^2+10\,000$, so $2x>\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}$ and $C'(x)>0$ ($C$ is strictly increasing).

Thus, $x^*=\frac{100\sqrt{3}}{3}\approx 57.74\text{ m}$ is the unique global minimum of $C(x)$ on $[0,500]$.

4. Physical interpretation (Snell's Law of Refraction)

Let $\theta$ be the angle between the underwater cable $AP$ and the line $AO$ perpendicular to the river bank. In right triangle $AOP$:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+100^2}}.$$

The optimality condition $C'(x)=0$ is literally $\frac{2x}{\sqrt{x^2+100^2}}=1$, which says

$$\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2} \implies \theta = 30^\circ, \qquad \tan 30^\circ = \frac{x}{100} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}.$$

Just like light refracting into a faster medium at the critical angle, the cable crosses the river at $30^\circ$ to the normal whenever the destination $B$ is at least $57.74\text{ m}$ downstream!

Quick verification

Let us compute the exact minimum cost at $x^*=\frac{100}{\sqrt{3}}$ and compare it with the two endpoints:

  • At $x^*=\frac{100}{\sqrt{3}}$: underwater length is $\sqrt{\frac{10\,000}{3}+10\,000}=\frac{200}{\sqrt{3}}$, so $$C(x^*) = c\left(\frac{400}{\sqrt{3}}+500-\frac{100}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = c\left(500+100\sqrt{3}\right) \approx 673.21c.$$
  • Endpoint $x=0$ (straight across, then $500\text{ m}$ on land): $C(0)=c(2\cdot 100+500)=700c > 673.21c$. ✓
  • Endpoint $x=500$ (diagonal all the way underwater): $C(500)=2c\sqrt{500^2+100^2}=200\sqrt{26}\,c\approx 1019.80c > 673.21c$. ✓

A concrete example

Suppose laying cable on land costs $c=100\text{ PLN/m}$ and underwater costs $2c=200\text{ PLN/m}$:

  • Crossing straight across ($x=0$) costs $70\,000\text{ PLN}$.
  • Going diagonally underwater all the way to $B$ ($x=500\text{ m}$) costs $101\,980\text{ PLN}$.
  • Landing at $x^*=\frac{100\sqrt{3}}{3}\approx 57.74\text{ m}$ downstream (spending $115.47\text{ m}$ underwater and $442.26\text{ m}$ on land) reduces the total cost to $67\,321\text{ PLN}$, saving $2\,679\text{ PLN}$ over the perpendicular crossing.

How I would explain this in class

Let $x\in[0,500]$ be how far downstream from the opposite point $O$ the cable comes ashore. By Pythagoras, the underwater section has length $\sqrt{x^2+100^2}$ at unit cost $2c$, and the remaining land section has length $500-x$ at unit cost $c$, giving $C(x)=c\bigl(2\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}+500-x\bigr)$. Setting $C'(x)=c\bigl(\frac{2x}{\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}}-1\bigr)=0$ gives $2x=\sqrt{x^2+10\,000}$, so $3x^2=10\,000$ and $x=\frac{100\sqrt{3}}{3}\approx 57.74\text{ m}$. Because $C'(x)$ goes from negative to positive there, exiting the water $57.74\text{ m}$ downstream (at a $30^\circ$ angle) minimizes the cost.

Exercise 11. Volume of a Solid of Revolution

The question

The region under the graph

$$y=\sqrt{x},\qquad x\in[0,4],$$

is rotated about the $x$-axis.

  1. Construct the integral describing the volume using the disk method.
  2. Compute the volume.
  3. Explain where the factor $\pi y^2$ in the integral comes from.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When a planar region under $y=f(x)$ ($f(x)\ge 0$) on $[a,b]$ is rotated a full $360^\circ$ around the $x$-axis, it sweeps out a three-dimensional solid of revolution. If we slice this solid perpendicular to the $x$-axis at position $x$ with a thin slab of thickness $dx$, the cross-section is a circle of radius $R(x)=y=f(x)$. Summing the volumes of these thin circular disks via an integral gives the total volume.

1. Constructing the volume integral (disk method)

At each point $x\in[0,4]$, the distance from the axis of rotation (the $x$-axis) to the boundary curve $y=\sqrt{x}$ is the disk radius

$$R(x) = y = \sqrt{x}.$$

The cross-sectional area of the circular slice at $x$ is

$$A(x) = \pi [R(x)]^2 = \pi(\sqrt{x})^2 = \pi x.$$

Integrating the cross-sectional area $A(x)$ from $x=0$ to $x=4$ yields the volume integral:

$$V = \int_0^4 \pi (\sqrt{x})^2\,dx = \pi \int_0^4 x\,dx.$$

2. Computing the volume

Evaluating the definite integral using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$V = \pi \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^4 = \pi\left(\frac{4^2}{2}-\frac{0^2}{2}\right) = \pi\cdot\frac{16}{2} = 8\pi \approx 25.13.$$

3. Where the factor $\pi y^2$ comes from

Let us trace the geometry of a single vertical strip at position $x$ of height $y=f(x)$ and infinitesimal width $dx$:

  1. Before rotation, the strip is a thin rectangle of height $y$ and width $dx$ resting on the $x$-axis.
  2. As the graph revolves around the $x$-axis, the top point $(x,y)$ traces a circle of radius $r=y$ in the plane perpendicular to the $x$-axis.
  3. The area of a circle of radius $r=y$ is $\pi r^2 = \pi y^2$.
  4. Multiplying the circular face area $\pi y^2$ by the slab thickness $dx$ gives the volume of an elemental cylinder (disk):
$$dV = (\text{area of circular face})\times(\text{thickness}) = \pi y^2\,dx.$$

4. Special property of the paraboloid of revolution

Rotating $y=\sqrt{x}$ around the $x$-axis produces a paraboloid (since $y^2=x$, the 3D surface is $y^2+z^2=x$). Because the cross-sectional area $A(x)=\pi y^2=\pi x$ grows linearly with $x$, the average cross-sectional area on $[0,4]$ is simply half the base area at $x=4$.

Quick verification

We can verify $V=8\pi$ by comparing our paraboloid of height $H=4$ and base radius $R=\sqrt{4}=2$ against inscribed and circumscribed standard solids:

  • Circumscribed cylinder of radius $R=2$ and height $H=4$: $V_{\text{cyl}}=\pi R^2 H = \pi(2^2)(4)=16\pi$.
  • Inscribed cone sharing the vertex $(0,0)$ and circular base at $x=4$: $V_{\text{cone}}=\frac{1}{3}\pi R^2 H = \frac{16\pi}{3}\approx 5.33\pi$.
  • Our paraboloid lies strictly outside the cone (since $\sqrt{x}>\frac{x}{2}$ on $(0,4)$) and inside the cylinder, and satisfies Archimedes' classic paraboloid theorem:
$$V_{\text{paraboloid}} = \frac{1}{2}V_{\text{cyl}} = \frac{1}{2}(16\pi) = 8\pi, \qquad \frac{16\pi}{3} < 8\pi < 16\pi. \quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

Headlight reflectors and satellite dishes are shaped as paraboloids of revolution. If a laboratory glass cup has an interior profile $y=\sqrt{x}$ (with $x$ and $y$ in centimeters) and depth $4\text{ cm}$ (so its rim has radius $2\text{ cm}$ and diameter $4\text{ cm}$), its capacity is $8\pi\approx 25.13\text{ cm}^3$ ($\text{mL}$)—exactly half the volume of a straight cylindrical cup of the same depth and rim diameter ($16\pi\approx 50.27\text{ mL}$).

How I would explain this in class

Rotating the curve $y=\sqrt{x}$ around the $x$-axis turns each vertical segment of height $y$ at coordinate $x$ into a circular disk of radius $y$. Since a circle of radius $y$ has area $\pi y^2$, a thin disk of thickness $dx$ has volume $\pi y^2\,dx$. Substituting $y=\sqrt{x}$ gives $(\sqrt{x})^2=x$, so the volume integral is $\int_0^4 \pi x\,dx = \pi\bigl[\frac{x^2}{2}\bigr]_0^4 = 8\pi$, which is exactly half the volume of the surrounding cylinder of radius $2$ and length $4$.

Exercise 12. Complete Model and Computer Verification

The question

A museum wants to create a rectangular exhibition room next to an existing straight wall. The side along the wall does not need to be built. The remaining three sides are to be constructed to two different standards: the two sides perpendicular to the wall cost $300\text{ PLN/m}$ each, while the side parallel to the wall costs $500\text{ PLN/m}$. The budget is $30\,000\text{ PLN}$.

Build a model that maximizes the area of the room and prepare a complete analysis: variables and units, mathematical model, domain, derivative, determination and verification of the optimum, interpretation, graph description, numerical verification near the optimum, and a distinction between the modeling and calculation stages.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

To maximize the area of the exhibition room, we should use the entire available budget of $30\,000\text{ PLN}$. Because the two perpendicular walls ($2\times 300=600\text{ PLN/m}$ combined) and the single parallel wall ($500\text{ PLN/m}$) have different unit costs, the optimal shape will not be a square. Instead, the budget constraint ties the two side lengths linearly, turning the area into a downward-opening quadratic function of a single variable.

1. Definition of variables, units, and mathematical model

  • Let $x\ [\text{m}]$ be the length of each of the two sides perpendicular to the existing wall.
  • Let $y\ [\text{m}]$ be the length of the single side parallel to the existing wall.
  • Let $A\ [\text{m}^2]$ be the floor area of the rectangular exhibition room:
$$A(x,y) = xy\quad [\text{m}^2].$$

Building two perpendicular walls of length $x$ at $300\text{ PLN/m}$ each costs $2\cdot 300x = 600x\text{ PLN}$, and building one parallel wall of length $y$ at $500\text{ PLN/m}$ costs $500y\text{ PLN}$. Using the full budget of $30\,000\text{ PLN}$ gives the constraint equation:

$$600x + 500y = 30\,000.$$

Dividing both sides by $100$ and solving for $y$:

$$6x + 5y = 300 \implies y = 60 - \frac{6}{5}x = 60 - 1.2x.$$

Substituting $y=60-1.2x$ into $A=xy$ yields the single-variable objective function:

$$A(x) = x\left(60 - \frac{6}{5}x\right) = 60x - \frac{6}{5}x^2 = 60x - 1.2x^2\quad [\text{m}^2].$$

2. Domain resulting from the problem statement

For a physical room, both dimensions $x$ and $y$ must be strictly positive:

$$x > 0 \quad\text{and}\quad y = 60 - \frac{6}{5}x > 0 \implies \frac{6}{5}x < 60 \implies x < 50.$$

Thus the feasible domain is the open interval

$$D = (0,50)\quad [\text{m}].$$

3. Derivative, determination, and verification of the optimum

Differentiating $A(x)=60x-\frac{6}{5}x^2$ on $D=(0,50)$ gives:

$$A'(x) = 60 - \frac{12}{5}x = 60 - 2.4x.$$

Setting $A'(x)=0$ to find the critical point:

$$60 - \frac{12}{5}x = 0 \iff \frac{12}{5}x = 60 \implies x^* = 25\text{ m}.$$

Clearly $x^*=25\in(0,50)$. We verify that $x^*=25\text{ m}$ is the global maximum on $(0,50)$:

  • First derivative sign: $A'(x)>0$ for $x\in(0,25)$ and $A'(x)<0$ for $x\in(25,50)$.
  • Second derivative test: $A''(x)=-\frac{12}{5}=-2.4<0$ for all $x\in(0,50)$.

Substituting $x^*=25\text{ m}$ into the constraint and objective function gives:

$$y^* = 60 - \frac{6}{5}(25) = 60 - 30 = 30\text{ m},\qquad A_{\max} = A(25) = 25\cdot 30 = 750\text{ m}^2.$$

Interpretation: The museum achieves the maximum exhibition area of $750\text{ m}^2$ by building two perpendicular walls of length $25\text{ m}$ each and one parallel wall of length $30\text{ m}$. Notice the equal budget split: the perpendicular walls cost $600\cdot 25 = 15\,000\text{ PLN}$ (half the budget), and the parallel wall costs $500\cdot 30 = 15\,000\text{ PLN}$ (the other half of the budget).
