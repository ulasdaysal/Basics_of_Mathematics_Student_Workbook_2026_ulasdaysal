Exercise 1. A Cubic Polynomial

The question

Analyze the monotonicity and extrema of

$$f(x)=x^3-3x^2+2.$$

Sketch the graph based on the results and mark the critical points.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

The first derivative $f'(x)$ gives the slope of the tangent line at each point:

  • $f'(x) > 0$: the function is strictly increasing (rising from left to right),
  • $f'(x) < 0$: the function is strictly decreasing (falling from left to right),
  • $f'(x) = 0$: the tangent is horizontal — a critical point and candidate for a local extremum.

When $f'(x)$ changes sign across a critical point, the graph turns around and forms a local maximum ($+$ to $-$) or a local minimum ($-$ to $+$).

1. Domain and derivative

Because $f$ is a polynomial, its domain is all real numbers: $D = \mathbb{R}$.

Differentiating term by term and factoring gives

$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x = 3x(x - 2).$$

2. Critical points and sign of the derivative

Solving $f'(x) = 0$:

$$3x(x - 2) = 0 \implies x = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x = 2.$$

Since $f'(x) = 3x(x-2)$ is a quadratic with positive leading coefficient ($a = 3 > 0$), its parabola opens upward: it is positive outside the roots and negative between them.

3. Monotonicity and extrema

Evaluate $f$ at the critical points:

$$f(0) = 0^3 - 3\cdot 0^2 + 2 = 2,$$
$$f(2) = 2^3 - 3\cdot 2^2 + 2 = 8 - 12 + 2 = -2.$$
  • Increasing on $(-\infty, 0]$ and $[2, \infty)$.
  • Decreasing on $[0, 2]$.
  • Local maximum at $x = 0$ with value $f(0) = 2$ (where $f'$ changes from $+$ to $-$).
  • Local minimum at $x = 2$ with value $f(2) = -2$ (where $f'$ changes from $-$ to $+$).

4. Sketching the graph

To anchor the sketch, note the limits at infinity and the zeros:

  • $\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = -\infty$ and $\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = +\infty$.
  • By inspection, $f(1) = 1 - 3 + 2 = 0$, and factoring gives $x^3 - 3x^2 + 2 = (x-1)(x^2 - 2x - 2) = 0$, so the $x$-intercepts are $x = 1$ and $x = 1 \pm \sqrt{3} \approx -0.73, \, 2.73$.

Graph description: Starting from $-\infty$ in the third quadrant, the curve rises through $(-0.73, 0)$ to a rounded peak at the critical point $(0, 2)$ on the $y$-axis. It then falls through $(1, 0)$ (which is also its inflection point) down to a trough at the critical point $(2, -2)$, before turning upward through $(2.73, 0)$ and climbing toward $+\infty$.

Quick verification

Using the second derivative test with $f''(x) = 6x - 6$:

  • At $x = 0$: $f''(0) = -6 < 0 \implies$ local maximum. ✓
  • At $x = 2$: $f''(2) = 6 > 0 \implies$ local minimum. ✓
  • Midpoint check: at $x = 1 \in (0, 2)$, $f(1) = 0$, which lies strictly between $f(0) = 2$ and $f(2) = -2$, confirming the downward transition. ✓

A concrete example

Compare the slopes just before, between, and after the critical points:

  • At $x = -1$: $f'(-1) = 3(-1)(-3) = 9 > 0$ (steeply rising).
  • At $x = 1$: $f'(1) = 3(1)(-1) = -3 < 0$ (falling).
  • At $x = 3$: $f'(3) = 3(3)(1) = 9 > 0$ (steeply rising again).

Also notice the symmetry around the inflection point $(1, 0)$: moving $1$ unit left to $x=0$ raises $y$ by $2$, while moving $1$ unit right to $x=2$ lowers $y$ by $2$.

How I would explain this in class

To sketch $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 2$, we first differentiate to get $f'(x) = 3x(x-2)$. The slope is zero at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. Because $f'(x)$ is an upward-opening parabola, the derivative is positive outside $[0, 2]$ and negative inside it. That tells us the cubic climbs to a local maximum at $(0, 2)$, drops to a local minimum at $(2, -2)$, and then climbs forever.

Exercise 2. A Parabola from the Perspective of the Derivative

The question

For $f(x)=x^2-4x+1$, find the minimum using the derivative. Compare the result with the vertex form and explain why both approaches lead to the same point.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

We have two complementary lenses for finding the lowest point of an upward-opening parabola:

  • The calculus lens (derivative): At the bottom of the valley, the tangent line is horizontal, so the instantaneous rate of change is zero ($f'(x) = 0$).
  • The algebraic lens (vertex form): Completing the square writes $f(x) = (x - p)^2 + q$. Since a square $(x - p)^2$ is never negative, the smallest value occurs precisely when the squared term vanishes ($x = p$).

1. Finding the minimum using the derivative

The domain of $f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 1$ is $\mathbb{R}$. Differentiating gives

$$f'(x) = 2x - 4 = 2(x - 2).$$

Setting $f'(x) = 0$ gives the single critical point $x = 2$:

Because $f'(x)$ changes sign from negative to positive at $x = 2$, $f$ has a global minimum at $x = 2$ with value

$$f(2) = 2^2 - 4(2) + 1 = 4 - 8 + 1 = -3.$$

2. Finding the minimum using the vertex form

Complete the square for $x^2 - 4x + 1$:

$$f(x) = (x^2 - 4x + 4) - 4 + 1 = (x - 2)^2 - 3.$$

Since $(x - 2)^2 \ge 0$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, we have

$$f(x) = (x - 2)^2 - 3 \ge -3,$$

with equality if and only if $x - 2 = 0$, that is, $x = 2$. Thus the vertex is $(2, -3)$.

3. Why both approaches lead to the same point

If we differentiate the vertex form $f(x) = (x - p)^2 + q$ directly by the chain rule, we get

$$f'(x) = 2(x - p).$$

The condition $f'(x) = 0$ is literally $2(x - p) = 0$, which forces $x = p$ — the exact same condition that makes the non-negative square $(x - p)^2$ vanish! Geometrically, the parabola is symmetric across the vertical line $x = p$. Any non-zero slope at the vertex would break left-right symmetry, so the unique lowest point of a smooth symmetric curve must have slope zero.

Quick verification

Check the vertex formula for $ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a = 1, b = -4, c = 1$:

$$p = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{-4}{2(1)} = 2, \qquad q = f(2) = -3. \quad \checkmark$$

Also $f''(x) = 2 > 0$ everywhere, confirming strict convexity and a unique global minimum. ✓

A concrete example

Step $h = 1$ unit to the left and right of $x = 2$:

$$f(1) = (1 - 2)^2 - 3 = 1 - 3 = -2, \qquad f(3) = (3 - 2)^2 - 3 = 1 - 3 = -2.$$

The slopes at these symmetric points are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign:

$$f'(1) = 2(1 - 2) = -2, \qquad f'(3) = 2(3 - 2) = +2.$$

Halfway between slope $-2$ and slope $+2$ sits the vertex $x = 2$ with slope $0$ and value $-3$.

How I would explain this in class

With derivatives, we set $f'(x) = 2x - 4 = 0$ to find where the tangent is flat, giving $x = 2$ and $f(2) = -3$. With algebra, completing the square gives $f(x) = (x-2)^2 - 3$, which is smallest when the square $(x-2)^2$ is zero, again at $x = 2$. Both give the exact same point because differentiating $(x-p)^2 + q$ produces $2(x-p)$, so zero slope and zero squared-distance from the axis of symmetry are the same equation.

Exercise 3. A Rational Function

The question

Analyze the domain, zeros, asymptotes, and monotonicity of

$$f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-2}.$$

Finally, sketch a graph consistent with all the information obtained.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A linear fractional function (homographic function) $f(x) = \frac{ax+b}{cx+d}$ splits its domain at the point where the denominator vanishes. Before differentiating, rewriting the numerator in terms of the denominator separates $f(x)$ into a constant plus a simple reciprocal $\frac{k}{x-2}$. That single algebraic step makes the asymptotes and derivative completely transparent.

1. Domain and zeros

  • Domain: The denominator cannot be zero: $x - 2 \neq 0 \implies x \neq 2$. Thus $$D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\} = (-\infty, 2) \cup (2, \infty).$$
  • Zero ($x$-intercept): A fraction equals zero when its numerator is zero and its denominator is non-zero: $$x + 1 = 0 \implies x = -1 \in D.$$
  • $y$-intercept: $f(0) = \frac{0+1}{0-2} = -\frac{1}{2}$.

2. Asymptotes

Rewrite the formula by splitting off the constant part:

$$f(x) = \frac{(x - 2) + 3}{x - 2} = 1 + \frac{3}{x - 2}.$$
  • Vertical asymptote at $x = 2$:
  • As $x \to 2^-$, $x - 2 \to 0^-$, so $\frac{3}{x-2} \to -\infty \implies \lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = -\infty$.
  • As $x \to 2^+$, $x - 2 \to 0^+$, so $\frac{3}{x-2} \to +\infty \implies \lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = +\infty$. Hence $x = 2$ is a two-sided vertical asymptote.
  • Horizontal asymptote at $y = 1$: $$\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \left(1 + \frac{3}{x - 2}\right) = 1 + 0 = 1.$$ Hence $y = 1$ is the horizontal asymptote at both $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ (and there are no oblique asymptotes).

3. Derivative and monotonicity

Differentiate either by the quotient rule or directly from $f(x) = 1 + 3(x-2)^{-1}$:

$$f'(x) = \frac{1\cdot(x-2) - (x+1)\cdot 1}{(x-2)^2} = \frac{-3}{(x-2)^2}.$$

Since $(x - 2)^2 > 0$ for every $x \neq 2$ and the numerator is $-3 < 0$, we have $f'(x) < 0$ everywhere on the domain:

  • $f$ is strictly decreasing on $(-\infty, 2)$ and strictly decreasing on $(2, \infty)$.
  • Note: We do not say $f$ is decreasing on the union $(-\infty, 2) \cup (2, \infty)$, because for $0 < 3$ we have $f(0) = -\frac{1}{2} < f(3) = 4$.
  • There are no local extrema ($f'(x)$ never vanishes).

4. Sketching the graph

Draw dashed guide lines for the vertical asymptote $x = 2$ and the horizontal asymptote $y = 1$:

  • Left branch ($x < 2$): Starts just below the horizontal line $y = 1$ as $x \to -\infty$, decreases smoothly through $(-1, 0)$ and $(0, -\frac{1}{2})$, and plunges toward $-\infty$ alongside the left side of $x = 2$.
  • Right branch ($x > 2$): Drops down from $+\infty$ alongside the right side of $x = 2$, passes through $(3, 4)$ and $(5, 2)$, and flattens out from above toward the horizontal line $y = 1$ as $x \to +\infty$.

Quick verification

Check points on both sides of $x = 2$ using both forms $f(x) = \frac{x+1}{x-2}$ and $1 + \frac{3}{x-2}$:

  • At $x = -1$: $\frac{-1+1}{-1-2} = 0$ and $1 + \frac{3}{-3} = 0$. ✓
  • At $x = 1$: $f(1) = -2 < f(0) = -0.5 < f(-1) = 0$ (decreasing on the left branch). ✓
  • At $x = 3, 5$: $f(3) = 4 > f(5) = 2 > 1$ (decreasing on the right branch and staying above $y = 1$). ✓

A concrete example

For very large inputs such as $x = 102$ and $x = -98$:

$$f(102) = 1 + \frac{3}{100} = 1.03, \qquad f(-98) = 1 + \frac{3}{-100} = 0.97.$$

You can see immediately why $y = 1$ is the horizontal asymptote: the term $\frac{3}{x-2}$ is positive on the right (holding the graph slightly above $1$) and negative on the left (holding the graph slightly below $1$).

How I would explain this in class

Rewrite $\frac{x+1}{x-2}$ as $1 + \frac{3}{x-2}$. That shows the graph is just the hyperbola $y = \frac{3}{x}$ shifted $2$ units right and $1$ unit up. Its asymptotes are $x = 2$ and $y = 1$, its zero is at $x = -1$, and its derivative $f'(x) = \frac{-3}{(x-2)^2}$ is always negative, so each of the two hyperbola branches decreases across its entire interval.

Exercise 4. A Function with Two Vertical Asymptotes

The question

Analyze

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{x^2-1}.$$

Determine the domain, asymptotes, sign, and monotonicity on the intervals of the domain. Explain why each interval must be analyzed separately.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When the denominator vanishes at several points, those points slice the real line into disjoint open intervals. Across a vertical asymptote, a function can jump from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ (or vice versa), so properties like sign and monotonicity only hold within each connected component of the domain, never across the breaks.

1. Domain, symmetry, and sign

Factor the denominator: $x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)$.

  • Domain: $x^2 - 1 \neq 0 \implies x \neq -1$ and $x \neq 1$. Thus the domain consists of three disjoint intervals: $$D = (-\infty, -1) \cup (-1, 1) \cup (1, \infty).$$
  • Symmetry: $f(-x) = \frac{1}{(-x)^2 - 1} = f(x)$, so $f$ is an even function (symmetric about the $y$-axis).
  • Zeros and sign: The numerator is $1 \neq 0$, so $f$ has no zeros. Its sign is the sign of $x^2 - 1$:
  • $f(x) > 0$ on $(-\infty, -1)$ and $(1, \infty)$ (above the $x$-axis),
  • $f(x) < 0$ on $(-1, 1)$ (below the $x$-axis).

2. Asymptotes

  • Vertical asymptotes at $x = -1$ and $x = 1$: $$\lim_{x\to -1^-} f(x) = +\infty, \qquad \lim_{x\to -1^+} f(x) = -\infty,$$ $$\lim_{x\to 1^-} f(x) = -\infty, \qquad \lim_{x\to 1^+} f(x) = +\infty.$$
  • Horizontal asymptote at $y = 0$: $$\lim_{x\to \pm\infty} \frac{1}{x^2 - 1} = 0.$$

3. Derivative and monotonicity on each interval

Differentiating $f(x) = (x^2 - 1)^{-1}$ by the chain rule:

$$f'(x) = -\frac{2x}{(x^2 - 1)^2}.$$

Since $(x^2 - 1)^2 > 0$ everywhere on $D$, the sign of $f'(x)$ is the sign of $-2x$:

  • Strictly increasing on $(-\infty, -1)$ and on $(-1, 0]$.
  • Strictly decreasing on $[0, 1)$ and on $(1, \infty)$.
  • Local maximum at $x = 0$ with value $f(0) = -1$.

4. Why each interval must be analyzed separately

The Mean Value Theorem guarantees that $f' > 0 \implies f$ is increasing only on an interval where $f$ is continuous and differentiable. Here, $f'(x) > 0$ on both $(-\infty, -1)$ and $(-1, 0)$, yet $f$ is not increasing on their union $(-\infty, -1) \cup (-1, 0)$: at the asymptote $x = -1$, the function blows up to $+\infty$ on the left and restarts at $-\infty$ on the right! Every break in the domain resets the function's values.

Quick verification

  • Even symmetry check: the increasing behavior on $(-\infty, -1)$ and $(-1, 0]$ mirrors the decreasing behavior on $[0, 1)$ and $(1, \infty)$. ✓
  • Second derivative at $x = 0$: $f''(x) = \frac{6x^2 + 2}{(x^2-1)^3} \implies f''(0) = -2 < 0$, confirming a local maximum at $(0, -1)$. ✓

A concrete example

Take $x_1 = -2 \in (-\infty, -1)$ and $x_2 = -0.5 \in (-1, 0)$. Even though $x_1 < x_2$ and $f'(x) > 0$ at both points, we get

$$f(-2) = \frac{1}{4 - 1} = \frac{1}{3} > 0, \qquad f(-0.5) = \frac{1}{0.25 - 1} = -\frac{4}{3} < 0.$$

So $f(-2) > f(-0.5)$ despite positive derivatives on both intervals — vivid proof that monotonicity cannot be merged across the vertical asymptote $x = -1$.

How I would explain this in class

The denominator $x^2 - 1$ is zero at $x = \pm 1$, splitting the domain into three separate intervals. On the outer intervals $f$ is positive; on the middle interval $(-1, 1)$ it is negative and forms an upside-down arch with a local maximum at $(0, -1)$. Because the graph jumps between $+\infty$ and $-\infty$ at $x = \pm 1$, we have to state monotonicity on each interval individually rather than gluing intervals together.

Exercise 5. An Exponential Function with a Maximum

The question

Analyze the monotonicity and extrema of

$$f(x)=xe^{-x}.$$

Also examine the behavior as $x\to\infty$ and explain how the limit affects the shape of the graph on the right-hand side.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

The function $f(x) = xe^{-x} = \frac{x}{e^x}$ is a tug-of-war between two factors: the linear factor $x$ wants to grow, while the exponential decay factor $e^{-x}$ pulls the product toward zero. Near $x = 0$, linear growth wins and the function rises; for larger $x$, exponential decay overwhelms any polynomial, creating a peak and then pulling the graph back down toward the horizontal axis.

1. Domain and derivative

The domain is $D = \mathbb{R}$, and the only zero is $x = 0$ (since $e^{-x} > 0$ for all $x$).

Using the product rule with $(e^{-x})' = -e^{-x}$:

$$f'(x) = 1\cdot e^{-x} + x(-e^{-x}) = (1 - x)e^{-x}.$$

2. Monotonicity and extrema

Because $e^{-x} > 0$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, the sign of $f'(x)$ is determined entirely by the linear factor $1 - x$:

  • $f'(x) = 0 \iff 1 - x = 0 \iff x = 1$,
  • $f'(x) > 0 \iff x < 1$,
  • $f'(x) < 0 \iff x > 1$.

  • Strictly increasing on $(-\infty, 1]$.
  • Strictly decreasing on $[1, \infty)$.
  • Local and global maximum at $x = 1$ with value $f(1) = 1\cdot e^{-1} = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368$.

3. Behavior as $x \to \infty$ (and $x \to -\infty$)

Rewrite $f(x) = \frac{x}{e^x}$ for $x \to \infty$ (an $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ indeterminate form) and apply L'Hôpital's rule (or standard growth hierarchy):

$$\lim_{x\to\infty} xe^{-x} = \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{x}{e^x} \stackrel{\text{H}}{=} \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{1}{e^x} = 0.$$

Thus $y = 0$ is a right horizontal asymptote (approached from above, since $f(x) > 0$ for $x > 0$).

On the left, as $x \to -\infty$, we have $x \to -\infty$ and $e^{-x} \to +\infty$, so $\lim_{x\to-\infty} xe^{-x} = -\infty$ (no asymptote on the left).

4. How the limit affects the shape of the graph on the right-hand side

After reaching its peak at $(1, \frac{1}{e})$, the function is strictly decreasing ($f'(x) < 0$) on $[1, \infty)$, yet it remains strictly positive ($f(x) > 0$) and approaches $0$. To decrease forever while never crossing $y = 0$, the graph cannot keep curving downward — it must flatten out, switching from concave (cupping downward near the peak) to convex (cupping upward) at an inflection point ($x = 2$, where $f''(x) = (x-2)e^{-x} = 0$) and skimming asymptotically along the top of the positive $x$-axis.

Quick verification

  • Check $f''(x) = (x - 2)e^{-x}$: at $x = 1$, $f''(1) = -e^{-1} < 0$ (confirms the maximum), and for $x > 2$, $f''(x) > 0$ (confirms the convex tail approaching $y = 0$). ✓
  • Compare values around $x = 1$: $f(0) = 0 < f(1) \approx 0.368 > f(2) = \frac{2}{e^2} \approx 0.271 > 0$. ✓

A concrete example

Watch how quickly $e^x$ overtakes $x$ as $x$ grows:

  • $x = 1$: $f(1) = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.3679$ (peak),
  • $x = 2$: $f(2) = \frac{2}{e^2} \approx 0.2707$ (inflection point),
  • $x = 5$: $f(5) = \frac{5}{e^5} \approx 0.0337$,
  • $x = 10$: $f(10) = \frac{10}{e^{10}} \approx 0.00045$.

By $x = 10$, the graph is less than half a thousandth of a unit above the $x$-axis.

How I would explain this in class

Differentiating $f(x) = xe^{-x}$ gives $f'(x) = (1-x)e^{-x}$. Since $e^{-x}$ is always positive, the sign of the derivative comes purely from $1-x$: positive before $1$ and negative after $1$. So the function climbs from $-\infty$ through the origin to a single maximum of $\frac{1}{e}$ at $x = 1$. After $x = 1$, $e^{-x}$ dominates $x$, pulling the graph back down toward $\lim_{x\to\infty} xe^{-x} = 0$, which forces the right tail to bend smoothly and hug the positive $x$-axis from above.

Exercise 6. A Logarithmic Function

The question

Analyze the domain, zero, and monotonicity of

$$f(x)=\ln x-x+1.$$

Explain why the analysis must begin with the domain.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Derivative formulas are purely algebraic, so they often look valid on a wider set of inputs than the original function! For instance, the derivative of $\ln x$ is $\frac{1}{x}$, which makes sense for negative $x$ even though $\ln x$ does not exist for $x \le 0$. Starting with the domain keeps us from inventing phantom intervals or false critical points where the function itself is undefined.

1. Why we must begin with the domain

The natural logarithm $\ln x$ is defined only for strictly positive real numbers, whereas $-x + 1$ is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$. Therefore, the domain of $f$ is

$$D = (0, \infty).$$

If we differentiated first without recording $D = (0, \infty)$, we would write $f'(x) = \frac{1}{x} - 1 = \frac{1-x}{x}$ and might mistakenly include the interval $(-\infty, 0)$ in our sign chart — an interval where $f(x)$ does not even exist.

2. Derivative and monotonicity

For $x \in (0, \infty)$, differentiate term by term:

$$f'(x) = \frac{1}{x} - 1 = \frac{1 - x}{x}.$$

Since $x > 0$ on the entire domain, the denominator is always positive, and the sign of $f'(x)$ is simply the sign of the numerator $1 - x$:

3. Finding the zero of $f$

Notice what the global maximum tells us:

$$\max_{x \in (0, \infty)} f(x) = f(1) = 0.$$

Because $f$ strictly increases up to $f(1) = 0$ on $(0, 1]$ and strictly decreases away from $f(1) = 0$ on $[1, \infty)$, we have $f(x) < 0$ for every $x \neq 1$. Consequently, $x = 1$ is the unique zero of $f(x)$ (the graph touches the $x$-axis at $(1, 0)$ from below and turns back down).

4. Boundary behavior and global picture

  • As $x \to 0^+$, $\ln x \to -\infty$ and $-x + 1 \to 1$, so $\lim_{x\to 0^+} f(x) = -\infty$ (vertical asymptote $x = 0$).
  • As $x \to \infty$, linear growth beats logarithmic growth, so $\lim_{x\to\infty} (\ln x - x + 1) = -\infty$.
  • This proves the classical inequality $\ln x \le x - 1$ for all $x > 0$, with equality only at $x = 1$.

Quick verification

  • Check $f''(x) = -\frac{1}{x^2} < 0$ for all $x > 0$: the function is strictly concave on $(0, \infty)$, so its tangent line at $x = 1$ (which is $y = 0$) lies above the graph everywhere else. ✓
  • Test points on either side of $x = 1$: $$f(e^{-1}) = -1 - e^{-1} + 1 = -e^{-1} \approx -0.368 < 0, \qquad f(e) = 1 - e + 1 = 2 - e \approx -0.718 < 0. \quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

At $x = 2$, $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$, so

$$f(2) = \ln 2 - 2 + 1 = \ln 2 - 1 \approx -0.307 < 0.$$

And if a student forgot the domain and plugged $x = -1$ into the derivative $f'(-1) = \frac{1}{ -1 } - 1 = -2$, they might claim "$f$ is decreasing at $x = -1$," even though $f(-1) = \ln(-1) + 2$ is undefined in $\mathbb{R}$.

How I would explain this in class

Always write down the domain $x > 0$ first, because $\ln x$ doesn't exist for $x \le 0$ even though its derivative formula $\frac{1}{x}$ does. For $x > 0$, $f'(x) = \frac{1-x}{x}$ has the same sign as $1-x$, so $f$ rises on $(0, 1]$ to a global maximum of $f(1) = 0$ and falls on $[1, \infty)$. Since the highest point on the entire graph is $y = 0$ at $x = 1$, $x = 1$ is the only zero of the function.

Exercise 7. A Trigonometric Function

The question

On $[0,2\pi]$, find the intervals of monotonicity and the extrema of

$$f(x)=\sin x+\cos x.$$

Include the endpoints of the interval and compare the function values at the critical points and at the endpoints.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When analyzing a continuous function on a closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$, local extrema inside the open interval $(a, b)$ occur at critical points where $f'(x) = 0$. However, the global (and interval-boundary) behavior also depends on the endpoints $x = a$ and $x = b$, so we must evaluate $f$ at both the interior critical points and the endpoints.

1. Derivative and critical points in $(0, 2\pi)$

Differentiating $f(x) = \sin x + \cos x$:

$$f'(x) = \cos x - \sin x.$$

$$\cos x - \sin x = 0 \iff \sin x = \cos x \iff \tan x = 1$$

(noting that $\cos x = 0$ does not give a solution since $\sin x = \pm 1 \neq 0$ there). Within $[0, 2\pi]$, $\tan x = 1$ yields two critical points:

Exercise 8. The Second Derivative

The question

For

Exercise 9. An Oblique Asymptote

The question

Analyze the asymptotes of

$$f(x)=\frac{x^2+1}{x-1}.$$

Perform polynomial division and explain why the remainder term vanishes for large $|x|$.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When the degree of the numerator of a rational function is exactly $1$ greater than the degree of the denominator, polynomial division splits $f(x)$ into a linear polynomial plus a proper fraction:

$$f(x) = \underbrace{(mx + b)}_{\text{linear part}} + \underbrace{\frac{r}{x - c}}_{\text{remainder term}}.$$

As $|x| \to \infty$, the remainder term shrinks to zero, meaning the graph of $f(x)$ becomes indistinguishable from the straight line $y = mx + b$ — an oblique (slant) asymptote.

1. Domain and polynomial division

  • Domain: $x - 1 \neq 0 \implies D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\} = (-\infty, 1) \cup (1, \infty)$.
  • Polynomial division: Write $x^2 + 1 = (x^2 - 1) + 2 = (x - 1)(x + 1) + 2$ and divide by $x - 1$:
$$f(x) = \frac{x^2 + 1}{x - 1} = \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1) + 2}{x - 1} = x + 1 + \frac{2}{x - 1}.$$

Here the quotient is the linear function $y = x + 1$ and the remainder term is $\frac{2}{x - 1}$.

2. Vertical asymptote

At the excluded point $x = 1$, the numerator $x^2 + 1$ approaches $1^2 + 1 = 2 > 0$, while $x - 1 \to 0$:

$$\lim_{x\to 1^-} f(x) = \lim_{x\to 1^-} \left(x + 1 + \frac{2}{x - 1}\right) = 2 + (-\infty) = -\infty,$$
$$\lim_{x\to 1^+} f(x) = \lim_{x\to 1^+} \left(x + 1 + \frac{2}{x - 1}\right) = 2 + (+\infty) = +\infty.$$

Therefore, $x = 1$ is a two-sided vertical asymptote.

3. Oblique asymptote and why the remainder vanishes

Consider the vertical distance between the graph of $f(x)$ and the line $y = x + 1$:

$$f(x) - (x + 1) = \frac{2}{x - 1}.$$

Why does $\frac{2}{x - 1}$ vanish for large $|x|$? Because the numerator $2$ is fixed while the denominator $x - 1$ grows without bound in magnitude as $x \to +\infty$ or $x \to -\infty$. Dividing a fixed constant by an arbitrarily large number produces a result arbitrarily close to zero:

$$\lim_{x\to \pm\infty} \bigl(f(x) - (x + 1)\bigr) = \lim_{x\to \pm\infty} \frac{2}{x - 1} = 0.$$

Hence $y = x + 1$ is a two-sided oblique asymptote (and there are no horizontal asymptotes). Moreover, the sign of $\frac{2}{x-1}$ tells us which side of the asymptote the graph is on:

  • For $x > 1$: $\frac{2}{x-1} > 0$, so the graph approaches $y = x + 1$ from above.
  • For $x < 1$: $\frac{2}{x-1} < 0$, so the graph approaches $y = x + 1$ from below.

Quick verification

Use the general limit formulas for an oblique asymptote $y = mx + b$:

$$m = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{f(x)}{x} = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - x} = 1,$$
$$b = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \bigl(f(x) - mx\bigr) = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \left(\frac{x^2 + 1}{x - 1} - x\right) = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{x + 1}{x - 1} = 1.$$

This gives the exact same line $y = x + 1$. ✓

A concrete example

Compare $f(x)$ with the line $y = x + 1$ for increasing values of $x$:

How I would explain this in class

By dividing $x^2 + 1$ by $x - 1$, we rewrite $f(x)$ as $(x + 1) + \frac{2}{x - 1}$. Near $x = 1$, the fraction $\frac{2}{x-1}$ blows up, creating the vertical asymptote $x = 1$. Far away from the origin ($|x| \to \infty$), dividing $2$ by a huge number $x - 1$ gives almost zero, so the fraction $\frac{2}{x-1}$ disappears and the graph hugs the slant line $y = x + 1$.

Exercise 10. Complete Function Analysis

The question

Perform a complete analysis of

$$f(x)=\frac{x}{x^2+1}.$$

Include the domain, symmetry, zeros, limits, monotonicity, extrema, and convexity. Finally, produce a sketch based only on this information.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A complete function analysis follows a systematic checklist so no geometric detail is missed:

  1. Algebraic structure (domain, symmetry, zeros, sign) gives the global frame.
  2. Limits and asymptotes show what happens at the edges of the domain.
  3. First derivative $f'(x)$ determines rising/falling intervals and peaks/valleys.
  4. Second derivative $f''(x)$ determines how the curve bends and where its curvature switches.

1. Domain, symmetry, zeros, and limits

  • Domain: Since $x^2 + 1 \ge 1 > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, the denominator never vanishes: $D = \mathbb{R}$ (no vertical asymptotes).
  • Symmetry: $f(-x) = \frac{-x}{(-x)^2 + 1} = -\frac{x}{x^2 + 1} = -f(x)$, so $f$ is an odd function (symmetric with respect to the origin $(0,0)$).
  • Zero and sign: $f(x) = 0 \iff x = 0$. Since $x^2 + 1 > 0$, $f(x) > 0$ on $(0, \infty)$ and $f(x) < 0$ on $(-\infty, 0)$.
  • Limits at infinity and horizontal asymptote: $$\lim_{x\to \pm\infty} \frac{x}{x^2 + 1} = \lim_{x\to \pm\infty} \frac{\frac{1}{x}}{1 + \frac{1}{x^2}} = 0.$$ Thus $y = 0$ is a two-sided horizontal asymptote (approached from above as $x \to +\infty$ and from below as $x \to -\infty$).

2. First derivative, monotonicity, and extrema

Using the quotient rule:

$$f'(x) = \frac{1\cdot(x^2 + 1) - x\cdot(2x)}{(x^2 + 1)^2} = \frac{1 - x^2}{(x^2 + 1)^2} = \frac{(1 - x)(1 + x)}{(x^2 + 1)^2}.$$

Exercise 11. A Parameter and the Number of Extrema

The question

For

$$f_a(x)=x^3-3ax$$

depending on $a$, determine the number of critical points and their character. Separate the parameter cases and justify why the structure of the graph changes.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When a function depends on a parameter $a \in \mathbb{R}$, its derivative $f_a'(x)$ is a family of quadratics whose discriminant depends on $a$. A critical point produces a local extremum only if $f_a'(x)$ changes sign across it:

  • Two simple roots of $f_a'(x)$ $\implies$ two sign changes $\implies$ two local extrema,
  • One double root of $f_a'(x)$ $\implies$ no sign change $\implies$ a horizontal inflection point (no extrema),
  • No real roots of $f_a'(x)$ $\implies$ no critical points $\implies$ strictly monotonic everywhere.

1. Derivative of the family $f_a(x)$

For every $a \in \mathbb{R}$, the domain of $f_a(x) = x^3 - 3ax$ is $\mathbb{R}$, and its derivative is

$$f_a'(x) = 3x^2 - 3a = 3(x^2 - a).$$

The equation for critical points is

$$f_a'(x) = 0 \iff x^2 = a.$$

Since $x^2 \ge 0$ for all real $x$, the number of real solutions depends on the sign of $a$: $a < 0$, $a = 0$, or $a > 0$.

2. Case-by-case analysis of the parameter $a$

Case 1: $a < 0$ (no critical points, no extrema)

When $a < 0$, $-a > 0$, so $x^2 - a \ge -a > 0$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

  • Number of critical points: $0$.
  • Monotonicity and extrema: $f_a'(x) = 3(x^2 - a) > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, so $f_a$ is strictly increasing on all of $\mathbb{R}$ and has no local extrema.

Case 2: $a = 0$ (one critical point, no extrema)

When $a = 0$, $f_0(x) = x^3$ and $f_0'(x) = 3x^2$.

  • Number of critical points: $1$, at $x = 0$.

Exercise 12. A Function with an Absolute Value

The question

Analyze

$$f(x)=|x^2-4|.$$

First sketch $x^2-4$, then explain the effect of the absolute value. Indicate the points where the derivative does not exist and justify the reason geometrically.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

For any function $g(x)$, taking the absolute value $f(x) = |g(x)|$ leaves the graph unchanged wherever $g(x) \ge 0$, and reflects the negative parts across the $x$-axis wherever $g(x) < 0$. Whenever the original graph $g(x)$ crosses the $x$-axis at a non-zero angle ($g(x_0) = 0$ with $g'(x_0) \neq 0$), reflecting that crossing creates a sharp corner (a V-shaped kink) where the left-hand and right-hand slopes are opposites, so the derivative fails to exist there.

1. Sketching $g(x) = x^2 - 4$ and applying the absolute value

  • The base parabola $g(x) = x^2 - 4$: Its domain is $\mathbb{R}$, it is even, has vertex $(0, -4)$, and crosses the $x$-axis at $x = -2$ and $x = 2$.
  • $x^2 - 4 \ge 0$ on $(-\infty, -2] \cup [2, \infty)$,
  • $x^2 - 4 < 0$ on $(-2, 2)$.
  • Effect of the absolute value: We write $f(x) = |x^2 - 4|$ piecewise: $$f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 - 4, & \text{if } x \in (-\infty, -2] \cup [2, \infty), \\ 4 - x^2, & \text{if } x \in (-2, 2). \end{cases}$$ Geometrically, the outer arms for $|x| \ge 2$ stay untouched, while the U-shaped dip between $x = -2$ and $x = 2$ (with vertex $(0, -4)$) is flipped upside down across the $x$-axis into a dome with peak $(0, 4)$.

2. Points where the derivative does not exist

On the open intervals away from $x = \pm 2$, we can differentiate each formula directly:

$$f'(x) = \begin{cases} 2x, & \text{if } x \in (-\infty, -2) \cup (2, \infty), \\ -2x, & \text{if } x \in (-2, 2). \end{cases}$$

At the junction points $x = -2$ and $x = 2$, compare the one-sided derivatives:

  • At $x = 2$: $$f'_-(2) = \lim_{x\to 2^-} (-2x) = -4, \qquad f'_+(2) = \lim_{x\to 2^+} (2x) = +4.$$ Since $-4 \neq +4$, $f'(2)$ does not exist.
  • At $x = -2$: $$f'_-(-2) = \lim_{x\to -2^-} (2x) = -4, \qquad f'_+(-2) = \lim_{x\to -2^+} (-2x) = +4.$$ Since $-4 \neq +4$, $f'(-2)$ does not exist.

Geometric justification: The parabola $y = x^2 - 4$ crosses the $x$-axis at $x = \pm 2$ with steep slopes $\pm 4$ rather than tangentially. Reflecting the underside across the $x$-axis folds the tangent line of slope $-4$ into a half-tangent of slope $+4$, producing two sharp corners (cusps/kinks) at $(-2, 0)$ and $(2, 0)$ where no single tangent line exists.

Exercise 13. A Logarithm and an Asymptote

The question

Perform a complete analysis of

$$f(x)=\frac{\ln x}{x},\qquad x>0.$$

Find the maximum and the behavior at the ends of the domain. Explain which information is necessary for a correct sketch.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Just as $xe^{-x}$ pits linear growth against exponential decay, $f(x) = \frac{\ln x}{x}$ pits slow logarithmic growth in the numerator against faster linear growth in the denominator:

  • Near $x = 0^+$, $\ln x \to -\infty$ and dividing by a tiny positive $x$ makes it plunge to $-\infty$ even faster.
  • For large $x$, $\ln x$ grows much more slowly than $x$, so the ratio $\frac{\ln x}{x}$ peaks and then decays back toward $0$.

1. Domain, zero, and sign

  • Domain: $D = (0, \infty)$ (since $\ln x$ requires $x > 0$).
  • Zero and sign: Since $x > 0$, $f(x)$ has the same sign as $\ln x$:
  • $f(1) = \frac{\ln 1}{1} = 0$ is the unique zero ($x$-intercept at $(1, 0)$),
  • $f(x) < 0$ on $(0, 1)$,
  • $f(x) > 0$ on $(1, \infty)$.

2. Limits at the ends of the domain and asymptotes

  • Right-hand limit at $x = 0^+$ (vertical asymptote): As $x \to 0^+$, $\ln x \to -\infty$ and $\frac{1}{x} \to +\infty$, so their product is not indeterminate: $$\lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{\ln x}{x} = (-\infty)\cdot(+\infty) = -\infty.$$ Thus $x = 0$ (the $y$-axis) is a vertical asymptote.
  • Limit as $x \to \infty$ (horizontal asymptote): This is of type $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$. By L'Hôpital's rule (or standard growth rates): $$\lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{\ln x}{x} \stackrel{\text{H}}{=} \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{\frac{1}{x}}{1} = 0.$$ Thus $y = 0$ (the positive $x$-axis) is a horizontal asymptote as $x \to \infty$, approached from above.

3. First and second derivatives: maximum, monotonicity, and convexity

Using the quotient rule on $(0, \infty)$:

$$f'(x) = \frac{\frac{1}{x}\cdot x - (\ln x)\cdot 1}{x^2} = \frac{1 - \ln x}{x^2}.$$

Since $x^2 > 0$, the sign of $f'(x)$ is the sign of $1 - \ln x$:

$$1 - \ln x = 0 \iff \ln x = 1 \iff x = e \approx 2.718.$$

  • Global and local maximum at $x = e$ with value $f(e) = \frac{\ln e}{e} = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368$.

Differentiating $f'(x) = (1 - \ln x)x^{-2}$ gives the second derivative:

$$f''(x) = \frac{-\frac{1}{x}\cdot x^2 - (1 - \ln x)\cdot 2x}{x^4} = \frac{2\ln x - 3}{x^3}.$$

Setting $2\ln x - 3 = 0$ gives $\ln x = \frac{3}{2} \implies x = e^{3/2} = e\sqrt{e} \approx 4.482$:

  • Convex ($\cup$) on $[e^{3/2}, \infty)$,

4. Which information is necessary for a correct sketch?

To avoid drawing a misleading graph, five pieces of information are indispensable:

  1. The domain $(0, \infty)$ and vertical asymptote $x = 0$ ($\lim_{x\to 0^+} f(x) = -\infty$), which confines the graph to the right of the $y$-axis.
  2. The unique zero $(1, 0)$, where the curve crosses from below the $x$-axis to above it.
  3. The global maximum at $(e, \frac{1}{e})$, where the curve stops rising and turns downward.
  4. The horizontal asymptote $\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = 0$, which shows the curve does not dive back below the $x$-axis after $x = e$, but instead stays positive and approaches $y = 0$ from above.
  5. The inflection point at $x = e^{3/2}$, where the curve switches from concave ($\cap$) to convex ($\cup$) so it can flatten out along $y = 0$.

Quick verification

  • Substitute $t = \ln x$ (so $x = e^t$): then $f(e^t) = \frac{t}{e^t} = te^{-t}$, which is the exact function from Exercise 5! Since $te^{-t}$ attains its maximum $\frac{1}{e}$ at $t = 1$ and has an inflection point at $t = 2$ (for $t$ as the variable, whereas in $x$-coordinates $f''(x) = 0$ at $t = \frac{3}{2}$), the maximum $\frac{1}{e}$ at $\ln x = 1 \iff x = e$ is confirmed immediately. ✓

A concrete example

A famous bonus of analyzing $f(x) = \frac{\ln x}{x}$ is that it compares $a^b$ vs. $b^a$ without a calculator! For instance, which is larger: $e^\pi$ or $\pi^e$?

Since $\pi > e$ and $f(x) = \frac{\ln x}{x}$ is strictly decreasing on $[e, \infty)$, we have

$$f(e) > f(\pi) \implies \frac{\ln e}{e} > \frac{\ln \pi}{\pi} \implies \pi \ln e > e \ln \pi \implies \ln(e^\pi) > \ln(\pi^e) \implies e^\pi > \pi^e.$$

How I would explain this in class

On $(0, \infty)$, the function $f(x) = \frac{\ln x}{x}$ starts at $-\infty$ along the vertical asymptote $x = 0$, crosses the $x$-axis at $(1, 0)$, and climbs to a single global maximum of $\frac{1}{e}$ at $x = e$ where $f'(x) = \frac{1 - \ln x}{x^2} = 0$. For $x > e$, $\ln x > 1$ makes the derivative negative, so the graph decreases, flips from concave to convex at $x = e^{3/2}$, and glides down toward the horizontal asymptote $y = 0$ from above.

The question

For the function $f(x)=x^3-3x$, the derivative was correctly computed as

$$f'(x)=3(x-1)(x+1).$$

However, a student wrote the following conclusion:

The derivative is positive between its zeros and negative outside them. Therefore, the function is decreasing on $(-\infty,-1)\cup(1,\infty)$, increasing on $(-1,1)$, has a local minimum at $x=-1$, and a local maximum at $x=1$.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Knowing the roots of a factored polynomial $f'(x) = a(x - x_1)(x - x_2)$ only tells us where the sign can change; it does not tell us which intervals are positive and which are negative until we check the leading coefficient $a$ (or evaluate test points).   • The student's specific error: In $f'(x) = 3(x-1)(x+1) = 3x^2 - 3$, the leading coefficient is $a = +3 > 0$, so the parabola of $f'(x)$ opens upward ($\cup$). An upward-opening parabola is negative between its roots and positive outside them — the exact opposite of what the student wrote! How I would explain this in class

The student found the right roots of $f'(x) = 3(x-1)(x+1)$ at $x = \pm 1$, but flipped the entire sign chart upside down. Since the leading coefficient $+3$ is positive, the parabola of $f'(x)$ opens upward, meaning $f'(x)$ is negative between $-1$ and $1$ and positive outside — as a quick check at $x = 0$ shows ($f'(0) = -3 < 0$). That reverses every conclusion: $f$ increases on $(-\infty, -1]$ to a local maximum of $2$ at $x = -1$, decreases on $[-1, 1]$ to a local minimum of $-2$ at $x = 1$, and increases on $[1, \infty)$.
