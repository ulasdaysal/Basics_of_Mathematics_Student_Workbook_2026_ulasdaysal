Exercise 1. Vector Operations and Their Meaning

The question

For $u=(2,-1,3)$ and $v=(-1,4,2)$ compute

$$u+v,\qquad u-v,\qquad 3u-2v.$$

Then verify that $(u+v)-v=u$, and explain geometrically what adding two vectors means.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

In $\mathbb R^3$, vector addition, subtraction, and scalar multiplication act component by component:

  • Addition combines two displacements performed one after another.
  • Subtraction $u-v=u+(-v)$ adds the opposite of $v$, or represents the displacement needed to go from the tip of $v$ to the tip of $u$.
  • Scalar multiplication stretches or shrinks a vector by a factor (and reverses its orientation if the factor is negative) before we combine it with another vector.

1. Vector sum $u+v$

We add corresponding coordinates:

$$u+v = (2,-1,3) + (-1,4,2) = (2+(-1),\, -1+4,\, 3+2) = (1,3,5).$$

2. Vector difference $u-v$

We subtract the coordinates of $v$ from the coordinates of $u$:

$$u-v = (2,-1,3) - (-1,4,2) = (2-(-1),\, -1-4,\, 3-2) = (3,-5,1).$$

3. Linear combination $3u-2v$

First we scale each vector separately:

$$3u = 3(2,-1,3) = (6,-3,9),\qquad 2v = 2(-1,4,2) = (-2,8,4).$$

Now we subtract $2v$ from $3u$:

$$3u-2v = (6,-3,9) - (-2,8,4) = (6-(-2),\, -3-8,\, 9-4) = (8,-11,5).$$

4. Geometric meaning of vector addition

Geometrically, each vector represents a directed displacement in space:

  • Tip-to-tail rule (triangle law): Start at the origin, move along $u$ to reach $(2,-1,3)$, and from there move by $v=(-1,4,2)$. You land at $u+v=(1,3,5)$. The sum vector is the direct shortcut from the starting point to the final point.
  • Parallelogram law: Place $u$ and $v$ with the same starting point. They span a parallelogram, and $u+v$ is the diagonal starting at their common origin.

Subtracting $v$ after adding $v$ simply walks back along $-v$ from the tip of $u+v$ to the tip of $u$.

Quick verification

We check that $(u+v)-v=u$ directly using our computed sum $u+v=(1,3,5)$:

$$(u+v)-v = (1,3,5) - (-1,4,2) = (1-(-1),\, 3-4,\, 5-2) = (2,-1,3) = u.\quad \checkmark$$

We can also check the linear combination $3u-2v$ by using the algebraic identity $3u-2v = (u+v) + 2(u-v) - v$:

$$(1,3,5) + 2(3,-5,1) - (-1,4,2) = (1+6+1,\, 3-10-4,\, 5+2-2) = (8,-11,5).\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

Imagine walking in a room where coordinates $(x,y,z)$ are measured in meters (east, north, up):

  • Displacement $u=(2,-1,3)$ moves you $2\text{ m}$ east, $1\text{ m}$ south, and $3\text{ m}$ up.
  • Displacement $v=(-1,4,2)$ moves you $1\text{ m}$ west, $4\text{ m}$ north, and $2\text{ m}$ up.

Combined, your net change in position is $u+v=(1,3,5)$: $1\text{ m}$ east, $3\text{ m}$ north, and $5\text{ m}$ up. If you then undo the second move by applying $-v=(1,-4,-2)$, you return to $(2,-1,3)$, which is your position after $u$ alone.

How I would explain this in class

Vector operations in $\mathbb R^3$ happen coordinate by coordinate. Adding $u$ and $v$ gives $(1,3,5)$, subtracting gives $(3,-5,1)$, and the combination $3u-2v$ gives $(8,-11,5)$. Geometrically, $u+v$ is the net displacement when we follow $u$ and then $v$ tip-to-tail. That also explains why $(u+v)-v=u$: adding $v$ and then subtracting $v$ just steps forward along $v$ and immediately steps back along $-v$.

Exercise 2. Distance as the Length of a Vector

The question

The points are $A=(1,2,3)$ and $B=(4,-2,6)$.

  1. Construct $\overrightarrow{AB}$.
  2. Compute its length.
  3. Compute the distance between the points using the coordinate formula.
  4. Explain why both calculations give the same result.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Given two points $A=(x_A,y_A,z_A)$ and $B=(x_B,y_B,z_B)$, the displacement from $A$ to $B$ is obtained by "terminal minus initial":

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = B - A = (x_B-x_A,\, y_B-y_A,\, z_B-z_A).$$

The Euclidean length (norm) of any vector $w=(w_1,w_2,w_3)$ is

$$\|w\| = \sqrt{w_1^2 + w_2^2 + w_3^2}.$$

The distance between two points in space is simply the length of the displacement vector connecting them.

1. Constructing the vector $\overrightarrow{AB}$

Subtracting the coordinates of the initial point $A=(1,2,3)$ from the terminal point $B=(4,-2,6)$:

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = (4-1,\, -2-2,\, 6-3) = (3,-4,3).$$

2. Computing the length of $\overrightarrow{AB}$

Applying the norm formula to $\overrightarrow{AB}=(3,-4,3)$:

$$\|\overrightarrow{AB}\| = \sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16 + 9} = \sqrt{34}.$$

3. Computing the distance using the coordinate formula

The three-dimensional Euclidean distance formula between $A=(x_A,y_A,z_A)$ and $B=(x_B,y_B,z_B)$ is

$$d(A,B) = \sqrt{(x_B-x_A)^2 + (y_B-y_A)^2 + (z_B-z_A)^2}.$$

Substituting $A=(1,2,3)$ and $B=(4,-2,6)$:

$$d(A,B) = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (-2-2)^2 + (6-3)^2} = \sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16 + 9} = \sqrt{34}.$$

4. Why both calculations give the same result

The two formulas are identical because the differences $(x_B-x_A)$, $(y_B-y_A)$, and $(z_B-z_A)$ inside the distance formula are the components of $\overrightarrow{AB}$.

Geometrically, moving from $A$ to $B$ changes the coordinates by $\Delta x=3$, $\Delta y=-4$, and $\Delta z=3$. These three mutually perpendicular displacements form the edges of a $3\times 4\times 3$ rectangular box, and the segment $AB$ (as well as the arrow $\overrightarrow{AB}$) is the space diagonal of that box. By the 3D Pythagorean theorem, the length of that diagonal is $\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2+(\Delta z)^2}$.

Quick verification

  1. Adding displacement to the start point recovers the endpoint: $$A + \overrightarrow{AB} = (1,2,3) + (3,-4,3) = (4,-2,6) = B.\quad \checkmark$$
  2. Reversing direction keeps the same distance: $$\overrightarrow{BA} = A - B = (-3,4,-3),\qquad \|\overrightarrow{BA}\| = \sqrt{(-3)^2+4^2+(-3)^2} = \sqrt{34}.\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

Notice the first two components of $\overrightarrow{AB}=(3,-4,3)$: in the horizontal $xy$-plane, the displacement $(3,-4,0)$ has length

$$\sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2} = \sqrt{9+16} = 5$$

(a classic $3\text{–}4\text{–}5$ right triangle). Then we climb $\Delta z = 3$ units vertically, creating a second right triangle with horizontal leg $5$ and vertical leg $3$. Its hypotenuse has length $\sqrt{5^2+3^2}=\sqrt{25+9}=\sqrt{34}\approx 5.83$.

How I would explain this in class

To find the vector from $A$ to $B$, we subtract the start point from the end point: $\overrightarrow{AB}=(4-1,-2-2,6-3)=(3,-4,3)$. Its length is $\sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2+3^2}=\sqrt{34}$. The point-distance formula does the exact same calculation in one expression because the distance between $A$ and $B$ is literally the length of the displacement vector $\overrightarrow{AB}$.

Exercise 3. Vector Normalization

The question

For $v=(3,4)$ find a unit vector $e$ with the same direction and orientation. Check that $\|e\|=1$, reconstruct $v$ in the form $v=\|v\|e$, and explain what changes during normalization and what remains unchanged.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Every nonzero vector $v$ carries two pieces of geometric information:

  1. Its length (magnitude): $\|v\| = \sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2}$, which is a positive number.
  2. Its direction and orientation: the ray along which the arrow points.

Normalization separates these two aspects. By dividing $v$ by its own length $\|v\|$ (which is multiplying by the positive scalar $\frac{1}{\|v\|}$), we rescale the vector to length $1$ without rotating or flipping it:

$$e = \frac{1}{\|v\|}v.$$

1. Length of $v$ and the unit vector $e$

First, we compute the norm of $v=(3,4)$:

$$\|v\| = \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = \sqrt{9+16} = \sqrt{25} = 5.$$

Next, we divide $v$ by $\|v\|=5$:

$$e = \frac{1}{5}(3,4) = \left(\frac{3}{5},\, \frac{4}{5}\right).$$

Since we multiplied $v$ by a positive scalar $\frac15 > 0$, $e$ has the exact same direction and orientation as $v$.

2. Checking that $\|e\|=1$

We compute the Euclidean norm of $e=\left(\frac35,\frac45\right)$:

$$\|e\| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{25} + \frac{16}{25}} = \sqrt{\frac{25}{25}} = \sqrt{1} = 1.$$

3. Reconstructing $v=\|v\|e$

Multiplying the unit direction vector $e$ by the magnitude $\|v\|=5$ gives back the original vector:

$$\|v\|e = 5\left(\frac{3}{5},\, \frac{4}{5}\right) = \left(5\cdot\frac{3}{5},\, 5\cdot\frac{4}{5}\right) = (3,4) = v.$$

4. What changes and what remains unchanged

During normalization:

  • What changes: The length (norm) of the vector changes from $\|v\|=5$ to $\|e\|=1$, and its individual coordinates are scaled down by a factor of $5$.
  • What remains unchanged:
  • The direction (the line of action, with slope $\frac{4/5}{3/5} = \frac{4}{3}$),
  • The orientation (pointing into the first quadrant, since $\frac15>0$),
  • The ratio of the components ($3:4$) and the angles the vector makes with the coordinate axes ($\cos\theta_x = \frac35$, $\cos\theta_y = \frac45$).

Quick verification

  1. Norm check: $\left(\frac35\right)^2 + \left(\frac45\right)^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1.00.\quad \checkmark$
  2. Collinearity and orientation check via dot product: $$v\cdot e = 3\cdot\frac{3}{5} + 4\cdot\frac{4}{5} = \frac{9+16}{5} = 5 = \|v\|\,\|e\|,$$ so $\cos\angle(v,e) = 1$, confirming the angle between $v$ and $e$ is $0^\circ$. $\checkmark$

A concrete example

Suppose a boat moves with velocity vector $v=(3,4)\text{ m/s}$ (so $3\text{ m/s}$ east and $4\text{ m/s}$ north).

  • Its speed is $\|v\|=5\text{ m/s}$.
  • Its pure heading is the unit vector $e=(0.6, 0.8)$: for every $1\text{ m}$ of travel along its path, the boat advances $0.6\text{ m}$ east and $0.8\text{ m}$ north.

Writing $v = 5\cdot(0.6, 0.8)$ cleanly factors the velocity into $\text{speed} \times \text{heading}$. If the boat speeds up to $10\text{ m/s}$ along the same heading, its new velocity is simply $10e = (6,8)\text{ m/s}$.

How I would explain this in class

A vector $v=(3,4)$ has length $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$. To turn it into a unit vector pointing the same way, we divide by its length: $e=\frac15(3,4)=(\frac35,\frac45)$. Normalization changes the length to $1$, while keeping the direction, orientation, and component ratio $3:4$ completely unchanged. Writing $v=5\cdot(\frac35,\frac45)$ separates "how far" ($5$) from "which way" ($e$).

Exercise 4. Dot Product and Perpendicularity

The question

For $u=(1,2,-1)$ and $v=(2,-1,3)$ compute $u\cdot v$ and determine whether the vectors are perpendicular. Then find $a$ so that $w=(2,-1,a)$ is perpendicular to $u$, and check the result.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

For two vectors $u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ and $v=(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ in $\mathbb R^3$, the dot product (scalar product) is computed algebraically as

$$u\cdot v = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + u_3v_3,$$

and geometrically satisfies

$$u\cdot v = \|u\|\,\|v\|\cos\theta,$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $u$ and $v$. For nonzero vectors, $\cos\theta=0$ if and only if $\theta=90^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{2}$). This gives the fundamental orthogonality criterion:

$$u \perp v \iff u\cdot v = 0.$$

This criterion works both as a test (check whether $u\cdot v=0$) and as a design tool (set $u\cdot w=0$ to solve for an unknown coordinate).

1. Computing $u\cdot v$ and testing perpendicularity

For $u=(1,2,-1)$ and $v=(2,-1,3)$:

$$u\cdot v = 1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot(-1) + (-1)\cdot 3 = 2 - 2 - 3 = -3.$$

Because $u\cdot v = -3 \neq 0$, the vectors $u$ and $v$ are not perpendicular (in fact, since $u\cdot v < 0$, the angle between them is obtuse).

2. Finding $a$ so that $w=(2,-1,a)$ is perpendicular to $u$

We require $u\perp w$, which means $u\cdot w = 0$. Using $u=(1,2,-1)$ and $w=(2,-1,a)$:

$$u\cdot w = 1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot(-1) + (-1)\cdot a = 2 - 2 - a = -a.$$

Setting this equal to zero gives:

$$-a = 0 \implies a = 0.$$

Thus, the required vector is $w=(2,-1,0)$.

3. Why $a=0$ works here

Notice that the first two coordinates of $u=(1,2,-1)$ and $w=(2,-1,a)$ already cancel in the dot product:

$$1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot(-1) = 2 - 2 = 0.$$

Because the horizontal ($xy$) parts are already orthogonal, the third product $(-1)\cdot a$ must also be $0$ for the total dot product to vanish. Since $-1\neq 0$, we must have $a=0$.

Quick verification

  1. Direct dot product check for $w=(2,-1,0)$: $$u\cdot w = (1,2,-1)\cdot(2,-1,0) = 1(2) + 2(-1) + (-1)(0) = 2 - 2 + 0 = 0.\quad \checkmark$$
  2. Pythagorean theorem check: Two vectors $u$ and $w$ are perpendicular if and only if $\|u\|^2 + \|w\|^2 = \|u-w\|^2$. Here: $$\|u\|^2 = 1^2+2^2+(-1)^2 = 6,\qquad \|w\|^2 = 2^2+(-1)^2+0^2 = 5,$$ $$\|u-w\|^2 = \|(-1,3,-1)\|^2 = (-1)^2+3^2+(-1)^2 = 11 = 6 + 5.\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

The equation $u\cdot (x,y,z) = 0$, or

$$x + 2y - z = 0,$$

describes the entire plane of vectors through the origin perpendicular to $u=(1,2,-1)$. Once we fix $x=2$ and $y=-1$, we are asking at what height $z=a$ the vertical line $(2,-1,z)$ pierces that plane:

$$2 + 2(-1) - a = 0 \implies a = 0.$$

For $v=(2,-1,3)$, the height is $z=3$ instead of $0$, which is why $v$ misses the perpendicular plane and gives $u\cdot v = -3$.

How I would explain this in class

Two nonzero vectors are perpendicular exactly when their dot product is zero. For $u=(1,2,-1)$ and $v=(2,-1,3)$, we get $u\cdot v = 2-2-3 = -3 \neq 0$, so they are not perpendicular. To make $w=(2,-1,a)$ perpendicular to $u$, we solve $u\cdot w = 2-2-a = 0$, which immediately gives $a=0$.

Exercise 5. Angle Between Vectors

The question

Given $u=(1,1,0)$ and $v=(1,0,1)$:

  1. Use the sign of $u\cdot v$ to predict the type of angle.
  2. Compute the angle.
  3. Compare the result with the prediction.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

The geometric formula for the dot product of two nonzero vectors $u$ and $v$ is

$$u\cdot v = \|u\|\,\|v\|\cos\theta, \qquad \theta \in [0,\pi].$$

Because the norms $\|u\|$ and $\|v\|$ are strictly positive, the sign of $\cos\theta$ is the same as the sign of $u\cdot v$:

  • $u\cdot v > 0 \iff \cos\theta > 0 \iff \theta \in \left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ (acute angle),
  • $u\cdot v = 0 \iff \cos\theta = 0 \iff \theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ (right angle),
  • $u\cdot v < 0 \iff \cos\theta < 0 \iff \theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\right]$ (obtuse angle).

This allows us to classify the angle qualitatively before computing any square roots or inverse trig functions.

1. Predicting the type of angle from the sign of $u\cdot v$

We compute the dot product of $u=(1,1,0)$ and $v=(1,0,1)$:

$$u\cdot v = 1\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 0 + 0\cdot 1 = 1 + 0 + 0 = 1.$$

Since $u\cdot v = 1 > 0$, we have $\cos\theta > 0$, so the angle $\theta$ between $u$ and $v$ must be acute ($\theta < 90^\circ$).

2. Computing the exact angle

First, we compute the lengths of both vectors:

$$\|u\| = \sqrt{1^2+1^2+0^2} = \sqrt{2},\qquad \|v\| = \sqrt{1^2+0^2+1^2} = \sqrt{2}.$$

Now we substitute $u\cdot v = 1$ and $\|u\|=\|v\|=\sqrt{2}$ into the cosine formula:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2}.$$

Since $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ and $\cos\theta = \frac{1}{2}$, we get

$$\theta = \frac{\pi}{3} \text{ radians} = 60^\circ.$$

3. Comparing the result with the prediction

Our exact calculation gives $\theta = 60^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{3}$), which lies strictly between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$. This matches our qualitative prediction from Step 1 that the angle is acute.

Quick verification

Let us check the triangle formed by the origin $O=(0,0,0)$ and the endpoints $P=(1,1,0)$ and $Q=(1,0,1)$:

  • $\|u\| = d(O,P) = \sqrt{2}$,
  • $\|v\| = d(O,Q) = \sqrt{2}$,
  • $\|u-v\| = \|(0,1,-1)\| = \sqrt{0^2+1^2+(-1)^2} = \sqrt{2}$.

All three sides have the same length $\sqrt{2}$! Therefore, $\triangle OPQ$ is an equilateral triangle, and every interior angle of an equilateral triangle is $60^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{3}$). $\checkmark$

A concrete example

Consider the unit cube $[0,1]^3$ with one corner at the origin $(0,0,0)$:

  • $u=(1,1,0)$ is the diagonal of the bottom face (in the $xy$-plane).
  • $v=(1,0,1)$ is the diagonal of the front face (in the $xz$-plane).
  • The segment connecting their tips $(1,1,0)$ and $(1,0,1)$ has vector $(0,-1,1)$, which is a diagonal of the right face (in the $yz$-plane).

Because all three segments are face diagonals of the same unit cube, they have equal length $\sqrt{2}$ and meet at $60^\circ$.

How I would explain this in class

First, $u\cdot v = 1\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 0 + 0\cdot 1 = 1 > 0$. Because the lengths are positive, a positive dot product immediately tells us $\cos\theta > 0$, so the angle is acute. Next, both vectors have length $\sqrt{2}$, so $\cos\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2}$, which gives $\theta = 60^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{3}$)—confirming our prediction.

Exercise 6. Projection and Perpendicular Component

The question

Find the projection of $u=(4,2)$ onto the direction $v=(1,1)$. Then compute

$$r=u-\operatorname{proj}_v u$$

and verify that $r\cdot v=0$. Explain this result geometrically.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Given a vector $u$ and a nonzero direction vector $v$, the orthogonal projection of $u$ onto $v$ is the vector along the line of $v$ formed by dropping a perpendicular from the tip of $u$ onto that line:

$$\operatorname{proj}_v u = \frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v = \frac{u\cdot v}{\|v\|^2}\,v.$$

Once we have the shadow $\operatorname{proj}_v u$ along $v$, the residual (perpendicular) vector

$$r = u - \operatorname{proj}_v u$$

connects the tip of the shadow on the line of $v$ directly to the tip of $u$. Because we dropped a perpendicular to find the shadow, $r$ must be orthogonal to $v$ ($r\cdot v=0$).

1. Computing the projection $\operatorname{proj}_v u$

For $u=(4,2)$ and $v=(1,1)$, we first compute the two dot products:

$$u\cdot v = 4\cdot 1 + 2\cdot 1 = 4 + 2 = 6,$$
$$v\cdot v = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2.$$

Now we substitute these into the projection formula:

$$\operatorname{proj}_v u = \frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v = \frac{6}{2}(1,1) = 3(1,1) = (3,3).$$

2. Computing the perpendicular residual $r$

Subtracting the projection from $u$:

$$r = u - \operatorname{proj}_v u = (4,2) - (3,3) = (4-3,\, 2-3) = (1,-1).$$

3. Verifying that $r\cdot v = 0$

We compute the dot product of $r=(1,-1)$ and $v=(1,1)$:

$$r\cdot v = 1\cdot 1 + (-1)\cdot 1 = 1 - 1 = 0.$$

Since $r\cdot v=0$, the vector $r$ is indeed perpendicular to $v$.

4. Geometric explanation

The vector $v=(1,1)$ defines the $45^\circ$ diagonal line $y=x$ in the plane:

  • $\operatorname{proj}_v u = (3,3)$ is the orthogonal shadow of $u=(4,2)$ on the line $y=x$. Notice that $3 = \frac{4+2}{2}$ is the average of the two coordinates of $u$.
  • $r = u - \operatorname{proj}_v u = (1,-1)$ is the altitude vector from the point $(3,3)$ on the line $y=x$ to the point $(4,2)$.
  • Together, the vectors $\operatorname{proj}_v u = (3,3)$ and $r=(1,-1)$ are the two legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is $u=(4,2)$.

Quick verification

  1. Reconstruction check: $$\operatorname{proj}_v u + r = (3,3) + (1,-1) = (4,2) = u.\quad \checkmark$$
  2. Pythagorean theorem check: Since $\operatorname{proj}_v u \perp r$, their squared lengths must add up to $\|u\|^2$: $$\|\operatorname{proj}_v u\|^2 + \|r\|^2 = (3^2+3^2) + (1^2+(-1)^2) = 18 + 2 = 20,$$ $$\|u\|^2 = 4^2+2^2 = 16 + 4 = 20.\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

Suppose a rail cart can only roll along a straight track pointing in the direction $v=(1,1)$, and you pull on it with force $u=(4,2)\text{ N}$:

  • The component $\operatorname{proj}_v u = (3,3)\text{ N}$ (of magnitude $3\sqrt{2}\approx 4.24\text{ N}$) is the effective force that actually accelerates the cart along the track.
  • The component $r=(1,-1)\text{ N}$ (of magnitude $\sqrt{2}\approx 1.41\text{ N}$) pulls sideways against the rails and is completely absorbed by the track without moving the cart forward.

How I would explain this in class

To project $u=(4,2)$ onto $v=(1,1)$, we compute $\frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}v = \frac{6}{2}(1,1) = (3,3)$. That is the part of $u$ parallel to $v$. Subtracting it from $u$ leaves $r = (4,2)-(3,3) = (1,-1)$, whose dot product with $v$ is $1(1)+(-1)(1)=0$. Geometrically, we split $u$ into two perpendicular legs of a right triangle: its shadow $(3,3)$ along the line of $v$, and the perpendicular drop $(1,-1)$ from $u$ to that line.

Exercise 7. Linear Dependence Without Heavy Computation

The question

Given

$$u=(1,2,3),\qquad v=(2,4,6),\qquad w=(0,1,1).$$

Identify a specific relation between $u$ and $v$, decide whether the three vectors can be linearly independent, and explain why no long calculation is needed.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Vectors $v_1, v_2, v_3$ are linearly dependent if there exist scalars $c_1, c_2, c_3$, not all zero, such that

$$c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + c_3 v_3 = \mathbf{0}.$$

A key structural rule is:

If any subset of a collection of vectors is already linearly dependent (for instance, if two vectors are scalar multiples of each other), then the entire collection is automatically linearly dependent.

We can simply assign coefficient $0$ to all the remaining vectors.

1. Identifying the relation between $u$ and $v$

Comparing the coordinates of $u=(1,2,3)$ and $v=(2,4,6)$ term by term:

$$v = (2,4,6) = (2\cdot 1,\, 2\cdot 2,\, 2\cdot 3) = 2(1,2,3) = 2u.$$

Equivalently,

$$2u - v = (0,0,0) = \mathbf{0}.$$

Geometrically, $u$ and $v$ are collinear: they lie on the exact same line through the origin, with $v$ having twice the length of $u$.

2. Deciding whether $u,v,w$ can be linearly independent

No—the three vectors $u,v,w$ are linearly dependent (so they cannot be linearly independent).

Indeed, using the relation $2u-v=\mathbf{0}$, we can write a nontrivial linear combination of all three vectors by giving $w$ the coefficient $0$:

$$2u - 1v + 0w = \mathbf{0}.$$

Because the coefficients $(c_1,c_2,c_3)=(2,-1,0)$ are not all zero, the definition of linear dependence is satisfied immediately.

3. Why no long calculation is needed

Normally, testing three vectors in $\mathbb R^3$ for linear independence might involve row-reducing a $3\times 3$ matrix or computing a $3\times 3$ determinant. Here, no such calculation is needed because:

  1. Redundancy is already visible in the first two vectors: Since $v=2u$, $v$ contributes no new direction beyond $u$. Together, $\operatorname{span}(u,v)$ is just a 1D line.
  2. Adding one more vector $w$ can increase the dimension by at most $1$: A line plus one vector $w$ can span at most a 2D plane in $\mathbb R^3$, never all of $\mathbb R^3$. Three vectors trapped inside a 2D plane are always linearly dependent.

Quick verification

  1. Direct substitution into the linear combination: $$2u - 1v + 0w = 2(1,2,3) - 1(2,4,6) + 0(0,1,1) = (2-2,\, 4-4,\, 6-6) = (0,0,0).\quad \checkmark$$
  2. Determinant check: If we place $u,v,w$ as the rows of a $3\times 3$ matrix, $$\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix} = 0$$ because Row 2 is twice Row 1, and any matrix with proportional rows has determinant $0$. $\checkmark$

A concrete example

Think of $u,v,w$ as three movement instructions in 3D space:

  • Instruction $u$: step along $(1,2,3)$.
  • Instruction $v$: step along $(2,4,6)$—which is just two steps of instruction $u$.
  • Instruction $w$: step along $(0,1,1)$.

Because instruction $v$ duplicates the direction of instruction $u$, you only have two independent directions of motion ($u$ and $w$), not three. You are confined to the plane spanned by $u$ and $w$, and taking $2$ steps of $u$ followed by $-1$ step of $v$ brings you right back to the origin without ever using $w$.

How I would explain this in class

Before setting up a matrix or a determinant, always look at the vectors first. Here $v=(2,4,6)$ is simply $2u$. That means $2u - v + 0w = \mathbf{0}$ is a nontrivial linear combination equal to the zero vector, with coefficients $(2,-1,0)\neq(0,0,0)$. Whenever two vectors in a set are proportional, the whole set is automatically linearly dependent—no row reduction or determinant calculation is needed.

Exercise 8. Linear Combination and Checking the Result

The question

Check whether $w=(5,1)$ can be written as

$$w=au+bv,$$

where $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(2,-1)$. If so, find $a,b$, reconstruct $w$ from them, and explain the geometric meaning of the existence of such coefficients.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Writing a target vector $w$ as a linear combination

$$w = au + bv$$

means finding how much we need to stretch or shrink $u$ (by factor $a$) and $v$ (by factor $b$) so that their vector sum reaches $w$.

In $\mathbb R^2$, equating the two coordinates of $au+bv$ and $w$ turns the single vector equation into a system of two linear equations in the two unknowns $a$ and $b$. Since $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(2,-1)$ are not scalar multiples of each other, they point in two different directions and span the entire plane $\mathbb R^2$.

1. Setting up the system of equations

We substitute $u=(1,1)$, $v=(2,-1)$, and $w=(5,1)$ into $au+bv=w$:

$$a(1,1) + b(2,-1) = (5,1) \iff (a+2b,\, a-b) = (5,1).$$

Comparing the first and second coordinates gives the linear system:

$$\begin{cases} a + 2b = 5, \\ a - b = 1. \end{cases}$$

2. Solving for $a$ and $b$

From the second equation, we express $a$ in terms of $b$:

$$a = b + 1.$$

Substituting $a = b + 1$ into the first equation:

$$(b + 1) + 2b = 5 \implies 3b + 1 = 5 \implies 3b = 4 \implies b = \frac{4}{3}.$$

Now we find $a$:

$$a = \frac{4}{3} + 1 = \frac{7}{3}.$$

So $w$ can be written as $au+bv$, with unique coefficients $a = \frac{7}{3}$ and $b = \frac{4}{3}$.

3. Reconstructing $w$ from $a$ and $b$

We compute $\frac{7}{3}u + \frac{4}{3}v$ directly:

$$\frac{7}{3}(1,1) + \frac{4}{3}(2,-1) = \left(\frac{7}{3},\, \frac{7}{3}\right) + \left(\frac{8}{3},\, -\frac{4}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{7+8}{3},\, \frac{7-4}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{15}{3},\, \frac{3}{3}\right) = (5,1) = w.$$

4. Geometric meaning of the existence of $a$ and $b$

Geometrically, the existence of $a=\frac73$ and $b=\frac43$ means:

  1. $u$ and $v$ form a basis (a skewed coordinate grid) for $\mathbb R^2$: Because $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(2,-1)$ are non-collinear, we can reach any point in the plane by moving exclusively along the directions of $u$ and $v$.
  2. Parallelogram decomposition: If we draw lines through the origin along $u$ and $v$, and lines through $w=(5,1)$ parallel to $u$ and $v$, we form a parallelogram with vertices $(0,0)$, $au=\left(\frac73,\frac73\right)$, $bv=\left(\frac83,-\frac43\right)$, and $w=(5,1)$.
  3. New coordinates: The pair $(a,b)=\left(\frac73,\frac43\right)$ are the coordinates of $w$ in the basis $\{u,v\}$: to reach $(5,1)$, take $\frac73$ steps of $u$ followed by $\frac43$ steps of $v$.

Quick verification

Let us check both scalar equations with $a=\frac73$ and $b=\frac43$:

  • First coordinate: $a + 2b = \frac{7}{3} + 2\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) = \frac{7+8}{3} = \frac{15}{3} = 5.\quad \checkmark$
  • Second coordinate: $a - b = \frac{7}{3} - \frac{4}{3} = \frac{3}{3} = 1.\quad \checkmark$

We can also check using Cramer's rule:

$$\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&-1\end{pmatrix} = -1 - 2 = -3 \neq 0,$$
$$a = \frac{\det\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}}{-3} = \frac{-5-2}{-3} = \frac{7}{3},\qquad b = \frac{\det\begin{pmatrix}1&5\\1&1\end{pmatrix}}{-3} = \frac{1-5}{-3} = \frac{4}{3}.\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

Compare this with standard coordinates: when we write $w=(5,1)$, we are writing

$$(5,1) = 5(1,0) + 1(0,1),$$

which uses the standard horizontal and vertical unit vectors $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$. When we instead use the tilted axes $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(2,-1)$, the exact same geometric arrow $w$ is described by the recipe $\frac73 u + \frac43 v$.

How I would explain this in class

Writing $w=au+bv$ gives the system $a+2b=5$ and $a-b=1$. Subtracting the second equation from the first gives $3b=4$, so $b=\frac43$ and $a=b+1=\frac73$. Reconstructing $\frac73(1,1)+\frac43(2,-1)$ gives $(\frac{15}{3},\frac33)=(5,1)=w$. Geometrically, because $u$ and $v$ point in different directions in $\mathbb R^2$, they act as a tilted coordinate grid, and $(\frac73,\frac43)$ tells us how far to travel along each direction to land on $w$.

Exercise 9. Cross Product as a Perpendicular Direction

The question

For $u=(1,2,0)$ and $v=(0,1,3)$ compute $u\times v$. Use dot products to check perpendicularity to both vectors, compute $v\times u$, and compare the results.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

In $\mathbb R^3$, the cross product of $u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ and $v=(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ is a vector given by the symbolic determinant

$$u\times v = \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\u_1&u_2&u_3\\v_1&v_2&v_3\end{vmatrix} = \left(u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\; u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\; u_1v_2-u_2v_1\right).$$

Key geometric properties of $u\times v$:

  1. Perpendicularity: $u\times v$ is orthogonal to both $u$ and $v$ (and therefore perpendicular to the plane spanned by $u$ and $v$).
  2. Anticommutativity (orientation): Swapping the order of the two vectors reverses the orientation of the cross product (by the right-hand rule): $$v\times u = -(u\times v).$$

1. Computing $u\times v$

For $u=(1,2,0)$ and $v=(0,1,3)$:

$$u\times v = \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\1&2&0\\0&1&3\end{vmatrix} = \left(\begin{vmatrix}2&0\\1&3\end{vmatrix},\; -\begin{vmatrix}1&0\\0&3\end{vmatrix},\; \begin{vmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{vmatrix}\right).$$

Evaluating the three $2\times 2$ determinants:

  • First component: $2\cdot 3 - 0\cdot 1 = 6$,
  • Second component: $-(1\cdot 3 - 0\cdot 0) = -3$,
  • Third component: $1\cdot 1 - 2\cdot 0 = 1$.

Therefore,

$$u\times v = (6,-3,1).$$

2. Checking perpendicularity using dot products

We take the dot product of $u\times v = (6,-3,1)$ with $u=(1,2,0)$ and with $v=(0,1,3)$:

$$(u\times v)\cdot u = (6,-3,1)\cdot(1,2,0) = 6\cdot 1 + (-3)\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 0 = 6 - 6 + 0 = 0,$$
$$(u\times v)\cdot v = (6,-3,1)\cdot(0,1,3) = 6\cdot 0 + (-3)\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 3 = 0 - 3 + 3 = 0.$$

Since both dot products are $0$, $u\times v$ is perpendicular to both $u$ and $v$.

3. Computing $v\times u$

Now we put $v=(0,1,3)$ in the second row and $u=(1,2,0)$ in the third row:

$$v\times u = \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\0&1&3\\1&2&0\end{vmatrix} = \left(\begin{vmatrix}1&3\\2&0\end{vmatrix},\; -\begin{vmatrix}0&3\\1&0\end{vmatrix},\; \begin{vmatrix}0&1\\1&2\end{vmatrix}\right).$$

Evaluating each component:

  • First component: $1\cdot 0 - 3\cdot 2 = -6$,
  • Second component: $-(0\cdot 0 - 3\cdot 1) = -(-3) = 3$,
  • Third component: $0\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 1 = -1$.

Thus,

$$v\times u = (-6,3,-1).$$

4. Comparing $u\times v$ and $v\times u$

Comparing the two results:

$$u\times v = (6,-3,1),\qquad v\times u = (-6,3,-1) = -(u\times v).$$
  • Same line and same length: Both vectors lie on the exact same line perpendicular to the plane of $u$ and $v$, and both have length $\sqrt{6^2+(-3)^2+1^2}=\sqrt{46}$.
  • Opposite orientation: They point in opposite directions. Geometrically, curling the fingers of your right hand from $u$ to $v$ makes your thumb point along $u\times v=(6,-3,1)$, whereas curling them from $v$ to $u$ flips your thumb to point along $v\times u=(-6,3,-1)$. Algebraically, swapping two rows of a determinant multiplies the determinant by $-1$.

Quick verification

Let us verify Lagrange's identity $\|u\times v\|^2 = \|u\|^2\|v\|^2 - (u\cdot v)^2$:

  • Left side: $\|u\times v\|^2 = 6^2 + (-3)^2 + 1^2 = 36 + 9 + 1 = 46$.
  • Right side: $\|u\|^2 = 1^2+2^2+0^2 = 5$, $\|v\|^2 = 0^2+1^2+3^2 = 10$, and $u\cdot v = 1(0)+2(1)+0(3) = 2$, so $$\|u\|^2\|v\|^2 - (u\cdot v)^2 = 5\cdot 10 - 2^2 = 50 - 4 = 46.\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

Think of turning a right-handed screw with a wrench:

  • If the wrench handle lies along $u$ and you push in the direction $v$, the torque vector is $u\times v$, which drives the screw outward along $(6,-3,1)$.
  • If the handle is along $v$ and you push in the direction $u$, the rotation reverses and the screw moves inward along $v\times u = (-6,3,-1)$.

How I would explain this in class

Using the $2\times 2$ minors, $u\times v = (6,-3,1)$. We immediately verify our arithmetic by checking $(6,-3,1)\cdot(1,2,0)=6-6=0$ and $(6,-3,1)\cdot(0,1,3)=-3+3=0$, confirming that $u\times v$ is perpendicular to both $u$ and $v$. When we compute $v\times u$, we get $(-6,3,-1) = -(u\times v)$: swapping the two vectors keeps the same perpendicular line and length, but flips the orientation to the opposite side of the plane.

Exercise 10. Triangle Area from Vectors

The question

The points $A=(0,0,0)$, $B=(2,1,0)$, and $C=(1,3,2)$ form a triangle. Construct $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$, compute the area of the parallelogram using the cross product, and then find the area of the triangle. Explain the factor $\frac12$.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

For two vectors $u=\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $v=\overrightarrow{AC}$ sharing the initial point $A$ in $\mathbb R^3$, the length of their cross product equals the area of the parallelogram they span:

$$\text{Area}_{\text{parallelogram}} = \|u\times v\| = \|u\|\,\|v\|\sin\theta,$$

where $\|u\|$ is the base of the parallelogram and $\|v\|\sin\theta$ is its perpendicular height.

Because the triangle $\triangle ABC$ is formed by cutting this parallelogram in half along the diagonal $BC$, the area of the triangle is half the area of the parallelogram:

$$\text{Area}_{\triangle ABC} = \frac{1}{2}\left\|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\right\|.$$

1. Constructing $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$

Since $A=(0,0,0)$ is at the origin, subtracting $A$ from $B=(2,1,0)$ and $C=(1,3,2)$ gives:

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = B - A = (2-0,\, 1-0,\, 0-0) = (2,1,0),$$
$$\overrightarrow{AC} = C - A = (1-0,\, 3-0,\, 2-0) = (1,3,2).$$

2. Computing the cross product and the parallelogram area

First we compute the cross product $\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}$:

$$\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\2&1&0\\1&3&2\end{vmatrix} = \left(\begin{vmatrix}1&0\\3&2\end{vmatrix},\; -\begin{vmatrix}2&0\\1&2\end{vmatrix},\; \begin{vmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{vmatrix}\right).$$

Evaluating the $2\times 2$ determinants:

  • First component: $1\cdot 2 - 0\cdot 3 = 2$,
  • Second component: $-(2\cdot 2 - 0\cdot 1) = -4$,
  • Third component: $2\cdot 3 - 1\cdot 1 = 6 - 1 = 5$.

So

$$\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = (2,-4,5).$$

The area of the parallelogram spanned by $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$ is the norm of this cross product:

$$\text{Area}_{\text{parallelogram}} = \left\|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\right\| = \sqrt{2^2 + (-4)^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{4 + 16 + 25} = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}.$$

3. Finding the area of $\triangle ABC$

Dividing the parallelogram area by $2$:

$$\text{Area}_{\triangle ABC} = \frac{1}{2}\text{Area}_{\text{parallelogram}} = \frac{3\sqrt{5}}{2}.$$

4. Explaining the factor $\frac{1}{2}$

Let $D = B + \overrightarrow{AC} = (3,4,2)$ be the fourth vertex of the parallelogram spanned by $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$:

  • The diagonal segment $BC$ splits the parallelogram $ABDC$ into two triangles: $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle DCB$.
  • These two triangles have identical side lengths ($\|\overrightarrow{AB}\|=\|\overrightarrow{CD}\|$, $\|\overrightarrow{AC}\|=\|\overrightarrow{BD}\|$, and common side $BC$), so they are congruent by Side-Side-Side (SSS) and have equal area.
  • Therefore, $\triangle ABC$ occupies exactly half of the parallelogram's area. This is the exact same $\frac12$ that appears in the familiar formula $\text{Area}_{\triangle} = \frac12\cdot\text{base}\cdot\text{height}$, whereas a parallelogram has $\text{Area} = \text{base}\cdot\text{height}$.

Quick verification

Let us verify the area without the cross product using $\text{Area}_{\triangle ABC} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\|\overrightarrow{AB}\|^2\|\overrightarrow{AC}\|^2 - (\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC})^2}$:

  • $\|\overrightarrow{AB}\|^2 = 2^2+1^2+0^2 = 5$,
  • $\|\overrightarrow{AC}\|^2 = 1^2+3^2+2^2 = 14$,
  • $\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC} = 2(1)+1(3)+0(2) = 5$.

Then:

$$\text{Area}_{\triangle ABC} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5\cdot 14 - 5^2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{70 - 25} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{45} = \frac{3\sqrt{5}}{2}.\quad \checkmark$$

Also check orthogonality of the cross product: $(2,-4,5)\cdot(2,1,0)=4-4=0$ and $(2,-4,5)\cdot(1,3,2)=2-12+10=0$. $\checkmark$

A concrete example

Notice the three components of $\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = (2,-4,5)$:

  • If we project $\triangle ABC$ onto the $yz$-plane (setting $x=0$), its shadow has vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(3,2)$ and area $\frac12|2| = 1$.
  • Its shadow on the $xz$-plane has area $\frac12|-4| = 2$.
  • Its shadow on the $xy$-plane has area $\frac12|5| = \frac52$.

By the 3D Pythagorean theorem for areas (De Gua's / Cauchy–Binet formula), the true tilted triangle area satisfies

$$\text{Area}_{\triangle ABC} = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + \left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4 + \frac{25}{4}} = \sqrt{\frac{45}{4}} = \frac{3\sqrt{5}}{2}.$$

How I would explain this in class

First, the edge vectors from $A$ are $\overrightarrow{AB}=(2,1,0)$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}=(1,3,2)$. Their cross product is $\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=(2,-4,5)$. The length of that cross product gives the area of the full parallelogram they span: $\sqrt{4+16+25}=\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt{5}$. Finally, because the diagonal $BC$ cuts the parallelogram into two congruent triangles, the area of $\triangle ABC$ is half of the parallelogram's area, giving $\frac{3\sqrt{5}}{2}$.

Exercise 11. Decomposition into Parallel and Perpendicular Components

The question

Decompose $u=(3,4)$ into components parallel and perpendicular to $v=(1,2)$. Check

$$u=u_{\parallel}+u_{\perp},\qquad u_{\perp}\cdot v=0.$$

Explain why these are two independent checks of correctness.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Every vector $u$ can be uniquely decomposed with respect to a nonzero reference direction $v$ as

$$u = u_{\parallel} + u_{\perp},$$

where:

  • $u_{\parallel} = \operatorname{proj}_v u = \dfrac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v$ is a scalar multiple of $v$ (parallel component),
  • $u_{\perp} = u - u_{\parallel}$ is orthogonal to $v$, meaning $u_{\perp}\cdot v = 0$ (perpendicular component).

Geometrically, we are setting up a local right-angled coordinate system aligned with $v$ and splitting $u$ into a leg along $v$ and a leg perpendicular to $v$.

1. Finding the parallel component $u_{\parallel}$

For $u=(3,4)$ and $v=(1,2)$, we compute the dot products:

$$u\cdot v = 3\cdot 1 + 4\cdot 2 = 3 + 8 = 11,$$
$$v\cdot v = 1^2 + 2^2 = 1 + 4 = 5.$$

Therefore, the parallel component is

$$u_{\parallel} = \operatorname{proj}_v u = \frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v = \frac{11}{5}(1,2) = \left(\frac{11}{5},\, \frac{22}{5}\right).$$

2. Finding the perpendicular component $u_{\perp}$

We subtract $u_{\parallel}$ from $u=(3,4)=\left(\frac{15}{5},\frac{20}{5}\right)$:

$$u_{\perp} = u - u_{\parallel} = \left(\frac{15}{5},\, \frac{20}{5}\right) - \left(\frac{11}{5},\, \frac{22}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{4}{5},\, -\frac{2}{5}\right).$$

3. Checking the two required conditions

  1. Sum check ($u = u_{\parallel} + u_{\perp}$): $$u_{\parallel} + u_{\perp} = \left(\frac{11}{5},\, \frac{22}{5}\right) + \left(\frac{4}{5},\, -\frac{2}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{11+4}{5},\, \frac{22-2}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{15}{5},\, \frac{20}{5}\right) = (3,4) = u.$$
  2. Orthogonality check ($u_{\perp}\cdot v = 0$): $$u_{\perp}\cdot v = \left(\frac{4}{5},\, -\frac{2}{5}\right)\cdot(1,2) = \frac{4}{5}\cdot 1 + \left(-\frac{2}{5}\right)\cdot 2 = \frac{4 - 4}{5} = 0.$$

4. Why these are two independent checks of correctness

Neither condition alone guarantees that $(u_{\parallel}, u_{\perp})$ is the true orthogonal decomposition of $u$ along $v$:

  • Why $u = u_{\parallel} + u_{\perp}$ alone is not enough: If we picked any wrong scalar multiple of $v$—say $\tilde{u}_{\parallel} = 2v = (2,4)$—and defined $\tilde{u}_{\perp} = u - \tilde{u}_{\parallel} = (1,0)$, their sum $\tilde{u}_{\parallel} + \tilde{u}_{\perp}$ would still equal $(3,4)=u$ automatically! However, $\tilde{u}_{\perp}\cdot v = (1,0)\cdot(1,2) = 1 \neq 0$, so the second check catches the wrong projection factor.
  • Why $u_{\perp}\cdot v = 0$ alone is not enough: Any multiple of $(2,-1)$, such as $(2,-1)$ or $(0,0)$, satisfies $w\cdot v = 0$, even if it has nothing to do with $u=(3,4)$. The sum check $u_{\parallel}+u_{\perp}=u$ ensures that our two components actually reconstruct the given vector $u$ without arithmetic mistakes during subtraction.

Together, (1) $u_{\parallel}$ being a multiple of $v$, (2) $u_{\perp}\cdot v = 0$, and (3) $u_{\parallel}+u_{\perp}=u$ uniquely lock in the decomposition.

Quick verification

Let us also check the Pythagorean theorem $\|u_{\parallel}\|^2 + \|u_{\perp}\|^2 = \|u\|^2$:

$$\|u_{\parallel}\|^2 = \left(\frac{11}{5}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{22}{5}\right)^2 = \frac{121 + 484}{25} = \frac{605}{25} = \frac{121}{5},$$
$$\|u_{\perp}\|^2 = \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{2}{5}\right)^2 = \frac{16 + 4}{25} = \frac{20}{25} = \frac{4}{5}.$$

Adding them:

$$\|u_{\parallel}\|^2 + \|u_{\perp}\|^2 = \frac{121}{5} + \frac{4}{5} = \frac{125}{5} = 25 = 3^2 + 4^2 = \|u\|^2.\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

Notice that $v=(1,2)$ has perpendicular direction $v_{\perp}=(2,-1)$ (obtained by swapping the two coordinates and negating one, since $(1,2)\cdot(2,-1)=0$). Writing $u=(3,4)$ in the orthogonal basis $\{v, v_{\perp}\}$ gives

$$(3,4) = \frac{11}{5}(1,2) + \frac{2}{5}(2,-1) = \left(\frac{11}{5},\, \frac{22}{5}\right) + \left(\frac{4}{5},\, -\frac{2}{5}\right),$$

which immediately produces $u_{\parallel}$ and $u_{\perp}$ at the same time!

How I would explain this in class

First, we project $u=(3,4)$ onto $v=(1,2)$: $u_{\parallel} = \frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}v = \frac{11}{5}(1,2) = (\frac{11}{5},\frac{22}{5})$. Subtracting this from $u$ gives $u_{\perp} = (3,4)-(\frac{11}{5},\frac{22}{5}) = (\frac45,-\frac25)$. The two checks test two completely different things: $u_{\parallel}+u_{\perp}=u$ verifies that the pieces add back up to $u$, while $u_{\perp}\cdot v=0$ verifies that we chose the exact multiple of $v$ that makes the remainder perpendicular to $v$.

Exercise 12. A Normal Vector to a Plane

The question

Given $A=(1,0,2)$, $B=(2,1,0)$, and $C=(0,3,1)$. Construct two vectors lying in the plane, find a vector perpendicular to both, and check the perpendicularity using dot products.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Three non-collinear points $A,B,C$ in $\mathbb R^3$ determine a unique plane. To find a normal vector $n$ (a vector perpendicular to the entire plane):

  1. Pick one point (such as $A$) as an anchor and form two displacement vectors $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$ lying inside the plane.
  2. Compute their cross product: $$n = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}.$$

Because $n$ is perpendicular to both spanning vectors $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$, it is automatically perpendicular to every linear combination of them—and therefore perpendicular to every line in the plane.

1. Constructing two vectors in the plane

Using $A=(1,0,2)$ as the common starting point:

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = B - A = (2-1,\, 1-0,\, 0-2) = (1,1,-2),$$
$$\overrightarrow{AC} = C - A = (0-1,\, 3-0,\, 1-2) = (-1,3,-1).$$

Both $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$ lie in the plane through $A,B,C$.

2. Finding a vector perpendicular to both

We compute the cross product $n = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}$:

$$n = \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\1&1&-2\\-1&3&-1\end{vmatrix} = \left(\begin{vmatrix}1&-2\\3&-1\end{vmatrix},\; -\begin{vmatrix}1&-2\\-1&-1\end{vmatrix},\; \begin{vmatrix}1&1\\-1&3\end{vmatrix}\right).$$

Evaluating the three $2\times 2$ determinants carefully:

  • First component: $1(-1) - (-2)(3) = -1 + 6 = 5$,
  • Second component: $-(1(-1) - (-2)(-1)) = -(-1 - 2) = 3$,
  • Third component: $1(3) - 1(-1) = 3 + 1 = 4$.

Therefore, a normal vector to the plane is

$$n = (5,3,4).$$

(Note: because $n\neq(0,0,0)$, the three points $A,B,C$ are not collinear and genuinely determine a unique plane. Any nonzero scalar multiple of $(5,3,4)$ is also a valid normal vector.)

3. Checking perpendicularity using dot products

We compute the dot products of $n=(5,3,4)$ with $\overrightarrow{AB}=(1,1,-2)$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}=(-1,3,-1)$:

$$n\cdot\overrightarrow{AB} = (5,3,4)\cdot(1,1,-2) = 5(1) + 3(1) + 4(-2) = 5 + 3 - 8 = 0,$$
$$n\cdot\overrightarrow{AC} = (5,3,4)\cdot(-1,3,-1) = 5(-1) + 3(3) + 4(-1) = -5 + 9 - 4 = 0.$$

Since both dot products vanish, $n=(5,3,4)$ is perpendicular to both $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$.

Quick verification

What about the third edge of the triangle, $\overrightarrow{BC} = C - B = (0-2,\, 3-1,\, 1-0) = (-2,2,1)$? It also lies in the plane, so $n$ must be perpendicular to $\overrightarrow{BC}$ as well:

$$n\cdot\overrightarrow{BC} = (5,3,4)\cdot(-2,2,1) = -10 + 6 + 4 = 0.\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

Once we have the normal vector $n=(5,3,4)$ and the point $A=(1,0,2)$, we can write the equation of the plane through $A,B,C$ in one line: a point $P=(x,y,z)$ lies in the plane if and only if $\overrightarrow{AP}\perp n$, that is,

$$n\cdot(x-1,\, y-0,\, z-2) = 0 \iff 5(x-1) + 3y + 4(z-2) = 0 \iff 5x + 3y + 4z = 13.$$

Let us plug all three given points into $5x+3y+4z$:

  • For $A=(1,0,2)$: $5(1)+3(0)+4(2) = 5+0+8 = 13$,
  • For $B=(2,1,0)$: $5(2)+3(1)+4(0) = 10+3+0 = 13$,
  • For $C=(0,3,1)$: $5(0)+3(3)+4(1) = 0+9+4 = 13$.

Every point in the plane has the exact same dot product $13$ with the normal vector $n=(5,3,4)$!

How I would explain this in class

To find a normal to the plane through $A,B,C$, we first anchor at $A$ and form two vectors inside the plane: $\overrightarrow{AB}=(1,1,-2)$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}=(-1,3,-1)$. Then we take their cross product, $\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=(5,3,4)$. Finally, we check our work with dot products: $(5,3,4)\cdot(1,1,-2)=5+3-8=0$ and $(5,3,4)\cdot(-1,3,-1)=-5+9-4=0$. Because $(5,3,4)$ is perpendicular to two non-parallel directions in the plane, it is perpendicular to the entire plane.

Exercise 13. Do Four Points Lie in One Plane?

The question

Given

$$A=(0,0,0),\qquad B=(1,2,0),\qquad C=(0,1,1),\qquad D=(2,5,1).$$
  1. Construct $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$.
  2. Find $n=\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}$.
  3. Construct $\overrightarrow{AD}$ and check whether $\overrightarrow{AD}\cdot n=0$.
  4. Based on this, decide whether $D$ lies in the plane determined by $A,B,C$, and justify the criterion.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Suppose $A,B,C$ are three non-collinear points in $\mathbb R^3$. The cross product

$$n = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}$$

is a nonzero normal vector perpendicular to the plane through $A,B,C$.

Now take any fourth point $D$. The plane through $A,B,C$ consists of all points whose displacement from $A$ is perpendicular to $n$. Therefore:

$$D \text{ lies in the plane } ABC \iff \overrightarrow{AD} \perp n \iff \overrightarrow{AD}\cdot n = 0.$$

Geometrically, $|\overrightarrow{AD}\cdot n|$ is the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by $\overrightarrow{AB},\overrightarrow{AC},\overrightarrow{AD}$; this volume is zero if and only if all three vectors lie flat in the same plane.

1. Constructing $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$

Since $A=(0,0,0)$, subtracting $A$ from $B=(1,2,0)$ and $C=(0,1,1)$ gives:

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = B - A = (1,2,0),$$
$$\overrightarrow{AC} = C - A = (0,1,1).$$

2. Finding the normal vector $n=\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}$

We compute the cross product of $\overrightarrow{AB}=(1,2,0)$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}=(0,1,1)$:

$$n = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\1&2&0\\0&1&1\end{vmatrix} = \left(\begin{vmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{vmatrix},\; -\begin{vmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{vmatrix},\; \begin{vmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{vmatrix}\right).$$

Evaluating the $2\times 2$ determinants:

  • First component: $2(1) - 0(1) = 2$,
  • Second component: $-(1(1) - 0(0)) = -1$,
  • Third component: $1(1) - 2(0) = 1$.

Thus,

$$n = (2,-1,1).$$

(Quick check: $n\cdot\overrightarrow{AB} = 2-2+0=0$ and $n\cdot\overrightarrow{AC} = 0-1+1=0$.)

3. Constructing $\overrightarrow{AD}$ and checking $\overrightarrow{AD}\cdot n=0$

For $D=(2,5,1)$ and $A=(0,0,0)$:

$$\overrightarrow{AD} = D - A = (2,5,1).$$

Taking the dot product with $n=(2,-1,1)$:

$$\overrightarrow{AD}\cdot n = (2,5,1)\cdot(2,-1,1) = 2\cdot 2 + 5\cdot(-1) + 1\cdot 1 = 4 - 5 + 1 = 0.$$

4. Decision and justification of the criterion

Because $\overrightarrow{AD}\cdot n = 0$, the point $D$ does lie in the plane determined by $A,B,C$ (the four points $A,B,C,D$ are coplanar).

Why this criterion works: Since $n=\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=(2,-1,1)\neq\mathbf{0}$, the vectors $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$ span a 2D plane through $A$, and $n$ is perpendicular to that plane. The orthogonal complement of the nonzero vector $n$ in $\mathbb R^3$ is a 2D subspace—namely, the exact plane spanned by $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$. Therefore, $\overrightarrow{AD}\cdot n=0$ means $\overrightarrow{AD}$ has zero component in the normal direction (zero height above or below the plane $ABC$), so $D$ lies inside the plane $ABC$.

Quick verification

If $D$ lies in the plane spanned by $\overrightarrow{AB}=(1,2,0)$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}=(0,1,1)$ through $A$, then $\overrightarrow{AD}=(2,5,1)$ must be an explicit linear combination of $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$:

$$\overrightarrow{AD} = \alpha\overrightarrow{AB} + \beta\overrightarrow{AC} = \alpha(1,2,0) + \beta(0,1,1) = (\alpha,\, 2\alpha+\beta,\, \beta).$$

Comparing with $(2,5,1)$ immediately gives $\alpha=2$ and $\beta=1$, and the middle coordinate satisfies $2(2)+1 = 5$! Indeed:

$$2\overrightarrow{AB} + 1\overrightarrow{AC} = 2(1,2,0) + (0,1,1) = (2,5,1) = \overrightarrow{AD}.\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

What if the fourth point were $D'=(2,5,2)$, just $1$ unit higher in the $z$-direction than $D=(2,5,1)$? Then $\overrightarrow{AD'}=(2,5,2)$, and

$$\overrightarrow{AD'}\cdot n = (2,5,2)\cdot(2,-1,1) = 4 - 5 + 2 = 1 \neq 0.$$

That nonzero result ($1$) tells us immediately that $D'$ does not lie in the plane $ABC$—it sticks out of the plane on the side toward which $n$ points.

How I would explain this in class

First, $\overrightarrow{AB}=(1,2,0)$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}=(0,1,1)$ lie in the plane through $A,B,C$, so their cross product $n=\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=(2,-1,1)$ is a normal vector perpendicular to that plane. Next, we check whether $\overrightarrow{AD}=(2,5,1)$ is also perpendicular to $n$: $(2,5,1)\cdot(2,-1,1) = 4-5+1 = 0$. Because $\overrightarrow{AD}$ has zero component along the normal $n$, it does not rise above or dip below the plane—in fact, $\overrightarrow{AD}=2\overrightarrow{AB}+\overrightarrow{AC}$, so $D$ lies in the plane $ABC$.

Exercise 14. Closest Point on a Line

The question

Given a point $P=(4,1)$ and a line through the origin with direction $v=(1,2)$. Using projection, find the closest point on the line, the distance from $P$ to the line, and verify that the appropriate vector is perpendicular to the direction of the line.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Let $\ell$ be the line through the origin $O=(0,0)$ with direction vector $v=(1,2)$, so every point on $\ell$ has position vector $tv$ for some $t\in\mathbb R$.

By the Pythagorean theorem, the shortest path from an external point $P$ to a line $\ell$ is along the perpendicular dropped from $P$ to $\ell$:

  1. The foot of the perpendicular—the closest point $Q$ on the line—has position vector equal to the orthogonal projection of $p=\overrightarrow{OP}$ onto $v$: $$\overrightarrow{OQ} = \operatorname{proj}_v p = \frac{p\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v.$$
  2. The displacement vector from the closest point $Q$ to $P$ is the perpendicular residual $$\overrightarrow{QP} = p - \operatorname{proj}_v p,$$ and its length $\|\overrightarrow{QP}\|$ is the distance from $P$ to the line.

1. Finding the closest point $Q$ on the line

Let $p = \overrightarrow{OP} = (4,1)$ and $v = (1,2)$. We compute the dot products:

$$p\cdot v = 4\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 2 = 4 + 2 = 6,$$
$$v\cdot v = 1^2 + 2^2 = 1 + 4 = 5.$$

The projection of $p$ onto $v$ is

$$\overrightarrow{OQ} = \operatorname{proj}_v p = \frac{p\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v = \frac{6}{5}(1,2) = \left(\frac{6}{5},\, \frac{12}{5}\right).$$

Therefore, the closest point on the line is

$$Q = \left(\frac{6}{5},\, \frac{12}{5}\right) = (1.2,\, 2.4).$$

2. Finding the distance from $P$ to the line

First, we construct the vector from the closest point $Q$ to $P$:

$$\overrightarrow{QP} = P - Q = (4,1) - \left(\frac{6}{5},\, \frac{12}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{20-6}{5},\, \frac{5-12}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{14}{5},\, -\frac{7}{5}\right).$$

The distance $d(P,\ell)$ from $P$ to the line is the norm of $\overrightarrow{QP}$:

$$d(P,\ell) = \left\|\overrightarrow{QP}\right\| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{14}{5}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{7}{5}\right)^2} = \frac{1}{5}\sqrt{196 + 49} = \frac{\sqrt{245}}{5} = \frac{7\sqrt{5}}{5}.$$

3. Verifying perpendicularity to the line

The vector connecting the closest point $Q$ on the line to $P$ is $\overrightarrow{QP} = \left(\frac{14}{5}, -\frac{7}{5}\right)$. Taking its dot product with the line's direction vector $v=(1,2)$:

$$\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot v = \left(\frac{14}{5},\, -\frac{7}{5}\right)\cdot(1,2) = \frac{14}{5}\cdot 1 + \left(-\frac{7}{5}\right)\cdot 2 = \frac{14 - 14}{5} = 0.$$

Since $\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot v = 0$, the segment $QP$ is perpendicular to the direction of the line.

4. Why this perpendicularity guarantees minimum distance

Take any point $X = tv$ on the line $\ell$. We can split the displacement $\overrightarrow{XP}$ into a piece along the line ($\overrightarrow{XQ}$) and the perpendicular piece ($\overrightarrow{QP}$):

$$\overrightarrow{XP} = \overrightarrow{XQ} + \overrightarrow{QP}.$$

Since $\overrightarrow{XQ}$ is parallel to $v$ and $\overrightarrow{QP} \perp v$, the Pythagorean theorem gives

$$\|\overrightarrow{XP}\|^2 = \|\overrightarrow{XQ}\|^2 + \|\overrightarrow{QP}\|^2 \ge \|\overrightarrow{QP}\|^2,$$

with equality if and only if $\|\overrightarrow{XQ}\|=0$, i.e., $X=Q$. Thus $Q=\left(\frac65,\frac{12}{5}\right)$ uniquely minimizes the distance.

Quick verification

Let us check the distance using the 2D point-to-line formula: the line through $(0,0)$ with direction $v=(1,2)$ has Cartesian equation $2x - y = 0$. The distance from $P=(4,1)$ to $2x - y = 0$ is

$$d = \frac{|2(4) - 1(1)|}{\sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2}} = \frac{|8 - 1|}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{7}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{7\sqrt{5}}{5}.\quad \checkmark$$

Also check the Pythagorean theorem on $\triangle OQP$:

$$\|\overrightarrow{OQ}\|^2 + \|\overrightarrow{QP}\|^2 = \frac{36+144}{25} + \frac{196+49}{25} = \frac{180 + 245}{25} = \frac{425}{25} = 17 = 4^2 + 1^2 = \|\overrightarrow{OP}\|^2.\quad \checkmark$$

A concrete example

Let us compare $Q=(1.2, 2.4)$ with two nearby integer-parameter points on the line $X(t)=t(1,2)$:

  • At $t=1$, $X(1)=(1,2)$ has distance $\|(4,1)-(1,2)\| = \|(3,-1)\| = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$.
  • At $t=2$, $X(2)=(2,4)$ has distance $\|(4,1)-(2,4)\| = \|(2,-3)\| = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.606$.
  • At the projection parameter $t=\frac65=1.2$, $Q=(1.2, 2.4)$ achieves the minimum distance $\frac{7\sqrt{5}}{5} = \sqrt{9.8} \approx 3.130$.

How I would explain this in class

Every point on the line has position $tv$, and the closest point $Q$ to $P=(4,1)$ is the orthogonal projection of $p=(4,1)$ onto $v=(1,2)$: $Q = \frac{p\cdot v}{v\cdot v}v = \frac65(1,2) = (\frac65,\frac{12}{5})$. The connecting vector from $Q$ to $P$ is $\overrightarrow{QP} = (4,1)-(\frac65,\frac{12}{5}) = (\frac{14}{5},-\frac75)$. We check $\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot(1,2) = \frac{14}{5}-\frac{14}{5}=0$, confirming $\overrightarrow{QP}\perp v$, and its length $\|\overrightarrow{QP}\| = \frac{7\sqrt{5}}{5}$ is the shortest distance from $P$ to the line.

Exercise 15. Comparing Directions Using the Cosine of the Angle

The question

Given

$$a=(1,2,1),\qquad b=(2,4,2),\qquad c=(2,0,3).$$

For each pair, compute

$$\frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}.$$

Order the pairs by increasing angle between the vectors. Explain why for $a$ and $b$ the value is $1$ even though the vectors have different lengths.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

For any two nonzero vectors $u$ and $v$, the quantity

$$\cos\theta = \frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}$$

is the cosine of the angle $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ between them. Notice that we can also write it as the dot product of their unit normalizations:

$$\frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|} = \left(\frac{u}{\|u\|}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right).$$

Because the function $\cos\theta$ is strictly decreasing on the interval $[0,\pi]$ (from $\cos 0 = 1$ down to $\cos\pi = -1$), a larger cosine means a smaller angle, and a smaller cosine means a larger angle.

1. Computing the lengths of $a,b,c$

First, we compute the norm of each vector:

$$\|a\| = \sqrt{1^2+2^2+1^2} = \sqrt{1+4+1} = \sqrt{6},$$
$$\|b\| = \sqrt{2^2+4^2+2^2} = \sqrt{4+16+4} = \sqrt{24} = 2\sqrt{6},$$
$$\|c\| = \sqrt{2^2+0^2+3^2} = \sqrt{4+0+9} = \sqrt{13}.$$

2. Computing $\dfrac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}$ for each pair

  1. Pair $(a,b)$: $$a\cdot b = 1(2) + 2(4) + 1(2) = 2 + 8 + 2 = 12,$$ $$\cos\angle(a,b) = \frac{a\cdot b}{\|a\|\,\|b\|} = \frac{12}{\sqrt{6}\cdot 2\sqrt{6}} = \frac{12}{12} = 1.$$
  2. Pair $(a,c)$: $$a\cdot c = 1(2) + 2(0) + 1(3) = 2 + 0 + 3 = 5,$$ $$\cos\angle(a,c) = \frac{a\cdot c}{\|a\|\,\|c\|} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{6}\,\sqrt{13}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{78}} \approx 0.566.$$
  3. Pair $(b,c)$: $$b\cdot c = 2(2) + 4(0) + 2(3) = 4 + 0 + 6 = 10,$$ $$\cos\angle(b,c) = \frac{b\cdot c}{\|b\|\,\|c\|} = \frac{10}{2\sqrt{6}\,\sqrt{13}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{78}} \approx 0.566.$$

3. Ordering the pairs by increasing angle

Comparing the cosines:

$$\cos\angle(a,b) = 1 > \cos\angle(a,c) = \cos\angle(b,c) = \frac{5}{\sqrt{78}}.$$

Since $\cos\theta$ decreases as $\theta$ increases on $[0,\pi]$:

  • $\angle(a,b) = \arccos(1) = 0^\circ$ ($0\text{ rad}$) is the smallest angle,
  • $\angle(a,c) = \angle(b,c) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{5}{\sqrt{78}}\right) \approx 55.5^\circ$ ($0.969\text{ rad}$) are equal and larger.

So in order of increasing angle:

$$\angle(a,b) < \angle(a,c) = \angle(b,c).$$

4. Why the value for $a$ and $b$ is $1$ despite different lengths

Notice that $b = (2,4,2) = 2(1,2,1) = 2a$. The vector $b$ is a positive scalar multiple of $a$: it points in the exact same direction and orientation as $a$, so the angle between $a$ and $b$ is $0^\circ$ and $\cos 0^\circ = 1$.

Algebraically, when we replace $a$ by $b=2a$ in the cosine formula, the scaling factor $2$ appears in both the numerator and the denominator and cancels out completely:

$$\frac{a\cdot(2a)}{\|a\|\,\|2a\|} = \frac{2(a\cdot a)}{2\|a\|\,\|a\|} = \frac{\|a\|^2}{\|a\|^2} = 1.$$

For the exact same reason, $\cos\angle(a,c)$ and $\cos\angle(b,c)$ are identical: dividing by $\|u\|\,\|v\|$ normalizes both vectors to unit length first, so $\frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}$ measures pure direction and is completely independent of vector length.

Quick verification

Let us normalize $a$ and $b$ first:

$$\hat{a} = \frac{a}{\|a\|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1,2,1),\qquad \hat{b} = \frac{b}{\|b\|} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{6}}(2,4,2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1,2,1).$$

Because $\hat{a} = \hat{b}$, their unit direction vectors are identical! Therefore:

  • $\hat{a}\cdot\hat{b} = \|\hat{a}\|^2 = 1$, $\checkmark$
  • $\hat{a}\cdot\hat{c} = \hat{b}\cdot\hat{c} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{78}}$. $\checkmark$

Also note that $\left(\frac{5}{\sqrt{78}}\right)^2 = \frac{25}{78} < 1$, satisfying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|u\cdot v| \le \|u\|\,\|v\|$. $\checkmark$

A concrete example

In information retrieval and machine learning, $\frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}$ is called cosine similarity. Suppose two readers rate three genres (Sci-Fi, Math, History):

  • Reader $A$ rates them $a=(1,2,1)$ (out of $5$),
  • Reader $B$ rates them $b=(2,4,2)$ (out of $10$),
  • Reader $C$ rates them $c=(2,0,3)$.

Even though Reader $B$'s raw numbers are twice as large as Reader $A$'s, their relative preferences are identical ($1:2:1$). Cosine similarity divides out the overall scale and reports $\cos\angle(a,b)=1$ (a perfect match in profile direction), while giving both $A$ and $B$ the exact same similarity $\frac{5}{\sqrt{78}}\approx 0.566$ with Reader $C$.

How I would explain this in class

First, $\|a\|=\sqrt{6}$, $\|b\|=2\sqrt{6}$, and $\|c\|=\sqrt{13}$. Computing $\frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}$ gives $1$ for $(a,b)$ and $\frac{5}{\sqrt{78}}$ for both $(a,c)$ and $(b,c)$. Because cosine decreases as the angle increases on $[0,\pi]$, $(a,b)$ has the smallest angle ($0^\circ$), followed by $(a,c)$ and $(b,c)$ which tie at $\arccos(5/\sqrt{78})\approx 55.5^\circ$. The value for $(a,b)$ is $1$—and $(a,c)$ matches $(b,c)$—because $b=2a$ points in the exact same direction as $a$, and dividing by the norms cancels out the factor of $2$ in length.
