Exercise 1. Recognizing Curves from Canonical Form

The question

Recognize the curves

$$x^2+y^2=9,$$
$$\frac{x^2}{16}+\frac{y^2}{9}=1,$$
$$y^2=8x,$$
$$\frac{x^2}{9}-\frac{y^2}{4}=1.$$

For each one, identify the most important features and make a sketch. Explain which parts of the equation let you recognize the type of curve.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When a second-degree curve in the plane is written in canonical form (centered at the origin or with vertex at the origin), the signs and degrees of the terms in $x$ and $y$ immediately reveal its geometric family:

  • Two squared terms with the same sign and equal coefficients ($x^2+y^2=r^2$): a circle of radius $r$.
  • Two squared terms with the same sign and different denominators ($\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$): an ellipse with semiaxes $a$ and $b$.
  • One squared term and one linear term ($y^2=2px$ or $x^2=2py$): a parabola.
  • Two squared terms with opposite signs ($\frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$): a hyperbola.

1. First curve: $x^2+y^2=9$

Both $x^2$ and $y^2$ appear with coefficient $+1$, and the right-hand side is $9=3^2>0$.

  • Type: Circle.
  • Key features: Center at $(0,0)$, radius $r=3$, axis intercepts at $(\pm 3,0)$ and $(0,\pm 3)$.
  • How we recognize it: $x^2+y^2$ measures the squared distance from $(0,0)$, so setting it equal to $3^2$ selects all points at distance $3$ from the origin.

2. Second curve: $\frac{x^2}{16}+\frac{y^2}{9}=1$

Both $x^2$ and $y^2$ are positive and sum to $1$, with denominators $16=4^2$ and $9=3^2$.

  • Type: Ellipse.
  • Key features: Center at $(0,0)$, horizontal semiaxis $a=4$, vertical semiaxis $b=3$, vertices at $(\pm 4,0)$ and $(0,\pm 3)$, stretched horizontally since $4>3$.
  • How we recognize it: A sum of two positive squares equals $1$, so neither coordinate can grow unboundedly ($|x|\le 4$, $|y|\le 3$), producing a closed oval with different scales along $Ox$ and $Oy$.

Only $y$ is squared ($y^2\ge 0$), while $x$ is linear with a positive coefficient ($8=2p$, so $p=4$).

4. Fourth curve: $\frac{x^2}{9}-\frac{y^2}{4}=1$

Both variables are squared, but they have opposite signs ($+$ before $\frac{x^2}{3^2}$ and $-$ before $\frac{y^2}{2^2}$).

  • Type: Hyperbola.
  • Key features: Center at $(0,0)$, real semiaxis $a=3$ along $Ox$, imaginary semiaxis $b=2$ along $Oy$, vertices at $(\pm 3,0)$, no $y$-intercepts, asymptotes $y=\pm\frac{2}{3}x$.
  • How we recognize it: The minus sign allows $x^2$ and $y^2$ to grow arbitrarily large together, while $\frac{x^2}{9}=1+\frac{y^2}{4}\ge 1$ forces $|x|\ge 3$, splitting the curve into two separate branches opening left and right.

Quick verification

Let us check one characteristic point on each curve:

  • Circle: $(3,0)\implies 3^2+0^2=9$. ✓
  • Ellipse: $(4,0)\implies \frac{16}{16}+\frac{0}{9}=1$, and $(0,3)\implies \frac{0}{16}+\frac{9}{9}=1$. ✓
  • Parabola: $(2,4)\implies 4^2=16=8\cdot 2$. ✓
  • Hyperbola: $(3,0)\implies \frac{9}{9}-\frac{0}{4}=1$, and $(0,y)$ gives $-\frac{y^2}{4}=1$ (no real solution). ✓

A concrete example

Suppose we change a single feature in $\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=1$:

  • Keep $+$ and make denominators equal ($9$ and $9$): $\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{9}=1 \iff x^2+y^2=9$, which turns the ellipse into a circle.
  • Change $+$ to $-$: $\frac{x^2}{9}-\frac{y^2}{4}=1$, which breaks the closed loop into the two unbounded branches of a hyperbola.

How I would explain this in class

To recognize a second-degree curve in canonical form, I first check which variables are squared and what signs they have. If only one variable is squared, like $y^2=8x$, it is a parabola. If both are squared with the same sign, the curve is bounded: equal coefficients give a circle ($x^2+y^2=9$ with radius $3$), and different denominators give an ellipse ($\frac{x^2}{16}+\frac{y^2}{9}=1$ with semiaxes $4$ and $3$). If the squared terms have opposite signs, like $\frac{x^2}{9}-\frac{y^2}{4}=1$, it is a hyperbola with vertices at $(\pm 3,0)$ and asymptotes $y=\pm\frac{2}{3}x$.

Exercise 2. Circle by Completing the Square

The question

Reduce

$$x^2+y^2-6x+4y-3=0$$

to canonical form. Find the center and radius, then substitute two simple points from the circle into both forms of the equation.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A circle with center $(x_0,y_0)$ and radius $r>0$ has the canonical equation

$$(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2=r^2.$$

When this equation is expanded, it produces linear terms $-2x_0 x$ and $-2y_0 y$. To reverse the expansion, we group the $x$-terms and $y$-terms separately and complete the square using

$$x^2+bx = \left(x+\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2.$$

1. Grouping terms and completing the square

Start by grouping the $x$-terms, grouping the $y$-terms, and moving the constant to the right-hand side:

$$(x^2-6x) + (y^2+4y) = 3.$$

For $x^2-6x$, half of $-6$ is $-3$, and $(-3)^2=9$:

$$x^2-6x = (x-3)^2 - 9.$$

For $y^2+4y$, half of $4$ is $2$, and $2^2=4$:

$$y^2+4y = (y+2)^2 - 4.$$

Substitute these into the grouped equation:

$$(x-3)^2 - 9 + (y+2)^2 - 4 = 3.$$

Add $9+4=13$ to both sides to obtain the canonical form:

$$(x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 16.$$

2. Reading the center and radius

Compare $(x-3)^2 + (y-(-2))^2 = 4^2$ with $(x-x_0)^2 + (y-y_0)^2 = r^2$:

  • Center: $C = (3,-2)$ (note the opposite signs of the numbers inside the parentheses).
  • Radius: $r = \sqrt{16} = 4$.

3. Finding two simple points on the circle

From the center $(3,-2)$, moving by the radius $r=4$ parallel to the coordinate axes gives four cardinal points:

  • Moving right by $4$: $P_1 = (3+4,-2) = (7,-2)$.
  • Moving up by $4$: $P_2 = (3,-2+4) = (3,2)$.
  • (Also left: $(-1,-2)$ and down: $(3,-6)$.)

4. Substituting the points into both forms

For $P_1 = (7,-2)$:

  • Canonical form: $(7-3)^2 + (-2+2)^2 = 4^2 + 0^2 = 16$.
  • General form: $7^2 + (-2)^2 - 6(7) + 4(-2) - 3 = 49 + 4 - 42 - 8 - 3 = 0$.

For $P_2 = (3,2)$:

  • Canonical form: $(3-3)^2 + (2+2)^2 = 0^2 + 4^2 = 16$.
  • General form: $3^2 + 2^2 - 6(3) + 4(2) - 3 = 9 + 4 - 18 + 8 - 3 = 0$.

Both forms represent the exact same set of points.

Quick verification

Expand the canonical form $(x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=16$ back out:

$$(x^2-6x+9) + (y^2+4y+4) - 16 = x^2+y^2-6x+4y-3 = 0.$$

This recovers the original equation identically. ✓

A concrete example

Notice that $(0,0)$ is at squared distance $(0-3)^2+(0+2)^2 = 9+4 = 13 < 16$ from the center $(3,-2)$, so the origin lies inside the circle. In the general equation, substituting $(0,0)$ gives $-3<0$, which also shows that $(0,0)$ is inside the circle.

How I would explain this in class

To find the center and radius of $x^2+y^2-6x+4y-3=0$, we complete the square separately for $x$ and $y$. Half of $-6$ is $-3$ and half of $+4$ is $+2$, which gives $(x-3)^2-9 + (y+2)^2-4 - 3 = 0$, or $(x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=16$. From this canonical form we immediately read the center $(3,-2)$ and radius $r=4$. Stepping $4$ units right or up from the center gives $(7,-2)$ and $(3,2)$, which satisfy both equations.

Exercise 3. Ellipse

The question

For

$$\frac{x^2}{25}+\frac{y^2}{9}=1,$$

find the semiaxis lengths, the intercepts with the coordinate axes, and make a sketch. Explain along which axis the ellipse is more stretched and how this is visible in the equation.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

The canonical equation of an ellipse centered at the origin is

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1\qquad (a>0,\;b>0).$$

Because a square is never negative ($x^2\ge 0$ and $y^2\ge 0$), neither fraction can exceed $1$:

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}\le 1 \iff |x|\le a,\qquad \frac{y^2}{b^2}\le 1 \iff |y|\le b.$$

Thus $a$ is the horizontal semiaxis (maximum horizontal reach from the center) and $b$ is the vertical semiaxis (maximum vertical reach from the center).

1. Semiaxis lengths

Write the denominators as squares of positive numbers:

$$25 = 5^2,\qquad 9 = 3^2.$$

Therefore:

  • Horizontal semiaxis (along $Ox$): $a = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
  • Vertical semiaxis (along $Oy$): $b = \sqrt{9} = 3$.

The full major axis has length $2a=10$, and the full minor axis has length $2b=6$.

2. Intercepts with the coordinate axes

  • Intercepts with the $x$-axis ($y=0$): $$\frac{x^2}{25} + 0 = 1 \implies x^2 = 25 \implies x = \pm 5.$$ The $x$-intercepts (major vertices) are $(-5,0)$ and $(5,0)$.
  • Intercepts with the $y$-axis ($x=0$): $$0 + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1 \implies y^2 = 9 \implies y = \pm 3.$$ The $y$-intercepts (minor vertices) are $(0,-3)$ and $(0,3)$.

3. Direction of stretching and why it appears in the equation

The ellipse is more stretched along the $x$-axis (horizontally) because $a=5 > b=3$.

How is this visible in the equation? The denominator under $x^2$ is $25$, which is larger than the denominator $9$ under $y^2$. Since $\frac{x^2}{25}+\frac{y^2}{9}=1$, dividing $x^2$ by a larger number means $x$ must reach a larger magnitude ($|x|=5$) than $y$ ($|y|=3$) to make its fraction equal to $1$.

Plot the center $(0,0)$ and the four vertices $(\pm 5,0)$ and $(0,\pm 3)$, then connect them with a smooth symmetric oval:

Quick verification

Let us check a non-vertex point on the ellipse. If $x=4$, then

$$\frac{4^2}{25}+\frac{y^2}{9}=1 \implies \frac{y^2}{9}=1-\frac{16}{25}=\frac{9}{25} \implies y=\pm\frac{9}{5}=\pm 1.8.$$

Substituting $(4, 1.8)$ gives $\frac{16}{25}+\frac{3.24}{9}=0.64+0.36=1$, and indeed $|x|=4\le 5$ and $|y|=1.8\le 3$. ✓

A concrete example

Think of the ellipse as a rescaled unit circle $u^2+v^2=1$ where $u=\frac{x}{5}$ and $v=\frac{y}{3}$, so $x=5u$ and $y=3v$. A unit circle is stretched by a factor of $5$ horizontally and by a factor of $3$ vertically, which directly explains why the horizontal span $[-5,5]$ is wider than the vertical span $[-3,3]$.

How I would explain this in class

In the equation $\frac{x^2}{25}+\frac{y^2}{9}=1$, the denominators are $5^2$ and $3^2$, so the semiaxes are $a=5$ along the $x$-axis and $b=3$ along the $y$-axis. Setting $y=0$ gives $x$-intercepts $(\pm 5,0)$, and setting $x=0$ gives $y$-intercepts $(0,\pm 3)$. Because $x^2$ is divided by the larger denominator $25$, $x$ can grow further before the fraction hits $1$, so the ellipse is stretched horizontally along $Ox$.

Exercise 4. Parabola

The question

For

$$y^2=12x,$$

identify the vertex, axis of symmetry, and opening direction. Find points on the parabola for $x=3$ and use them to make a sketch.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A parabola in canonical form has one variable squared and the other variable linear:

$$y^2 = 2px \qquad\text{or}\qquad x^2 = 2py.$$

For $y^2 = 12x$:

  • Because $y^2\ge 0$ for every real $y$, the right-hand side must satisfy $12x\ge 0$, so $x\ge 0$.
  • Replacing $y$ with $-y$ leaves $y^2$ unchanged, which means the curve is symmetric across the line $y=0$ (the $x$-axis).

1. Vertex, axis of symmetry, and opening direction

From the equation $y^2=12x$:

  • Vertex: $(0,0)$, the extreme point where $x$ takes its minimum value $x=0$ (giving $y=0$).
  • Axis of symmetry: The $x$-axis ($y=0$), because $(-y)^2 = y^2 = 12x$ pairs every point $(x,y)$ with $(x,-y)$.
  • Opening direction: Opens to the right (along the positive $x$-axis), since real solutions exist only when $12x\ge 0 \iff x\ge 0$.
  • (Optional focal parameter): Comparing $y^2=12x$ with $y^2=2px$ (or $4fx$) gives $p=6$, so the focus is $(3,0)$ and the directrix is $x=-3$.

2. Points on the parabola for $x=3$

Substitute $x=3$ into $y^2=12x$:

$$y^2 = 12\cdot 3 = 36 \implies y = \pm\sqrt{36} = \pm 6.$$

This gives two symmetric points on the parabola:

$$A = (3,6)\qquad\text{and}\qquad B = (3,-6).$$

3. Additional reference points and coordinate table

To make an accurate sketch, we can also check $x=0$ and $x=\frac{4}{3}$ (or $x=1$):

Notice that at $x=3$, the segment connecting $(3,6)$ and $(3,-6)$ passes right through the focus $F=(3,0)$ and has total length $6-(-6)=12$, which matches the coefficient $2p=12$ in $y^2=12x$ (this segment is called the latus rectum of the parabola).

How I would explain this in class

In $y^2=12x$, only $y$ is squared, so the curve is a parabola with vertex at $(0,0)$. Since $y^2\ge 0$, we need $x\ge 0$, so the parabola opens to the right, and because $(\pm y)^2$ gives the same value, the $x$-axis ($y=0$) is the axis of symmetry. Plugging in $x=3$ gives $y^2=36$, so $y=\pm 6$; plotting $(0,0)$, $(3,6)$, and $(3,-6)$ immediately gives the shape of the parabola.

Exercise 5. Hyperbola and Asymptotes

The question

For

$$\frac{x^2}{16}-\frac{y^2}{9}=1,$$

find the intercepts with the $x$-axis, the asymptotes, and make a sketch. Explain why the asymptotes describe the behavior of the curve far from the center.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A hyperbola in canonical form

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$$

has a positive term in $x^2$ and a negative term in $y^2$. From $\frac{x^2}{a^2} = 1 + \frac{y^2}{b^2} \ge 1$, we see that $|x|\ge a$: the curve has two separate branches ($x\le -a$ and $x\ge a$) and never crosses the $y$-axis. Its global opening is guided by the rectangle of dimensions $2a\times 2b$ centered at the origin and its diagonal lines $y=\pm\frac{b}{a}x$, called the asymptotes.

1. Semiaxes and intercepts with the $x$-axis

From the denominators $16=4^2$ and $9=3^2$, we read:

  • Real semiaxis: $a = 4$ (along $Ox$).
  • Imaginary semiaxis: $b = 3$ (along $Oy$).

To find the intercepts with the $x$-axis, set $y=0$:

$$\frac{x^2}{16} - 0 = 1 \implies x^2 = 16 \implies x = \pm 4.$$

Thus the $x$-intercepts (the vertices of the hyperbola) are $(-4,0)$ and $(4,0)$. (Setting $x=0$ gives $-\frac{y^2}{9}=1$, which has no real solutions, so there are no $y$-intercepts.)

2. Finding the asymptotes

Replace the $1$ on the right-hand side with $0$ to find the asymptotic directions:

$$\frac{x^2}{16}-\frac{y^2}{9}=0 \iff \frac{y^2}{9}=\frac{x^2}{16} \iff y = \pm\frac{3}{4}x.$$

The two asymptotes are the lines $y = \frac{3}{4}x$ and $y = -\frac{3}{4}x$.

3. Why the asymptotes describe the curve far from the center

Solve the hyperbola equation for $y^2$ and factor out $x^2$ (for $|x|\ge 4$):

$$\frac{y^2}{9} = \frac{x^2}{16}-1 = \frac{x^2}{16}\left(1-\frac{16}{x^2}\right) \implies y = \pm\frac{3}{4}x\sqrt{1-\frac{16}{x^2}}.$$

When $|x|$ is very large (far from the center $(0,0)$), the fraction $\frac{16}{x^2}$ becomes tiny and approaches $0$. Consequently, the square root $\sqrt{1-\frac{16}{x^2}}$ approaches $1$, and the $y$-coordinate on the hyperbola approaches $\pm\frac{3}{4}x$. Equivalently, the vertical distance between the curve and the line $y=\frac{3}{4}x$ in the first quadrant is

$$\frac{3}{4}x - \frac{3}{4}\sqrt{x^2-16} = \frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{16}{x+\sqrt{x^2-16}} = \frac{12}{x+\sqrt{x^2-16}} \xrightarrow{x\to\infty} 0.$$

Quick verification

Let us test $x=5$:

  • On the hyperbola: $\frac{25}{16}-\frac{y^2}{9}=1 \implies \frac{y^2}{9}=\frac{9}{16} \implies y=\pm\frac{9}{4}=\pm 2.25$. ✓
  • On the asymptote: $y=\pm\frac{3}{4}(5)=\pm\frac{15}{4}=\pm 3.75$.

Now test $x=20$:

  • On the hyperbola: $y=\frac{3}{4}\sqrt{400-16}=\frac{3}{4}\sqrt{384}\approx 14.697$.
  • On the asymptote: $y=\frac{3}{4}(20)=15$.

The gap shrinks from $1.5$ at $x=5$ down to $0.303$ at $x=20$. ✓

A concrete example

At $x=4$, the hyperbola is at $(4,0)$ while the asymptote is at $(4,3)$ (gap $=3$). By $x=40$, the hyperbola is at $y=\frac{3}{4}\sqrt{1584}\approx 29.85$ while the asymptote is at $y=30$ (gap $\approx 0.15$). Far from the origin, the constant $1$ on the right-hand side of $\frac{x^2}{16}-\frac{y^2}{9}=1$ becomes negligible compared to the huge numbers $\frac{x^2}{16}$ and $\frac{y^2}{9}$.

How I would explain this in class

For $\frac{x^2}{16}-\frac{y^2}{9}=1$, we have $a=4$ and $b=3$. Setting $y=0$ gives the $x$-intercepts $(\pm 4,0)$, and replacing $1$ by $0$ gives the asymptotes $y=\pm\frac{3}{4}x$. Rewriting the equation as $y=\pm\frac{3}{4}x\sqrt{1-\frac{16}{x^2}}$ shows why the asymptotes control the shape far from the origin: as $|x|\to\infty$, the term $\frac{16}{x^2}$ vanishes, so the curve becomes indistinguishable from the lines $y=\pm\frac{3}{4}x$.

Exercise 6. Shifted Ellipse

The question

Reduce

$$4x^2+9y^2-16x+18y-11=0$$

to canonical form. Give the center and semiaxis lengths. Check that substituting the coordinates of the center into the left-hand side gives a value consistent with the form you obtained.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

When $x^2$ and $y^2$ have different positive coefficients ($4$ and $9$), the curve is an ellipse, and the linear terms $-16x$ and $+18y$ indicate that its center has been shifted away from $(0,0)$.

Before completing the square in each variable, we must factor out the leading coefficient ($4$ from the $x$-terms and $9$ from the $y$-terms) so that the expression inside each parenthesis starts with a plain $x^2$ or $y^2$.

1. Factoring and completing the square

Group the $x$-terms and $y$-terms, and move $-11$ to the right-hand side:

$$(4x^2-16x) + (9y^2+18y) = 11.$$

Factor $4$ from the $x$-group and $9$ from the $y$-group:

$$4(x^2-4x) + 9(y^2+2y) = 11.$$

Complete the square inside each parenthesis:

  • $x^2-4x = (x-2)^2 - 4$,
  • $y^2+2y = (y+1)^2 - 1$.

Substitute these back, remembering to multiply the subtracted constants by the outside factors $4$ and $9$:

$$4\big((x-2)^2 - 4\big) + 9\big((y+1)^2 - 1\big) = 11$$
$$4(x-2)^2 - 16 + 9(y+1)^2 - 9 = 11$$
$$4(x-2)^2 + 9(y+1)^2 = 11 + 16 + 9 = 36.$$

2. Dividing to obtain canonical form

Divide both sides of $4(x-2)^2 + 9(y+1)^2 = 36$ by $36$:

$$\frac{4(x-2)^2}{36} + \frac{9(y+1)^2}{36} = 1 \implies \frac{(x-2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y+1)^2}{4} = 1.$$

This is the canonical form of the shifted ellipse.

3. Center and semiaxis lengths

Comparing $\frac{(x-2)^2}{3^2} + \frac{(y-(-1))^2}{2^2} = 1$ with $\frac{(x-x_0)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-y_0)^2}{b^2} = 1$:

  • Center: $C = (2,-1)$.
  • Horizontal semiaxis (along $Ox$): $a = \sqrt{9} = 3$.
  • Vertical semiaxis (along $Oy$): $b = \sqrt{4} = 2$.

4. Checking the center in the left-hand side

Before dividing by $36$, the completed-square form of the left-hand side is

$$L(x,y) = 4x^2+9y^2-16x+18y-11 = 4(x-2)^2 + 9(y+1)^2 - 36.$$

If we substitute the center $(x,y)=(2,-1)$ into the completed-square form, both squared brackets vanish and we get

$$L(2,-1) = 4(0)^2 + 9(0)^2 - 36 = -36.$$

Now let us substitute $(2,-1)$ directly into the original left-hand side:

$$L(2,-1) = 4(2)^2 + 9(-1)^2 - 16(2) + 18(-1) - 11 = 16 + 9 - 32 - 18 - 11 = -36.$$

Both expressions evaluate to $-36$ (and in the normalized form $\frac{(x-2)^2}{9}+\frac{(y+1)^2}{4}-1$, substituting $(2,-1)$ gives $-1 = \frac{-36}{36}$).

Quick verification

Let us also check one vertex of the shifted ellipse, say $(2+3,-1) = (5,-1)$, in the original equation:

$$4(5)^2 + 9(-1)^2 - 16(5) + 18(-1) - 11 = 100 + 9 - 80 - 18 - 11 = 0.$$

It satisfies the original equation. ✓

A concrete example

By introducing shifted coordinates $X = x-2$ and $Y = y+1$, the origin $(X,Y)=(0,0)$ is placed at $(x,y)=(2,-1)$, and our equation becomes the standard centered ellipse

$$\frac{X^2}{9} + \frac{Y^2}{4} = 1.$$

Thus the four vertices in $(x,y)$-coordinates are $(2\pm 3, -1) = (-1,-1),\;(5,-1)$ and $(2, -1\pm 2) = (2,-3),\;(2,1)$.

How I would explain this in class

First, I group the $x$- and $y$-terms and factor out the leading coefficients: $4(x^2-4x)+9(y^2+2y)=11$. Completing the squares gives $4(x-2)^2-16+9(y+1)^2-9=11$, so $4(x-2)^2+9(y+1)^2=36$. Dividing by $36$ yields the canonical form $\frac{(x-2)^2}{9}+\frac{(y+1)^2}{4}=1$, which is an ellipse centered at $(2,-1)$ with semiaxes $a=3$ and $b=2$. Plugging $(2,-1)$ into the original left-hand side gives $16+9-32-18-11=-36$, matching $4(x-2)^2+9(y+1)^2-36$ at the center.

Exercise 7. Classifying a Second-Degree Equation

The question

Determine the type of object described by:

  1. $4x^2+9y^2=36$,
  2. $x^2-y^2=1$,
  3. $x^2-4y=0$,
  4. $x^2+y^2+1=0$.

In the last case, explain why there are no real points.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

To classify a second-degree equation in the plane:

  1. Check which variables are squared (both $x$ and $y$, or only one).
  2. If both are squared, check the signs of the squared terms and the sign of the constant term after bringing constants to the right-hand side.
  3. Always verify whether the equation admits real solutions—a sum of squares of real numbers can never be negative!

1. Equation 1: $4x^2+9y^2=36$

Divide both sides by $36$ to put the equation in canonical form:

$$\frac{4x^2}{36} + \frac{9y^2}{36} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1.$$
  • Both $x^2$ and $y^2$ have positive coefficients, the right-hand side is $+1>0$, and the denominators $9=3^2$ and $4=2^2$ are different.
  • Type of object: Ellipse centered at $(0,0)$ with semiaxes $a=3$ (horizontal) and $b=2$ (vertical).

2. Equation 2: $x^2-y^2=1$

This is already in the canonical form

$$\frac{x^2}{1^2} - \frac{y^2}{1^2} = 1.$$
  • Both $x$ and $y$ are squared, but their coefficients have opposite signs ($+1$ and $-1$).
  • Type of object: Hyperbola (specifically a rectangular or equilateral hyperbola, since $a=b=1$ and the asymptotes $y=\pm x$ are perpendicular), with vertices at $(\pm 1,0)$.

3. Equation 3: $x^2-4y=0$

Move $4y$ to the right-hand side:

$$x^2 = 4y.$$
  • Only $x$ is squared, while $y$ appears in the first power.
  • Type of object: Parabola with vertex at $(0,0)$, symmetric across the $y$-axis ($x=0$), opening upward ($y = \frac{1}{4}x^2 \ge 0$).

4. Equation 4: $x^2+y^2+1=0$ (and why there are no real points)

Move $1$ to the right-hand side:

$$x^2 + y^2 = -1.$$
  • Type of object: Empty set in the real plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ (often called an imaginary circle).
  • Why there are no real points: For any real numbers $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, their squares are non-negative: $$x^2 \ge 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad y^2 \ge 0.$$ Therefore, their sum satisfies $$x^2 + y^2 \ge 0,$$ so $x^2+y^2+1 \ge 1 > 0$. It can never equal $0$ for any pair of real coordinates $(x,y)$.

Quick verification

Let us summarize and verify with test points for the first three and a bound for the fourth:

A concrete example

Compare the family of equations $x^2+y^2 = c$ as the constant $c$ changes:

  • For $c = 1$: $x^2+y^2=1$ is the unit circle of radius $1$.
  • For $c = 0$: $x^2+y^2=0$ has only the single point $(0,0)$.
  • For $c = -1$: $x^2+y^2=-1$ (which is $x^2+y^2+1=0$) has no real points at all, because shrinking the radius past $0$ makes the circle disappear from the real plane.

How I would explain this in class

To classify these four equations, I put each one into standard form. First, $4x^2+9y^2=36$ divides by $36$ to give $\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=1$, an ellipse with semiaxes $3$ and $2$. Second, $x^2-y^2=1$ has opposite signs on the squared terms, so it is a hyperbola. Third, $x^2-4y=0$ or $x^2=4y$ has only $x$ squared and $y$ linear, so it is a parabola opening upward. Fourth, $x^2+y^2+1=0$ means $x^2+y^2=-1$; since squares of real numbers are never negative ($x^2+y^2\ge 0$), no real point $(x,y)$ can satisfy the equation, so it describes the empty set.

Exercise 8. Sphere

The question

Reduce

$$x^2+y^2+z^2-4x+2y-6z-2=0$$

to canonical form. Find the center and radius. Give two points on the sphere and check them in the equation.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

A sphere in three-dimensional space is the set of all points $(x,y,z)$ at a fixed distance $r$ from a center $C=(x_0,y_0,z_0)$. By the 3D distance formula, its canonical equation is

$$(x-x_0)^2 + (y-y_0)^2 + (z-z_0)^2 = r^2.$$

Just as for a circle in the plane, we reduce a general sphere equation to canonical form by completing the square independently in $x$, $y$, and $z$.

1. Grouping terms and completing the three squares

Group the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-terms, and move the constant $-2$ to the right-hand side:

$$(x^2-4x) + (y^2+2y) + (z^2-6z) = 2.$$

Complete each square using $u^2+bu = \left(u+\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$:

  • For $x$: $x^2-4x = (x-2)^2 - 4$,
  • For $y$: $y^2+2y = (y+1)^2 - 1$,
  • For $z$: $z^2-6z = (z-3)^2 - 9$.

Substitute these into the equation:

$$(x-2)^2 - 4 + (y+1)^2 - 1 + (z-3)^2 - 9 = 2.$$

Add $4+1+9=14$ to both sides to get the canonical form:

$$(x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 + (z-3)^2 = 16.$$

2. Center and radius

Comparing $(x-2)^2 + (y-(-1))^2 + (z-3)^2 = 4^2$ with $(x-x_0)^2 + (y-y_0)^2 + (z-z_0)^2 = r^2$:

  • Center: $C = (2,-1,3)$.
  • Radius: $r = \sqrt{16} = 4$.

3. Finding two points on the sphere

Starting from the center $C=(2,-1,3)$, we can step by the radius $r=4$ along any coordinate axis:

  • Step $+4$ along the $x$-axis: $P_1 = (2+4, -1, 3) = (6,-1,3)$.
  • Step $+4$ along the $y$-axis: $P_2 = (2, -1+4, 3) = (2,3,3)$.
  • (Other cardinal points include $(-2,-1,3)$, $(2,-5,3)$, $(2,-1,7)$, and $(2,-1,-1)$.)

4. Checking the two points in the equation

Let us check $P_1=(6,-1,3)$ and $P_2=(2,3,3)$ in both forms of the equation:

  • For $P_1=(6,-1,3)$:
  • Canonical: $(6-2)^2 + (-1+1)^2 + (3-3)^2 = 4^2 + 0 + 0 = 16$.
  • Original: $6^2 + (-1)^2 + 3^2 - 4(6) + 2(-1) - 6(3) - 2 = 36 + 1 + 9 - 24 - 2 - 18 - 2 = 0$.
  • For $P_2=(2,3,3)$:
  • Canonical: $(2-2)^2 + (3+1)^2 + (3-3)^2 = 0 + 4^2 + 0 = 16$.
  • Original: $2^2 + 3^2 + 3^2 - 4(2) + 2(3) - 6(3) - 2 = 4 + 9 + 9 - 8 + 6 - 18 - 2 = 0$.

Quick verification

Let us also check the antipodal point along the $z$-axis, $P_3 = (2,-1,3-4) = (2,-1,-1)$, in the original equation:

$$2^2 + (-1)^2 + (-1)^2 - 4(2) + 2(-1) - 6(-1) - 2 = 4 + 1 + 1 - 8 - 2 + 6 - 2 = 0.$$

And expanding $(x-2)^2+(y+1)^2+(z-3)^2-16 = (x^2-4x+4)+(y^2+2y+1)+(z^2-6z+9)-16 = x^2+y^2+z^2-4x+2y-6z-2=0$. ✓

A concrete example

Does this sphere intersect the $xy$-plane ($z=0$)? Setting $z=0$ in the canonical equation gives

$$(x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 + (0-3)^2 = 16 \implies (x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 = 16 - 9 = 7.$$

So the sphere slices the $xy$-plane in a circle centered at $(2,-1,0)$ with radius $\sqrt{7}$.

How I would explain this in class

Completing the square for a sphere in 3D works just like for a circle in 2D, with one extra variable $z$. Grouping terms gives $(x^2-4x)+(y^2+2y)+(z^2-6z)=2$, which becomes $(x-2)^2-4+(y+1)^2-1+(z-3)^2-9=2$, or $(x-2)^2+(y+1)^2+(z-3)^2=16$. That tells us the center is $(2,-1,3)$ and the radius is $r=4$. Moving $4$ units along the $x$- or $y$-axis from the center gives $(6,-1,3)$ and $(2,3,3)$, which both make the original equation equal to $0$.

Exercise 9. Ellipsoid and Its Cross-Sections

The question

Consider

$$\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}+\frac{z^2}{16}=1.$$

Give the semiaxis lengths, describe the cross-section $z=0$, and explain how the cross-sections $z=c$ change for $|c|<4$.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

An ellipsoid centered at the origin has the canonical equation

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}+\frac{z^2}{c_0^2}=1.$$

To understand a 3D surface, we slice it with horizontal planes $z=c$ (like a CT scan). Substituting $z=c$ freezes one variable and leaves a 2D equation in $x$ and $y$, whose size depends on $c$.

1. Semiaxis lengths

Write the denominators as squares:

$$9 = 3^2,\qquad 4 = 2^2,\qquad 16 = 4^2.$$

Thus the three semiaxis lengths are:

  • Along the $x$-axis ($Ox$): $a = \sqrt{9} = 3$ (vertices at $(\pm 3,0,0)$).
  • Along the $y$-axis ($Oy$): $b = \sqrt{4} = 2$ (vertices at $(0,\pm 2,0)$).
  • Along the $z$-axis ($Oz$): $c_0 = \sqrt{16} = 4$ (vertices at $(0,0,\pm 4)$).

The ellipsoid is longest vertically along $Oz$ ($c_0=4$) and narrowest along $Oy$ ($b=2$).

2. The cross-section $z=0$

Substitute $z=0$ into the equation of the ellipsoid:

$$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} + \frac{0^2}{16} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1\quad\text{in the plane }z=0.$$

This cross-section (the "equator" of the ellipsoid in the $xy$-plane) is an ellipse centered at $(0,0,0)$ with semiaxes $a=3$ along $Ox$ and $b=2$ along $Oy$.

3. How the cross-sections $z=c$ change for $|c|<4$

Fix a constant height $z=c$ with $|c|<4$ (so $c^2<16$ and $1-\frac{c^2}{16}>0$). Substituting $z=c$ gives:

$$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} + \frac{c^2}{16} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1 - \frac{c^2}{16}.$$

To put this 2D curve into canonical ellipse form, divide both sides by $1-\frac{c^2}{16} > 0$:

$$\frac{x^2}{9\left(1-\frac{c^2}{16}\right)} + \frac{y^2}{4\left(1-\frac{c^2}{16}\right)} = 1.$$

Let $k(c) = \sqrt{1-\frac{c^2}{16}} \in (0,1]$. Then the slice at height $z=c$ is an ellipse with semiaxes

$$a(c) = 3\sqrt{1-\frac{c^2}{16}},\qquad b(c) = 2\sqrt{1-\frac{c^2}{16}}.$$

How the slices behave as $c$ varies:

  1. At $c=0$: $k(0)=1$, so the ellipse has its maximum size ($a(0)=3$, $b(0)=2$).
  2. As $|c|$ increases from $0$ toward $4$: The factor $\sqrt{1-\frac{c^2}{16}}$ decreases steadily toward $0$. The cross-sections remain similar ellipses (their aspect ratio $\frac{a(c)}{b(c)} = \frac{3}{2}$ never changes!), but they shrink symmetrically as we move either up ($c>0$) or down ($c<0$).
  3. At the poles $c=\pm 4$: $1-\frac{c^2}{16}=0$, so the ellipse shrinks to a single point $(0,0,\pm 4)$. (For $|c|>4$, the right-hand side is negative and the plane misses the ellipsoid completely.)

Let us check a point on the slice $z = 2\sqrt{3}$ (where the scale factor is $\frac{1}{2}$): its $x$-vertex in that plane is $(x,y,z) = \left(\frac{3}{2}, 0, 2\sqrt{3}\right)$. Substitute into the ellipsoid equation:

$$\frac{(3/2)^2}{9} + \frac{0^2}{4} + \frac{(2\sqrt{3})^2}{16} = \frac{9/4}{9} + 0 + \frac{12}{16} = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = 1.$$

A concrete example

At height $z = \frac{12}{5} = 2.4$, the scale factor is

$$\sqrt{1 - \frac{(12/5)^2}{16}} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{144}{400}} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{9}{25}} = \sqrt{\frac{16}{25}} = \frac{4}{5}.$$

So the horizontal cross-section at $z=2.4$ is an ellipse with exact rational semiaxes $a = 3\cdot\frac{4}{5} = 2.4$ and $b = 2\cdot\frac{4}{5} = 1.6$.

How I would explain this in class

From $\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}+\frac{z^2}{16}=1$, the denominators are $3^2, 2^2, 4^2$, so the semiaxes are $3$ along $Ox$, $2$ along $Oy$, and $4$ along $Oz$. Setting $z=0$ gives the biggest horizontal cross-section, the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=1$ with semiaxes $3$ and $2$. For any height $z=c$ with $|c|<4$, we get $\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=1-\frac{c^2}{16}$, which is a family of ellipses with the same $3:2$ proportion whose semiaxes $3\sqrt{1-c^2/16}$ and $2\sqrt{1-c^2/16}$ shrink as $|c|$ grows, until they collapse to a single point at the top and bottom poles $z=\pm 4$.

Exercise 10. Paraboloids

The question

Compare

$$z=x^2+y^2$$

and

$$z=x^2-y^2.$$

For each surface, describe the cross-sections $x=0$, $y=0$, and $z=c$, then make a qualitative sketch.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

In both equations, $z$ appears linearly while $x$ and $y$ are squared—this is the signature of a paraboloid. The entire geometric difference between the two surfaces comes from the relative sign of $x^2$ and $y^2$:

  • Same sign ($z=x^2+y^2$): both vertical coordinate slices curve in the same direction (upward), forming a bowl (elliptic/circular paraboloid).
  • Opposite signs ($z=x^2-y^2$): the two vertical coordinate slices curve in opposite directions (one upward, one downward), forming a saddle (hyperbolic paraboloid).

  • Horizontal cross-sections $z=c$: $x^2+y^2 = c$.
  • For $c < 0$: empty set (no real points, since $x^2+y^2\ge 0$).
  • For $c = 0$: the single point $(0,0,0)$ (the vertex/bottom of the bowl).
  • For $c > 0$: a circle of radius $r = \sqrt{c}$, which expands as we move higher.

Exercise 11. Two Similar Surfaces

The question

Compare

$$x^2+y^2-z^2=1$$

and

$$x^2+y^2-z^2=-1.$$

For several values $z=c$, check whether the cross-section exists and what its radius is. Based on this, describe qualitatively the difference between the surfaces and make sketches.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Both surfaces have the exact same left-hand side $x^2+y^2-z^2$ and differ only in the sign of the right-hand side ($+1$ vs. $-1$). To see what each surface looks like without memorizing names, we move $-z^2$ to the right-hand side and slice with horizontal planes $z=c$:

  • First surface: $x^2+y^2 = 1 + c^2$.
  • Second surface: $x^2+y^2 = c^2 - 1$.

In each plane $z=c$, the equation $x^2+y^2 = R^2$ describes a circle of radius $R$ whenever the right-hand side is positive, a single point when it is $0$, and the empty set when it is negative.

1. First surface: $x^2+y^2-z^2 = 1$

For $z=c$, the horizontal slice is

$$x^2+y^2 = 1 + c^2.$$

Since $1+c^2 \ge 1 > 0$ for every real number $c\in\mathbb{R}$, the cross-section always exists and is a circle of radius

$$r_1(c) = \sqrt{1+c^2}.$$

Let us check several values of $z=c$:

  • $c = 0$: circle $x^2+y^2=1$, radius $r_1(0) = 1$ (the narrowest "waist" or "neck" of the surface).
  • $c = \pm 1$: circle $x^2+y^2=2$, radius $r_1(\pm 1) = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$.
  • $c = \pm 2$: circle $x^2+y^2=5$, radius $r_1(\pm 2) = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$.

Because a circle exists at every height $z=c$, all these circles stack continuously into one connected piece shaped like a cooling tower (hyperboloid of one sheet).

2. Second surface: $x^2+y^2-z^2 = -1$

For $z=c$, the horizontal slice is

$$x^2+y^2 = c^2 - 1.$$

Here a real cross-section exists only when $c^2 - 1 \ge 0 \iff |c| \ge 1$:

  • $c = 0$: $x^2+y^2 = -1$, does not exist (empty set). In fact, for any $|c|<1$, there are no real points!
  • $c = \pm 1$: $x^2+y^2 = 0$, a single point $(0,0,1)$ and $(0,0,-1)$ (radius $r_2(\pm 1) = 0$).
  • $c = \pm\sqrt{2}$: circle $x^2+y^2 = 1$, radius $r_2(\pm\sqrt{2}) = 1$.
  • $c = \pm 2$: circle $x^2+y^2 = 3$, radius $r_2(\pm 2) = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73$.

In general, for $|c|\ge 1$, the slice is a circle of radius $r_2(c) = \sqrt{c^2-1}$. Because the slab $-1 < z < 1$ is completely empty, the surface is split into two separate bowls—one opening upward for $z\ge 1$ and one opening downward for $z\le -1$ (hyperboloid of two sheets).

Exercise 12. Recognizing a Surface from Cross-Sections

The question

A certain surface has the following properties:

  • the cross-section $z=0$ is a point,
  • for $z>0$, the cross-sections $z=\text{const}$ are circles with increasing radius,
  • vertical cross-sections through the $Oz$ axis are parabolas.

Propose a simple equation for such a surface and justify your choice.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

Here we reverse the usual workflow: instead of starting from an equation and finding its slices, we translate each geometric slice condition into an algebraic requirement on $x,y,z$:

  1. Horizontal cross-sections $z=\text{const}$ are circles centered on the $z$-axis $\implies x$ and $y$ must appear together in the rotational combination $x^2+y^2$.
  2. Vertical cross-sections through the $Oz$ axis are parabolas $\implies$ the height $z$ must be a first-degree (linear) term while the horizontal distance is squared.

1. Translating the circular cross-sections into algebra

Since every horizontal cross-section $z=c$ ($c>0$) is a circle centered on the $Oz$ axis, the equation in the plane $z=c$ must have the form

$$x^2+y^2 = r(c)^2,$$

where $r(c)>0$ is the radius of the circle at height $z=c$, and $r(c)$ increases with $c$.

2. Using the vertical cross-sections to determine $r(c)^2$

The third property states that vertical planes through the $Oz$ axis—for example, the plane $y=0$ (the $xz$-plane) or $x=0$ (the $yz$-plane)—cut the surface in parabolas.

  • In the plane $y=0$, our equation $x^2+y^2 = f(z)$ becomes $x^2 = f(z)$.
  • For $x^2 = f(z)$ to be a parabola opening upward from $z=0$, $f(z)$ must be a positive constant multiple of $z$ (the simplest choice is $f(z)=z$).

(Note: If we chose $f(z)=z^2$, then $x^2=z^2 \iff z=\pm x$ would give straight lines—a cone—rather than parabolas!)

3. Proposed equation and full justification

The simplest equation satisfying all three conditions is the circular paraboloid:

$$z = x^2+y^2.$$

Let us verify each of the three given properties directly from $z = x^2+y^2$:

  1. Cross-section $z=0$ is a point: Setting $z=0$ gives $x^2+y^2=0$. Since $x^2\ge 0$ and $y^2\ge 0$ for real $x,y$, the only real solution is the single point $(0,0,0)$.
  2. For $z>0$, cross-sections $z=c$ are circles with increasing radius: Setting $z=c>0$ gives $x^2+y^2=c$, which is a circle of radius $r(c)=\sqrt{c}$. Because $\sqrt{c}$ is strictly increasing for $c>0$, the radius grows as $c$ increases.
  3. Vertical cross-sections through $Oz$ are parabolas: Any vertical plane through the $Oz$ axis is parameterized by setting $x = t\cos\theta$, $y = t\sin\theta$ where $t\in\mathbb{R}$ is the signed horizontal coordinate in that plane. Substituting into $z=x^2+y^2$ gives $$z = t^2(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta) = t^2,$$ which is a standard parabola in every vertical plane through $Oz$ (in particular, $z=x^2$ when $y=0$ and $z=y^2$ when $x=0$).

Quick verification

Let us check a few concrete slices of $z=x^2+y^2$:

  • At $z=0$: $x^2+y^2=0 \implies (0,0,0)$ (1 point). ✓
  • At $z=1$: $x^2+y^2=1$ (circle of radius $1$). ✓
  • At $z=4$: $x^2+y^2=4$ (circle of radius $2 > 1$). ✓
  • At $y=0$: $z=x^2$ (parabola). ✓

(Any positive scaling $z = a(x^2+y^2)$ with $a>0$ is also a valid answer.)

A concrete example

Why couldn't the surface be the upper half-cone $z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ (i.e., $x^2+y^2=z^2$ for $z\ge 0$)?

  • For the cone, $z=0$ is also a point $(0,0,0)$, and $z=c>0$ is also a circle of increasing radius $r=c$.
  • However, setting $y=0$ on the cone gives $z=|x|$, which is a V-shaped pair of rays, not a smooth parabola. The third condition ("vertical cross-sections through $Oz$ are parabolas") uniquely picks out the quadratic relation $z = x^2+y^2$ instead of $z^2 = x^2+y^2$.

How I would explain this in class

Because the horizontal slices $z=c>0$ are circles centered on the $z$-axis, $x$ and $y$ must appear as $x^2+y^2$. Because the vertical slices through the $z$-axis (like $x=0$ or $y=0$) are parabolas, $z$ must be in the first power while $x$ and $y$ are squared. Putting those together with the vertex at $z=0$ gives $z=x^2+y^2$: at $z=0$ we get the single point $(0,0,0)$, for $z=c>0$ we get circles $x^2+y^2=c$ of increasing radius $\sqrt{c}$, and slicing with $x=0$ or $y=0$ gives the parabolas $z=y^2$ and $z=x^2$.

Exercise 13. Intersection of a Sphere and a Plane

The question

Given the sphere

$$x^2+y^2+z^2=25$$

and the plane $z=3$, find the equation of the intersection curve and its radius. Then describe what happens for $z=c$ depending on $c$.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

The equation $x^2+y^2+z^2=25$ describes a sphere centered at the origin $(0,0,0)$ with radius $R = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

A point lies on the intersection of the sphere and the horizontal plane $z=c$ if and only if its coordinates satisfy both equations simultaneously. Algebraically, we substitute $z=c$ into the sphere's equation; geometrically, $c^2 + r^2 = R^2$ forms a right triangle with hypotenuse $R=5$ (sphere radius), vertical leg $|c|$ (plane height), and horizontal leg $r = \sqrt{25-c^2}$ (cross-section radius).

1. Intersection curve and radius for $z=3$

Substitute $z=3$ into $x^2+y^2+z^2=25$:

$$x^2 + y^2 + 3^2 = 25 \implies x^2 + y^2 + 9 = 25 \implies x^2 + y^2 = 16.$$

Thus, the intersection curve is given by the system

$$\begin{cases} x^2+y^2 = 16, \\ z = 3. \end{cases}$$

Geometrically, this is a circle lying in the horizontal plane $z=3$, centered at $(0,0,3)$ on the $z$-axis, with radius

$$r = \sqrt{16} = 4.$$

2. What happens for $z=c$ depending on $c$

Substitute a general height $z=c$ into $x^2+y^2+z^2=25$:

$$x^2 + y^2 = 25 - c^2.$$

Depending on the sign of $25-c^2$, there are three distinct geometric regimes:

  1. Case 1: $|c| < 5$ (i.e., $-5 < c < 5$): Here $25-c^2 > 0$. The plane cuts through the interior of the sphere, and the intersection is a circle centered at $(0,0,c)$ of radius $$r(c) = \sqrt{25-c^2}.$$
  • At $c=0$ (the equator), the radius is maximal: $r(0) = 5$.
  • As $|c|$ increases from $0$ toward $5$, the circle shrinks.
  1. Case 2: $|c| = 5$ (i.e., $c = 5$ or $c = -5$): Here $25-c^2 = 0$, so $x^2+y^2 = 0 \implies x=0,\;y=0$. The plane is tangent to the sphere at the north pole $(0,0,5)$ or south pole $(0,0,-5)$, so the intersection is a single point (a degenerate circle of radius $r=0$).
  1. Case 3: $|c| > 5$ (i.e., $c > 5$ or $c < -5$): Here $25-c^2 < 0$. Since $x^2+y^2\ge 0$ for all real $x,y$, there are no real solutions. The plane passes completely above or below the sphere, so the intersection is the empty set.

Exercise 14. Cone and Cylinder

The question

Compare

$$x^2+y^2=z^2$$

and

$$x^2+y^2=4.$$

For each surface, describe the cross-sections $z=c$, $x=0$, and $y=0$, make a sketch, and explain why the radius of the cross-section depends on $z$ in the first case but is constant in the second.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

In three-dimensional space $(x,y,z)$, the quantity $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ is the horizontal distance of a point $(x,y,z)$ from the vertical $Oz$ axis:

  • In $x^2+y^2 = z^2$, the squared distance from the $z$-axis is set equal to $z^2$, so the distance grows proportionally with $|z|$ (circular cone).
  • In $x^2+y^2 = 4$, the variable $z$ does not appear in the equation at all! That means $z$ is completely free while the distance from the $z$-axis is locked at $2$ (circular cylinder).

1. First surface: $x^2+y^2 = z^2$ (Circular Cone)

Let us find its cross-sections:

  • Horizontal cross-sections $z=c$: $x^2+y^2 = c^2$.
  • For $c = 0$: $x^2+y^2 = 0$, a single point $(0,0,0)$ (the apex/vertex of the cone).
  • For $c \neq 0$: a circle of radius $r(c) = \sqrt{c^2} = |c|$, growing linearly with $|c|$.
  • Vertical cross-section $x=0$ (in the $yz$-plane): $y^2 = z^2 \iff z = \pm y$, a pair of intersecting straight lines passing through the origin at $45^\circ$.
  • Vertical cross-section $y=0$ (in the $xz$-plane): $x^2 = z^2 \iff z = \pm x$, a pair of intersecting straight lines passing through the origin at $45^\circ$.

3. Why the radius depends on $z$ in the first case but is constant in the second

In any horizontal plane $z=c$, the left-hand side $x^2+y^2$ represents $r^2$, the square of the horizontal radius:

  • In $x^2+y^2=z^2$: The right-hand side is $z^2$, which explicitly contains $z$. When we slice at height $z=c$, the equation sets $r^2 = c^2$, forcing the radius $r = |c|$ to scale directly with the height $|z|$.
  • In $x^2+y^2=4$: The right-hand side is the constant $4=2^2$, and the variable $z$ is absent from the equation (its coefficient is $0\cdot z^2 + 0\cdot z$). Because the equation places no restriction on $z$ and does not link $x,y$ to $z$, shifting the slice to any height $z=c$ leaves the 2D equation $x^2+y^2=4$ unchanged, so the radius stays constant at $r=2$.

  • On the cone $x^2+y^2=z^2$: $2^2+0^2=z^2 \implies z=\pm 2$, so a radius of $2$ occurs only at the two heights $z=2$ and $z=-2$. ✓

Exercise 15. Recognition and Sketch Without a Given Name

The question

Consider the two equations:

$$4x^2+9y^2-8x+36y+4=0,$$
$$4x^2+y^2=z^2.$$

For the first equation, reduce it to canonical form, recognize the curve, give its most important features, and make a sketch.

For the second equation, do not begin by using a ready-made name for the surface. First describe the cross-sections $z=c$ and the cross-sections by the planes $x=0$ and $y=0$, paying attention to the different scales along the axes. Only then identify the surface and make a sketch.

Before calculating: what idea do we need?

This exercise brings together the two central tools of the entire set:

  1. Completing the square (for shifted equations): Factor out the leading coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ to find the center and semiaxes of a shifted 2D curve.
  2. Slicing by coordinate planes (for 3D surfaces): Freeze one variable at a time ($z=c$, $x=0$, $y=0$) to read the 2D cross-sections and their axis scales before naming the 3D surface.

1. First equation: reducing $4x^2+9y^2-8x+36y+4=0$ to canonical form

Group the $x$-terms and $y$-terms, and move the constant $+4$ to the right-hand side:

$$(4x^2-8x) + (9y^2+36y) = -4.$$

Factor out $4$ from the $x$-group and $9$ from the $y$-group:

$$4(x^2-2x) + 9(y^2+4y) = -4.$$

Complete the square inside each bracket:

  • $x^2-2x = (x-1)^2 - 1$,
  • $y^2+4y = (y+2)^2 - 4$.

Substitute back:

$$4\big((x-1)^2 - 1\big) + 9\big((y+2)^2 - 4\big) = -4$$
$$4(x-1)^2 - 4 + 9(y+2)^2 - 36 = -4$$
$$4(x-1)^2 + 9(y+2)^2 = 36.$$

Divide both sides by $36$ to get the canonical form:

$$\frac{(x-1)^2}{9} + \frac{(y+2)^2}{4} = 1.$$

  • First equation: Substitute the top vertex $(1,0)$ and right vertex $(4,-2)$ into $4x^2+9y^2-8x+36y+4$:
  • At $(1,0)$: $4(1) + 0 - 8(1) + 0 + 4 = 0$. ✓
  • At $(4,-2)$: $4(16) + 9(4) - 8(4) + 36(-2) + 4 = 64 + 36 - 32 - 72 + 4 = 0$. ✓
  • Second equation: At height $z=2$, the ellipse vertices are $(1,0,2)$ along $Ox$ and $(0,2,2)$ along $Oy$:
  • At $(1,0,2)$: $4(1)^2 + 0^2 = 4 = 2^2$. ✓
  • At $(0,2,2)$: $4(0)^2 + 2^2 = 4 = 2^2$. ✓

A concrete example

At height $z=4$ on the surface $4x^2+y^2=z^2$, the horizontal cross-section is $4x^2+y^2=16$, or

$$\frac{x^2}{2^2} + \frac{y^2}{4^2} = 1.$$

Thus, at $z=4$, the surface extends from $x=-2$ to $x=2$ along the $x$-axis (total width $4$), and from $y=-4$ to $y=4$ along the $y$-axis (total width $8$).

How I would explain this in class

For the first equation, I factor out $4$ and $9$ and complete the squares: $4(x-1)^2-4+9(y+2)^2-36+4=0$, which simplifies to $\frac{(x-1)^2}{9}+\frac{(y+2)^2}{4}=1$. That is a shifted ellipse centered at $(1,-2)$ with horizontal semiaxis $3$ and vertical semiaxis $2$. For the second equation $4x^2+y^2=z^2$, slicing with $z=c\neq 0$ gives $\frac{x^2}{(c/2)^2}+\frac{y^2}{c^2}=1$, which is a family of ellipses twice as wide along $Oy$ ($b=|c|$) as along $Ox$ ($a=|c|/2$), shrinking to a single point at $z=0$. Slicing with $x=0$ gives the lines $z=\pm y$ and slicing with $y=0$ gives the steeper lines $z=\pm 2x$. Together, these slices show that the surface is an elliptic cone with vertex at the origin.
